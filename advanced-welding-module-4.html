1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Stick welding, also called SMAW (Shielded Metal Arc Welding), is the most common welding process in African workshops because it is cheap, portable, and works outdoors. With a simple electrode holder, ground clamp, and a welding machine that runs on 220V single-phase power, you can weld gates, burglar bars, trailers, and water tanks. Advanced stick welding means you can weld out of position (vertical, overhead), on thick metal, and with different electrode types. This skill gets you hired on construction sites, in fabrication shops, and for repair work. In many African countries, a certified stick welder earns 2-3 times more than a general laborer. You will learn to run clean beads, avoid defects, and work safely. The demand is high: from fixing broken ploughs in rural areas to building steel roofs in cities. This module builds on basic stick welding. You will learn to control heat, travel speed, and electrode angle for strong welds. We focus on practical techniques you can practice with a 3.2mm or 4.0mm electrode, a chipping hammer, and a wire brush. By the end, you will be able to weld a T-joint in the flat, horizontal, and vertical positions. That is the standard for most jobs. Remember: stick welding is forgiving but requires steady hands and good preparation. Clean your metal, set your amperage right, and practice daily. In Africa, where resources are limited, stick welding is the go-to process because it does not need gas cylinders or fancy equipment. You can run it off a generator. That makes it perfect for remote sites. So let's get started. Your ability to stick weld well will open doors to steady income and respect in the trade.

2. Tools and Materials

To do advanced stick welding, you need the right tools. First, a welding machine. In Africa, common brands are ESAB, Lincoln, and local Chinese units like TELWIN. For 3.2mm and 4.0mm electrodes, you need a machine that delivers 80-200 amps. A 200-amp inverter welder works on 220V and is portable. Second, electrodes. The most common are E6013 (for general purpose, easy to strike) and E7018 (for high strength, low hydrogen). Buy from a reputable supplier because fake electrodes cause porosity. Store them in a dry place; if they get damp, bake them at 100Â°C for an hour. Third, safety gear: a welding helmet with a #10 or #11 shade lens, leather gloves, a leather apron, and safety boots. Fourth, tools: a chipping hammer, a wire brush, a 4-inch angle grinder with grinding and cutting discs, a tape measure, a square, and clamps. For practice, get mild steel plates 6mm to 10mm thick, cut into 100mm x 150mm pieces. You also need a metal table or a firebrick surface to weld on. A bucket of water and a fire extinguisher are mandatory. In many African workshops, you can find all these at a local hardware store or market. For a starter kit, budget around $200-$300 for a decent inverter welder and accessories. If you cannot afford a machine, look for a nearby vocational training center that rents time. Always check your cables for cracks; replace them if damaged. Keep your electrode holder clean and tight. A loose connection causes overheating and poor arc. Finally, a good ground clamp is essential. Clamp it directly to the workpiece, not to a painted surface. With these tools, you are ready to practice advanced techniques.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for a strong stick weld on a T-joint (vertical plate on horizontal plate). Step 1: Prepare the metal. Grind the edges to bare shiny steel. Remove rust, paint, and oil. Use a wire brush. Step 2: Set your machine. For 3.2mm E6013 electrode, set amperage at 90-120 amps. For 4.0mm, set 120-160 amps. If the arc sounds like bacon frying, it's right. If it pops, increase amps. If it burns through, decrease. Step 3: Position yourself. For flat welding, sit or stand comfortably. For vertical, stand to the side. Step 4: Strike the arc. Scratch the electrode like a match or tap it down. Once lit, hold a short arc (arc length about equal to electrode diameter). Step 5: Maintain a 10-15 degree push angle (electrode tilted in the direction of travel). For vertical up, use a slight drag angle (tilt down). Step 6: Use the correct motion. For a straight bead, move steadily. For a weave, use a slight side-to-side motion, pausing at the edges. Step 7: Watch the puddle. The molten pool should be oval and bright. If it gets too large, speed up or lower amps. Step 8: Finish the weld. At the end, pause and backstep slightly to fill the crater, then lift the electrode. Step 9: Clean the slag. Wait for the weld to cool until it turns from red to grey. Then chip with a chipping hammer and brush. Step 10: Inspect. Look for undercut, porosity, and cracks. If you see defects, grind and re-weld. Practice this on scrap plates daily. In Africa, many welders learn by doing. Start with flat position, then horizontal, then vertical. Each position requires a different angle and travel speed. For vertical up, use lower amps (about 10% less) and a faster travel speed to prevent the puddle from dripping. For overhead, use 3.2mm electrode and higher amps to push the metal in. Keep your arc tight. With repetition, you will develop muscle memory. Remember: a good weld looks like a stack of dimes. That comes from consistent travel speed and puddle control.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Arc blow. This happens when the arc wanders due to magnetic forces, common when welding near the ground clamp or on thick steel. Fix: Move the ground clamp to the other end of the workpiece. Wrap the electrode cable around the work a few times to create a magnetic field. Use AC instead of DC if possible. Mistake 2: Porosity (holes in the weld). Causes: damp electrodes, dirty metal, or too long an arc. Fix: Bake electrodes at 100Â°C for 1 hour, clean metal to bare shine, and keep a short arc. Mistake 3: Slag inclusion. This is when slag gets trapped in the weld. Causes: not cleaning between passes, wrong electrode angle. Fix: Chip and brush thoroughly after each pass. Use a 10-15 degree push angle. Mistake 4: Undercut (groove melted into base metal along the weld edge). Causes: too high amps, too fast travel, or wrong angle. Fix: Lower amps by 10-15, slow down, and pause at the edges. Mistake 5: Spatter. Causes: too high amps, long arc, or damp electrodes. Fix: Lower amps, shorten arc, and use anti-spatter spray. Mistake 6: Cracking. Causes: rapid cooling, high sulfur in steel, or wrong electrode. Fix: Pre-heat thick steel to 100-150Â°C, use E7018 low hydrogen electrodes, and cool slowly by covering with sand or a blanket. Mistake 7: Incomplete fusion (weld does not stick to base metal). Causes: too low amps, too fast travel, or wrong angle. Fix: Increase amps, slow down, and aim the arc at the joint root. Mistake 8: Burn-through on thin metal. Causes: too high amps, slow travel. Fix: Use 2.5mm electrode, lower amps to 60-80, and move faster. In Africa, many welders use E6013 because it's cheap and easy. But for critical jobs like trailer hitches, use E7018. Always practice on scrap before welding a real part. If you see a mistake, grind it out and re-weld. Never try to cover a bad weld with more metal. That creates a weak joint. Keep a notebook of your settings and results. That is how you improve.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. Stick welding produces intense UV light, sparks, fumes, and electric shock hazards. Follow these rules. 1. Wear a welding helmet with a proper shade lens. For stick welding up to 200 amps, use shade #10 to #13. Never use a torch filter. 2. Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and safety boots. No open shoes. 3. Protect your ears and hair. Wear a cap and ear plugs if welding overhead. 4. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. In Africa, many workshops are wooden, so fire risk is high. Also have a bucket of sand or water. 5. Ensure good ventilation. Welding fumes contain manganese and other metals. Weld outdoors or use a fan to blow fumes away. Do not weld in a closed room. 6. Check electrical cables for damage. Do not weld in wet conditions. Wear rubber-soled boots and stand on a dry rubber mat. 7. Turn off the machine before changing electrodes or adjusting the holder. 8. Do not weld on containers that held fuel or chemicals. They can explode. 9. Use a screen to protect others from arc flash. Arc flash can cause painful eye burns. 10. Keep your work area clean. Remove flammable materials like paper, cloth, and petrol. Best practices: Always clamp your ground directly to the workpiece. Use the correct amperage for the electrode size. Do not exceed the duty cycle of your machine. For example, a 200-amp inverter welder at 60% duty cycle can run 6 minutes out of 10 at 200 amps. Let it cool. Keep your electrodes dry. Store them in a sealed container with silica gel. Practice good posture to avoid back strain. In Africa, many welders work long hours. Take breaks. Drink water. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Finally, get trained in first aid. Know how to treat burns and eye injuries. A safe welder is a productive welder.

6. Practical Exercise

Now put your skills to the test. This exercise will help you master advanced stick welding. You will weld a T-joint in three positions: flat, horizontal, and vertical. Materials: Two mild steel plates, each 100mm x 150mm x 8mm thick. One will be the base, the other the vertical web. You also need a 3.2mm E6013 electrode, a 4.0mm E7018 electrode, a chipping hammer, wire brush, grinder, and your welding machine. Step 1: Clean both plates with a grinder and wire brush. Step 2: Tack weld the vertical plate to the center of the base plate at 90 degrees. Use two tacks, one at each end. Step 3: Set your machine to 110 amps for the 3.2mm E6013. Step 4: Flat position: Weld the joint on the top side. Use a 10-15 degree push angle and a slight weave. Run a bead from left to right. Clean slag, then run a second pass if needed. Step 5: Horizontal position: Rotate the T-joint so the weld is on the vertical side. Weld from left to right. Use a 5-10 degree drag angle. Keep a short arc. Step 6: Vertical position: Stand the T-joint up. Weld from bottom to top. Use 100 amps and a slight upward push angle. Use a triangle motion, pausing at the edges. Step 7: After each weld, chip and brush. Inspect for defects. Step 8: For the 4.0mm E7018, repeat the vertical weld but set amps to 140. Use a short arc and a slight drag. Step 9: Cut the welded T-joint across the weld with a hacksaw or angle grinder. Examine the cross-section. Look for fusion, porosity, and slag inclusion. A good weld will show complete fusion and no holes. Step 10: Record your settings and observations. Repeat until you can produce a clean weld in all positions. In Africa, this exercise is often used in welding tests for jobs. Practice on scrap until you are confident. Then try on a real project like a gate or a trailer. Remember: consistency is key. Weld every day for at least an hour. Your hands will learn.

Common Mistakes and Business Impact

In agribusiness fabrication, a failed weld is not just a technical problem â it is a business problem. A cracked trailer chassis can cost a customer's harvest; a leaking water tank wastes a day's labour; a broken plough delays planting. The most frequent advanced-level mistakes and their fixes are:

  • Incorrect polarity: E7018 requires DC+ (electrode positive) for proper penetration. Running it on AC or DCâ produces a soft arc and poor fusion. Fix: verify machine polarity before every job.
  • Long arc: Causes spatter, porosity, and loss of shielding. Fix: keep arc length equal to electrode diameter; listen for a crisp "frying" sound.
  • Travel speed too fast: Produces a narrow, convex bead with poor toe fusion. Fix: slow down and watch the puddle wet out at the edges.
  • Slag entrapment in multi-pass: Caused by not cleaning between passes. Fix: chip, brush, and inspect every pass â no exceptions.
  • Moisture in low-hydrogen electrodes: E7018 absorbs moisture, causing porosity and hydrogen cracking. Fix: store in a sealed container with desiccant; re-bake at 300â350Â°C for 1â2 hours if exposed.
  • Welding over rust or paint: Causes porosity and lack of fusion. Fix: grind to bright metal; use E6011 for dirty field repairs.
