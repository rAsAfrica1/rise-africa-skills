1. Measuring and Cutting Metal Sheets

Before you cut any metal sheet, you must measure twice. Use a steel tape measure (5m or 8m) and a carpenter's square. Mark your cut line with a fine-tip marker or chalk. For straight cuts, use a nibbler or electric shears. Never use a grinder with a cutting discâit heats the metal, damages the zinc coating, and causes rust. If you only have hand tools, use aviation snips (left and right). For long cuts, clamp a straight edge (like a piece of angle iron) to guide your snips. Always wear glovesâmetal edges are razor-sharp.

When cutting corrugated sheets, cut from the flat side, not the ribs. This prevents distortion. For standing seam panels, cut with a profile cutter or hand snips, then deburr the edge with a file. Measure the roof slope length first: from the ridge to the eave, add 50mm overhang. For example, if your slope is 4.2m, cut sheets to 4.25m. Always allow 100mm overlap for end laps on corrugated sheets. Mark sheets with a crayon or marker on the back side to avoid visible marks.

In Africa, many roofs are installed in hot sun. Work early morning or late afternoon. Metal expands: a 3m sheet can grow 3mm when heated. Cut sheets at the same temperature they will be installed. If you cut in cool morning, they may buckle when the sun hits. Store cut sheets flat on wooden pallets, not on bare ground. Use a straight edge and measure diagonals to ensure square cuts. A square cut saves time during installation and prevents leaks.

Tools you need: tape measure, chalk line, aviation snips, nibbler, electric shears, file, gloves, safety glasses. For curved cuts, use a jigsaw with a metal blade. Always cut away from yourself. After cutting, sweep away metal shavingsâthey rust and stain the roof. A clean cut is a professional cut. Practice on scrap pieces until you can cut a straight line within 1mm. That skill will make your installations look neat and last longer.

2. Fastening Methods: Screws, Clips, and Seams

Fastening metal roofing correctly is the difference between a leak-free roof and a call-back. Use self-drilling screws with EPDM washers. For corrugated sheets, screw through the crest (the top of the rib) not the valley. For trapezoidal sheets, screw through the flat pan. Screw length: for 0.5mm sheet to timber purlin, use 65mm screws. For steel purlins, use self-tapping screws with a drill point. Drive screws perpendicular to the sheet. Over-tightening crushes the washer and causes leaks; under-tightening leaves the washer loose. The washer should just compressâabout 1mm.

Screw spacing: for corrugated sheets, fix at every second crest along the purlin, and every 300mm along the sheet edge. In high-wind areas (coastal or open plains), reduce spacing to every crest at edges and 200mm at ridges. Use at least 6 screws per square metre. For standing seam, use concealed clips. Clips are made of stainless steel or galvanised steel. Space clips at 400-600mm centres. Clips allow the metal to expand and contract. Never screw through the standing seam itselfâthat creates a leak path.

Seams: for standing seam, you can use a hand seamer or a mechanical seamer. For snap-lock seams, press the seam together by hand or with a rubber mallet. For double-lock seams, use a seaming tool. Always start seaming from the eave and work up to the ridge. For corrugated side laps, overlap one corrugation and fasten with screws every 300mm. Use stitch screws (short screws with washers) for side laps. Do not use nailsâthey loosen and rust. In Africa, many local roofs use nails; upgrade to screws for longevity.

For clips on standing seam, ensure the clip is engaged fully. Test by pulling upâit should not release. For seams, apply a bead of butyl sealant between laps before fastening. This is extra protection. Always wear gloves when handling screwsâthey are sharp. Use a magnetic screwdriver bit for speed. A cordless impact driver (18V) is ideal. Set the clutch to a low torque to avoid over-driving. Practice on scrap until you can drive a screw to the correct depth consistently.

3. Installing Standing Seam Roofing

Standing seam roofing is premium but requires precision. Start by installing the first panel at the gable end. Align it square to the eave and ridge. Use a string line along the eave to keep panels straight. Fix the first panel with clips at 500mm centres. The clips are screwed to the purlins, then the panel snaps over them. Ensure the male leg of the first panel faces away from the prevailing wind. In Africa, wind-driven rain can come from any direction, but generally face the male leg away from the most common storm direction.

Next, lay the second panel. Its female leg overlaps the male leg of the first. Snap them together by pressing down with your foot or a rubber mallet. Do not force itâif it doesn't snap, check for debris. Continue across the roof. At the ridge, cut panels to length allowing 50mm overhang into the ridge cap. At the eave, allow 50mm overhang into the gutter. For long roofs, you may need to join panels end-to-end. Use a 150mm overlap with butyl tape and stitch screws. But for standing seam, it's better to order long panels to avoid end laps.

Thermal movement: standing seam panels can expand up to 1mm per metre. That's why clips are usedâthey allow the panel to slide. Never fix panels directly with screws through the seam. For a 10m panel, expansion can be 10mm. If you fix it rigidly, it will buckle or tear. Clips must be spaced so that the panel can move. At the ridge, use a ridge cap that allows movementâa 'floating' ridge cap. At the eave, use a fixed drip edge.

Seaming: if you have a mechanical seamer, run it along the seam from eave to ridge. For snap-lock, you can use a hand seamer every 500mm. For double-lock, use a seamer tool. Always test a seam by pulling. In Africa, many installers use a simple hand seamer and a mallet. That's fine for small roofs. For large roofs, rent a seamer. After installation, check for any open seams. Apply sealant if needed. Clean the roof with a soft brushânever use abrasive pads. A properly installed standing seam roof can last 50 years.

4. Installing Corrugated Metal Roofing

Corrugated metal roofing is the most common in Africa. It's affordable and easy to install. Start at the eave and work up to the ridge. Lay the first sheet with the corrugations running down the slope. Overhang the eave by 50mm into the gutter. Fix the sheet with screws at every second crest along the purlin. For the first sheet, screw at the edge crests and then every second crest. Use a string line to keep the bottom edge straight. If the eave is not straight, the roof looks bad and leaks.

Side laps: overlap one full corrugation. The overlapping sheet should be on the side away from prevailing wind. So if wind comes from the left, the left sheet goes under the right sheet. This prevents wind-driven rain from entering. Fix the side lap with stitch screws every 300mm. End laps: when you need to join sheets lengthwise, overlap 150mm. Apply butyl tape between the laps. Fix with stitch screws at every crest. For roofs with a slope less than 15 degrees, increase end lap to 200mm and use more screws.

Purlins: corrugated sheets need purlins at 900mm to 1200mm centres. For heavy rain areas, use 900mm. Purlins can be timber (50x75mm) or steel (C-section). Ensure purlins are straight and level. Fix sheets to purlins with self-drilling screws. For timber, use 65mm screws. For steel, use self-tapping screws. Drive screws perpendicular. Don't overdrive. In hot climates, metal expands. Leave a 2mm gap between sheets at side laps? No, overlap tightly. Expansion is handled by the corrugations.

Cutting: use nibbler or snips. Never use a grinder. For ridges, use ridge caps (bent to match corrugation). Fix ridge caps with screws every 300mm. For hips, use hip caps. For valleys, use valley flashing. At gable ends, use barge caps. Always walk on the crests, not the valleys, to avoid denting. Wear soft-soled shoes. In Africa, many roofs are installed by hand. Use a hammer? No, use a screw gun. If you must use nails, use umbrella head nails, but screws are better. After installation, check for any missing screws. A corrugated roof should last 20-30 years if installed correctly.

5. Flashing and Trim Installation

Flashing is what keeps water out at edges, valleys, and penetrations. Start with eave flashing (drip edge). This is an L-shaped metal strip that goes under the roofing sheet and over the fascia. Fix it with screws every 300mm. The drip edge should extend 20mm beyond the fascia and have a drip lip. Next, gable flashing (barge cap) covers the edge of the roof at the gable. It goes over the roofing sheet and down the side. Fix with screws every 300mm. For corrugated sheets, use a gable cap that matches the corrugation profile.

Ridge flashing: at the peak, use a ridge cap. For corrugated, use a corrugated ridge. For standing seam, use a standing seam ridge. The ridge cap must overlap the sheets by at least 150mm on each side. Fix with screws through the crests. Apply butyl tape under the ridge cap for extra seal. For hips, use hip flashing. For valleys, use valley flashing. Valley flashing is critical: it must be at least 500mm wide, with a centre rib. Fix it under the sheets. The sheets should overlap the valley flashing by 150mm. Use butyl tape between the sheet and the flashing.

Penetrations: for pipes, use a pipe boot (neoprene or silicone). Cut a hole slightly smaller than the pipe. Slide the boot over the pipe and fix the base to the roof with screws and sealant. For chimneys, use a chimney flashing kit. It includes a front apron, side flashing, and back flashing. The back flashing goes under the roofing sheet, the side flashing goes over, and the front apron goes over. Seal all edges with polyurethane sealant. For vents, use a vent flashing.

In Africa, many leaks come from poor flashing. Always use butyl tape or polyurethane sealant under flashings. Never rely on just screws. For all flashings, use screws with EPDM washers. Space screws at 200-300mm. After installation, check for gaps. If you can see daylight, water will come in. Use a sealant that is UV-resistant. In hot climates, cheap sealants crack. Use Sikaflex or similar. Also, ensure flashings are fixed to solid backingânot just to the sheet. A flashing that moves will leak. Always run a bead of sealant along the top edge of flashings. That's the most common leak point.

6. Sealing and Waterproofing Joints

Even the best roof can leak if joints are not sealed. Use butyl tape for laps between sheets. Butyl tape is a mastic tape that comes in rolls. It is sticky and never hardens. Apply it between the overlapping sheets at side laps and end laps. For end laps, place the tape 20mm from the edge. When you screw the lap, the tape compresses and seals. For side laps, apply a continuous bead. Do not stretch the tapeâjust lay it. In Africa, butyl tape is available in most hardware stores. It is better than silicone for laps because it doesn't crack.

For flashings, use polyurethane sealant. Apply a bead along the top edge of the flashing and where it meets the roof. Smooth it with a spatula or your finger (wear gloves). For pipe boots, seal the base with polyurethane. For ridge caps, apply butyl tape under the cap. For valleys, apply butyl tape under the sheet where it overlaps the valley flashing. For any screw that is in a vulnerable area, you can apply a dab of sealant over the screw head. But if you use EPDM washers correctly, that's usually enough.

Waterproofing: after installation, inspect the roof on a sunny day. Look for any gaps, missing screws, or open laps. Use a hose to test for leaks. Start at the ridge and work down. If you find a leak, mark it and fix it. For small leaks, use a brushable sealant (like a fibreglass roof sealant). For larger gaps, use a flashing patch. For emergency repairs, use a self-adhesive flashing tape. Clean the area first with a wire brush. Apply the tape and press firmly. In Africa, many roofs leak at the ridge and at penetrations. So focus there.

Maintenance: check your roof twice a yearâbefore and after the rainy season. Tighten any loose screws. Replace cracked washers. Reapply sealant if it's peeling. Keep gutters clean. Trim tree branches that rub the roof. If you have a corrugated roof, you can paint it with a metal roof paint to extend life. For standing seam, never walk on the seamsâwalk on the pans. Use a soft brush to clean. A well-sealed roof will keep you dry for decades. Remember: water always finds the lowest point. So make sure every joint is higher than the next, and every lap sheds water. That's the principle.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Installation Techniques for Metal Roofing Sheets

This practical reference is written for roofing contractors, hardware suppliers, and agribusiness facility managers across Africa who install or supervise metal roofing on warehouses, poultry houses, grain stores, agro-processing sheds, and staff housing. Metal roofing is one of the highest-margin product lines for building-material dealers in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, and South Africa â but margin is destroyed by callbacks, leaks, and rust complaints. Getting installation right the first time protects your reputation, reduces warranty claims, and keeps labour crews productive during the rainy season when demand peaks.

Metal roofing in Africa typically means either corrugated galvanised or Al-Zn coated sheets (the low-cost, high-volume product sold through hardware shops) or standing seam / concealed-fix systems (premium, longer spans, better for large agricultural buildings). The techniques below apply to both, with notes on where they diverge. Always confirm the manufacturer's installation manual for the specific profile you are selling or installing â profiles differ, and a wrong lap or fastener choice is the most common cause of leaks.

1. Measuring and Cutting Metal Sheets

Measure roof length and width, then add for overhangs (typically 300â600 mm at eaves, 150â300 mm at gables). Order sheets in the longest practical length to reduce end laps â for corrugated sheets, keep runs under 6 m where transport allows, because longer sheets are harder to handle on site and more prone to buckling. Cut with a nibbler, power shear, or fine-tooth hacksaw. Never use an angle grinder with an abrasive disc â the heat burns off the zinc/aluminium coating and the steel swarf embeds in the surface, causing rust within months. This is the single biggest avoidable failure we see on African sites. Cut sheets on a clean tarpaulin, not on bare ground, and wipe off all filings before installation.

2. Fastening Methods: Screws, Clips, and Seams

For corrugated and IBR (inverted box rib) sheets, use self-drilling screws with EPDM bonded washers, driven at the crest of the corrugation, not the trough. Over-tightening crushes the washer and creates a leak path; under-tightening leaves the sheet loose and noisy. For standing seam, use concealed clips fixed to the purlin, with the seam folded or snapped over â no penetrations through the sheet face, which is why standing seam lasts longer in coastal and high-rainfall areas. Seams must be closed with the manufacturer's seaming tool, not by hand.

3. Installing Standing Seam Roofing

Standing seam is increasingly specified for large agribusiness buildings â grain silos, cold stores, and export-pack houses â because it handles thermal movement and resists wind uplift. Install clips on purlins, lay sheets in the direction of prevailing wind so laps face away from driving rain, and engage the seam by hand before closing with the seaming tool. Allow for thermal expansion: use fixed clips at the centre and sliding clips at the ends. This is not a DIY product; train your crew or sub-contract to a certified installer.

4. Installing Corrugated Metal Roofing

Start at the leeward gable end and work across, laying sheets so the overlapping edge faces away from prevailing wind. Walk on the purlin lines, not between them, and wear soft-soled shoes. For long runs, use a string line to keep the eave straight â a wavy eave line is the first thing a client notices and the first thing they complain about. Fix edge sheets first, then work inward.

5. Flashing and Trim Installation

Ridge caps, barge boards, eave flashings, and valley gutters must be fixed with the same fastener system and sealed with neutral-cure silicone or butyl tape â not with cement mortar, which cracks and traps water. Overlap flashings by at least 150 mm in the direction of water flow. On agricultural buildings, pay special attention to penetrations for vents, chimneys, and solar mounts â these are leak hotspots.

6. Sealing and Waterproofing Joints

Use EPDM washers under every fastener, butyl tape under flashings, and neutral-cure silicone at complex junctions. Avoid acid-cure silicone on metal â it corrodes the coating. Re-check all fasteners after the first heavy rain; thermal movement in African day/night temperature swings is significant, and a screw that was tight at midday may be loose by morning.

  • Metal roofing is slippery when wet, dusty, or oily â never walk on a wet roof.
  • Use edge protection, harnesses anchored to a certified point, and safety lines. Falls are the leading cause of fatality in roofing across Africa.
  • Never walk on the unsupported pan of a sheet â step only on purlin lines or use crawl boards.
  • Wear cut-resistant gloves when handling sheet edges; they are razor-sharp.
  • Keep the site clear of children and untrained workers, especially on school and clinic projects.
  • During lightning season, do not work on or near metal roofs when storms are approaching.
