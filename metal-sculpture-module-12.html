1. Building a Portfolio: Photography and Documentation

A strong portfolio is your ticket to galleries, commissions, and clients. You don't need a fancy cameraâa modern smartphone will do. Start by documenting every piece you make. Before you sell or ship a sculpture, take these five shots: front, back, side, top, and a detail close-up of the most interesting part. Use natural light, ideally on a cloudy day or in open shade, to avoid harsh shadows. Place the sculpture on a neutral backgroundâa plain concrete wall, a large cardboard sheet, or a clean cloth. If the piece is small, set it on a table; if large, clear the area around it. Use a tripod or steady your phone on a stack of bricks. Set your phone to the highest resolution and avoid digital zoom. After shooting, edit lightly: crop to square or 4:5 ratio for Instagram, adjust brightness and contrast, but don't over-filter. For each piece, write a short description: title, materials (e.g., 'welded scrap steel from a Nairobi junkyard'), dimensions (height x width x depth in cm), year, and a sentence about your inspiration. Store all photos and text in a folder on your phone and back it up to Google Drive or a free Dropbox account. Create a simple PDF portfolio using Canva or Microsoft Wordâ10 to 15 best works. Also make a physical portfolio: print 8x10 inch photos and put them in a plastic sleeve binder. When you meet a gallery owner or client, you can show both. Update your portfolio every 3 months. Remember: good photos sell sculptures. A client in Lagos or London will decide based on your images, not the actual piece. So invest time in learning basic photographyâwatch free YouTube tutorials. Also document your process: photos of you welding, grinding, and assembling. These 'behind-the-scenes' shots build trust and tell your story. Finally, get a free website using WordPress or Wix to host your portfolio online. Link it to your Instagram and WhatsApp Business. This is your 24/7 gallery.

2. Pricing and Marketing Your Work

Pricing metal sculpture is part art, part math. Start by calculating your costs: materials (steel, welding rods, gas, grinding discs, paint), labor (hours x hourly rate), and overhead (rent, electricity, tools depreciation). For example, if you spend 10 hours at 500 KES/hour, that's 5,000 KES labor. Materials might cost 3,000 KES. Overhead add 20% = 1,600 KES. Total cost = 9,600 KES. Now add profit marginâaim for 30-50% for local sales, 100% for international. So wholesale price to a gallery might be 12,000 KES, retail 18,000 KES. Never underprice; it devalues your work and makes it hard to raise prices later. For commissions, ask for 50% deposit upfront, 50% on delivery. For marketing, use social media: Instagram and Facebook are key. Post 3-4 times a week: finished pieces, work-in-progress, and short videos of you welding or grinding. Use hashtags like #metalsculpture #africanart #scrapmetalart #kenyanartist. Engage with local businesses: hotels, restaurants, offices often need lobby sculptures. Visit them with your portfolio. Also attend craft markets and trade fairsârent a booth for 5,000-10,000 KES and display your best 5-10 pieces. Price clearly. Accept mobile money (M-Pesa, Airtel Money) and have a card reader if possible. For international sales, use PayPal or Wise. Shipping is expensiveâget quotes from DHL or local courier. Offer to crate the sculpture yourself to save costs. Collaborate with interior designers and architectsâthey have clients with budgets. Give them 10-15% commission. Finally, build an email list: collect contacts at shows and send a monthly newsletter with new work. Consistency beats occasional viral posts. Treat your art as a business: keep receipts, track sales in a notebook or Excel, and set aside 20% for taxes and reinvestment.

3. Studio Setup and Shop Management

A functional metal shop needs three zones: dirty (cutting, grinding, welding), clean (assembly, finishing, painting), and storage (tools, materials, finished work). If you're in a shared space or backyard, use tarps or metal sheets to separate. Floor should be concreteâwood floors are fire hazards. Ventilation is critical: welding fumes and grinding dust are toxic. Install a roof vent or use a large fan to pull air out. If you weld inside, buy a fume extractor (around 20,000 KES) or at least a respirator with P100 filters. Lighting: bright LED shop lights (5000K) so you can see your work. Power: you need at least 3-phase if you run a big welder, but a 220V single-phase MIG welder works for most. Get a surge protector for your tools. Organize tools on pegboards and shelves. Label everything. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry chemical, 5kg) near the welding area and a first aid kit. For materials, sort scrap by type: mild steel, stainless, aluminum, brass. Keep them dry to prevent rust. Use a cutting area with a chop saw or angle grinder with a metal cut-off wheel. Have a dedicated welding tableâa steel plate on a sturdy frame, with clamps. For finishing, a separate table with vice, files, sandpaper, and paint. Shop management: create a daily checklist: turn on ventilation, check gas lines, inspect cords. After work: sweep floor, put tools away, turn off gas and power. Keep a logbook for maintenance: change grinder discs, check oil in compressor. Budget for consumables monthly: welding wire, gas, discs, paint, electrodes. Buy in bulk to save. Insurance: if you have employees, get workman's compensation. For your own safety, wear PPE: welding helmet, gloves, apron, boots, ear protection. Set up a small office corner with a desk, computer, and printer for invoices and portfolio. If you can't afford a separate studio, partner with a local mechanic or welder and share rent. Many successful African sculptors started in a corner of a garage. The key is consistency and safety.

4. Health and Safety Regulations for Metal Shops

Metalwork is dangerous. Every year, African artisans suffer burns, eye injuries, and lung damage from ignoring safety. You must follow basic rules. First, personal protective equipment (PPE): always wear a welding helmet with at least a #10 lens for arc welding, safety glasses for grinding and cutting, leather gloves, a flame-resistant apron or jacket, steel-toe boots, and earplugs when using loud tools. For welding, use a respirator with P100 filters to avoid metal fume fever. Never wear synthetic clothingâit melts. Second, ventilation: weld and grind outdoors or in a space with a fan pulling air out. If you feel dizzy or nauseous, stop and get fresh air. Third, fire safety: keep a dry chemical fire extinguisher within 3 meters of your work area. Never weld near flammable liquids, paper, or wood. Have a bucket of sand or water for small fires. Fourth, electrical safety: inspect cords for cuts, use grounded outlets, don't overload circuits. If you get a shock, turn off power before touching the person. Fifth, tool safety: use guards on angle grinders, never remove them. Use the right disc for the jobâcutting discs for cutting, grinding discs for grinding. Don't force a tool. Keep blades sharp. Sixth, chemical safety: paints, solvents, and degreasers are toxic. Store them in sealed containers away from sparks. Wear nitrile gloves when handling. Seventh, ergonomics: lift with your legs, not your back. Use a cart for heavy steel. Take breaks every hour to stretch. Eighth, first aid: know where your kit is. For burns, cool with water for 10 minutes, cover with sterile gauze. For cuts, apply pressure and seek medical help if deep. For eye injuries, flush with water and go to a clinic. Ninth, legal: in many African countries, you need a license to operate a metal shop. Check with local authorities. If you employ workers, you must provide PPE and training. Keep a record of accidents. Finally, create a safety culture: post signs, hold weekly 5-minute safety talks, and lead by example. Your life and limbs are worth more than any sculpture.

5. Exhibition Opportunities and Galleries

Exhibiting your work is the fastest way to gain recognition and sales. Start local: coffee shops, restaurants, hotels, and community centers often display art for free or a small commission. Approach them with a polite email or visit in person with your portfolio. Offer to hang 5-10 pieces for one month. For a more formal show, apply to group exhibitions at national museums, art centers, or biennials. In Africa, key events include the Dakar Biennale, Cape Town Art Fair, and Nairobi's GoDown Arts Centre. Check their websites for open callsâusually 6 months in advance. Prepare a proposal: artist statement, 10 images, dimensions, and a budget (if seeking funding). For a solo exhibition, you need 15-20 pieces. Rent a gallery spaceâcosts range from 20,000 to 100,000 KES per week in Nairobi. Alternatively, use an alternative space: a warehouse, a park, or your own studio. Invite press, collectors, and fellow artists. Send invitations via WhatsApp and email. On opening night, have a guest book, price list, and someone to handle sales. Galleries typically take 30-50% commission. Before signing a contract, read the terms: exclusivity, duration, insurance, and who pays for shipping. Never give a gallery your only copy of a piece. For international exposure, submit to online galleries like Saatchi Art or Artsy. They charge a fee but reach global buyers. Also consider art fairs: rent a booth for 50,000-200,000 KES. Share a booth with another artist to cut costs. When you exhibit, document everything: photos, videos, visitor comments. Use these for future proposals. Follow up with everyone you meetâsend a thank-you note and add them to your mailing list. Finally, don't wait for a gallery to discover you. Create your own exhibition: a pop-up show in a parking lot, a hotel lobby, or a friend's shop. The key is to get your work seen. As a metal sculptor, your pieces are heavyâso choose venues with easy access and strong floors. Always have a helper for installation. And remember: every exhibition is a learning opportunity. Even if you sell nothing, you gain feedback and contacts.

6. Final Project: Complete a Sculpture for Exhibition

This is your capstone: create a sculpture worthy of a public exhibition. Start by choosing a theme that matters to youâmaybe a social issue, a local legend, or a abstract form. Sketch 3 ideas on paper. Pick one that is feasible with your skills and materials. For example, a 1.5-meter tall welded steel figure made from scrap car parts. Write a project plan: dimensions, materials list, tools needed, timeline (4-6 weeks), and budget. Gather materials: visit a junkyard and buy 50kg of mild steel scrap for about 2,000 KES. Collect cutting discs, welding wire, gas, grinding discs, and paint. Set up your workspace with good ventilation and safety gear. Begin by building a maquetteâa small model (30cm) using wire or cardboard to test proportions. Then cut and weld the main structure. Work in stages: first the armature (skeleton), then add mass. Use jigs and clamps to hold pieces square. Weld safely: tack weld first, then complete. Grind smooth where needed. For texture, leave some welds visible or hammer the metal. Once assembled, clean with a wire brush and degrease. Apply a primer (red oxide) then topcoatâeither clear lacquer for raw steel look or colored enamel. Let dry 24 hours. Photograph the finished piece from all angles. Write an artist statement (150 words) explaining your concept. Prepare a price: cost of materials + labor (say 40 hours at 500 KES = 20,000 KES) + 30% profit = around 30,000 KES. Now find an exhibition venue. Contact a local gallery, hotel, or community center. Offer to show your piece for one month. If they agree, arrange transportâa pickup truck or boda boda with a trailer. Install safely: use a dolly, secure with straps. Create a label with title, materials, dimensions, and price. Invite friends, family, and potential buyers via WhatsApp and social media. At the opening, be present to talk about your work. If it doesn't sell, don't be discouragedâthe experience is invaluable. Document the entire process for your portfolio. After the show, ask for feedback and write a reflection. This final project proves you are a professional metal sculptor. It also gives you a centerpiece for future exhibitions and a confidence boost. Remember: every great sculptor started with one finished piece. Now you have yours.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Professional Practice and Portfolio for Metal Sculptors

This module is built for working African metal sculptors who want to turn craft into a sustainable business. It covers pricing, photography, studio management, health and safety, and exhibition strategy. The emphasis is on practical systems you can implement immediately, using locally available materials and markets.

1. Building a Portfolio: Photography and Documentation

Your portfolio is your shop window. Buyers, galleries, and grant panels in Africa and abroad decide within seconds whether to engage. You do not need an expensive camera. A modern smartphone with a 12MP sensor, shot in natural daylight against a neutral background, outperforms a poorly lit studio shot every time.

Shoot each piece from at least three angles: front, three-quarter, and detail. Include a scale reference such as a coin or a person. For outdoor works, photograph in situ. Keep a written record: title, year, materials, dimensions, weight, and price. This data feeds directly into your pricing and marketing.

Store images in a cloud folder and back them up on a physical drive. Name files consistently: 2024_OxidisedSteel_Guardian_120x45x30.jpg. This makes cataloguing and submission easy.

3. Studio Setup and Shop Management

A functional metal shop needs four zones: hot work, cold work, finishing, and storage. Separate them with fire-resistant screens. Good lighting, ventilation, and a clear layout reduce accidents and increase output.

Keep a job board. Every commission gets a card with client name, deposit paid, deadline, and balance due. Use a simple ledger or a free app like Wave or Zoho Books. Track income and expenses weekly. This discipline is what separates a business from a hobby.

4. Health and Safety Regulations for Metal Shops

African countries have national OHS frameworks. South Africaâs OHS Act 85 of 1993 and the General Safety Regulations require risk assessments, PPE, and incident reporting. 6. Final Project: Complete a Sculpture for Exhibition

Your final project is a single exhibition-ready sculpture. It must be documented, priced, insured, and installed. Deliverables: a professional photo set, a one-page artist statement, a price sheet, and a condition report. Present it to a panel or at a pop-up show. This is your proof of professional practice.
