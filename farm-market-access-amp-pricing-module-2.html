Lessons

1. Listing all your farm costs: seeds, labour, transport

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know every cost that goes into your produce. Start by writing down every single thing you spend money on for your farm. This includes seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, and tools. But it also includes things you might forget, like the cost of renting land, water for irrigation, and even the fuel you use to go to the market. If you pay someone to help you, that labour cost must be listed. If you transport your produce to the market, that transport cost must be listed. If you use your own bicycle or motorbike, estimate the fuel and wear-and-tear cost.

Let's take a real example. Mama Aisha grows tomatoes on half an acre in Tanzania. She buys seeds for 20,000 TZS, fertiliser for 50,000 TZS, and pesticides for 15,000 TZS. She hires two workers for weeding and harvesting at 10,000 TZS each, so labour is 20,000 TZS. She rents a pickup to take her tomatoes to the market for 30,000 TZS. She also spends 5,000 TZS on packaging boxes. Her total costs are 20,000 + 50,000 + 15,000 + 20,000 + 30,000 + 5,000 = 140,000 TZS. That is her total cost for the season.

But waitdid she include the cost of her own time? Many farmers forget this. Even if you do the work yourself, your time has value. If you could earn 5,000 TZS per day working for someone else, and you spent 20 days on the farm, that is 100,000 TZS of your own labour. For now, you can note it separately, but it is important to know. Also, if you used manure from your own cows, that has a value tooyou could have sold it. List everything, even if you didn't pay cash.

Use a simple notebook. Draw three columns: Date, Item, Cost. Every time you spend on the farm, write it down immediately. Don't wait until the end of the season. At the end of the season, add up all the costs. This total is your farm cost. Without this list, you are guessing. And guessing leads to losses. So start today: take a notebook and list every cost from your last season. You will be surprised how much you actually spent.

2. Calculating the cost per kilo of your produce

Once you have listed all your costs, the next step is to find out how much each kilo of your produce costs you to grow. This is called the cost per kilo. It tells you the minimum price you must charge to break even. To calculate it, divide your total farm costs by the total number of kilos you harvested. For example, if your total costs are 140,000 TZS and you harvested 200 kilos of tomatoes, your cost per kilo is 140,000 200 = 700 TZS per kilo. That means you cannot sell below 700 TZS per kilo without losing money.

But be careful: your harvest is not always perfect. Some produce gets spoiled, eaten by pests, or lost in transport. So you should calculate your cost per kilo based on the kilos you actually sell, not the kilos you harvest. If you harvested 200 kilos but only 160 kilos were good enough to sell, then your cost per kilo is 140,000 160 = 875 TZS per kilo. That is a big difference! So always use the sellable kilos.

Another thing to remember: your cost per kilo changes with the season. If you have a bad harvest, your cost per kilo goes up. If you have a bumper harvest, it goes down. So you should calculate it every season. Also, if you grow more than one crop, calculate the cost per kilo for each crop separately. You cannot mix tomatoes and maize. For each crop, list only the costs that went into that crop. If you used the same labour for both, you need to estimate how much time was spent on each. For example, if you spent 10 days on tomatoes and 20 days on maize, then two-thirds of your labour cost goes to maize.

Keep a simple record: Total cost for the crop, total sellable kilos, and then cost per kilo. Write it in your notebook. This number is your break-even price. When a buyer offers you a price, you can quickly check: is it above my cost per kilo? If not, you are losing money. So always know your cost per kilo before you go to the market.

3. Separating personal and farm expenses

One of the biggest mistakes small-scale farmers make is mixing personal money with farm money. If you use the same pocket for buying food for your family and buying seeds for your farm, you will never know if your farm is profitable. You might think you are making money, but actually you are eating your profits. To avoid this, you must separate your personal expenses from your farm expenses. This means having two separate records, or even two separate purses or bank accounts if possible.

Let's say you sell a sack of maize for 100,000 TZS. If you take that money and buy shoes for your child, that is a personal expense. It should not be counted as a farm cost. But if you take that same money and buy fertiliser for the next season, that is a farm cost. The problem is when you use farm money for personal needs and then forget to replace it. For example, you sell tomatoes for 50,000 TZS and use 20,000 TZS to pay school fees. If you don't record that as a personal withdrawal, you might later think you have 50,000 TZS for the farm, but you only have 30,000 TZS. This leads to confusion and poor planning.

A simple method: keep a notebook with two sections. One section for farm income and expenses, another for personal income and expenses. Every time you spend money, decide: is this for the farm or for me? Write it in the correct section. If you take money from the farm for personal use, write it as a loan from the farm to yourself. Then when you get personal money, pay it back. This keeps your farm business clean.

Another tip: pay yourself a small salary from the farm. For example, decide that each month you will take 20,000 TZS as your personal pay. That way, you don't randomly take money. You know exactly how much you can use. At the end of the season, you can see if the farm made a profit after paying you. If it didn't, then you are not running a businessyou are just surviving. Separating expenses helps you see the truth and make better decisions.

4. Why selling below cost is dangerous

Selling below your cost per kilo is like digging a hole and throwing your money into it. Many farmers do this without knowing. They see a buyer offering a low price and think, 'At least I am getting some money.' But if that price is below your cost, you are actually losing money with every sale. The more you sell, the poorer you become. This is dangerous because it can push you into debt, force you to sell your land or animals, and eventually destroy your farm business.

Let's use an example. You calculated your cost per kilo for tomatoes as 875 TZS. A trader comes and offers you 600 TZS per kilo. If you sell 100 kilos, you receive 60,000 TZS. But your cost for those 100 kilos was 87,500 TZS. So you have lost 27,500 TZS. That is money you will never get back. If you do this every season, you will soon have no money to buy seeds for the next season. You might borrow from a friend or a microfinance institution, but then you have to pay interest. The hole gets deeper.

Why do farmers sell below cost? Sometimes it is because they don't know their cost. Sometimes it is because they are desperate for cash. Sometimes it is because they have no storage and the produce will spoil. But even in desperation, selling below cost is not a solutionit is a slow death. It is better to find a way to add value, like drying the tomatoes, or to find another market, or to negotiate. If you must sell below cost, do it only as a last resort, and know exactly how much you are losing.

A better strategy is to set a minimum price. Before you go to the market, decide: 'I will not sell below 900 TZS per kilo.' If the buyer offers less, walk away. You might have to wait, but you will protect your business. Also, try to reduce your costs so that your cost per kilo is lower. Can you make your own compost instead of buying fertiliser? Can you share transport with a neighbour? The lower your cost, the more room you have to negotiate. Remember: profit is not what you receiveit is what you keep after all costs are paid.

5. Using a simple notebook to track costs

You don't need a computer or a fancy app to track your farm costs. A simple notebook and a pen are enough. The key is to write down every cost as soon as it happens. Don't rely on your memoryyou will forget. At the end of the day, sit down and record what you spent. Even if it is just 500 TZS for a bottle of water for your workers, write it down. These small amounts add up.

Set up your notebook with clear columns. On each page, draw lines to make columns for: Date, Description, Category, Amount, and Total. The category helps you group costs. For example, you can have categories like Seeds, Labour, Transport, Fertiliser, and Other. At the bottom of the page, write the total for that page. At the end of the month, add up all the totals from each page to get your monthly total. At the end of the season, add up all the monthly totals to get your total farm cost.

Here is a real example from a farmer in Kenya. John grows kale. His notebook has entries like: '5 Jan, Kale seeds, Seeds, 1,200 KES', '7 Jan, Paid Mary for weeding, Labour, 800 KES', '10 Jan, Bought manure, Fertiliser, 2,000 KES', '15 Jan, Transport to market, Transport, 500 KES'. At the end of the month, he adds them up: 1,200 + 800 + 2,000 + 500 = 4,500 KES. He does this every month. At the end of the season, he knows exactly how much he spent. Then he divides by the kilos he sold to get his cost per kilo.

You can also use your notebook to track income. Make a separate section for sales. Write the date, what you sold, how many kilos, and the price per kilo. Then at the end, subtract total costs from total income to see your profit or loss. Keep your notebook in a safe, dry place. Bring it to the market so you can record sales immediately. Over time, you will see patterns: which costs are highest, which months you spend more. This information helps you plan better. A notebook is your best business tool.

6. Adjusting costs when prices change

Farm costs are not fixed. The price of seeds, fertiliser, labour, and transport can change from season to season, or even within a season. When these prices change, your cost per kilo changes too. If you don't adjust your selling price, you might end up selling below cost without realising it. So you must review your costs regularly and update your calculations.

For example, last season you bought a bag of fertiliser for 30,000 TZS. This season, the price has gone up to 40,000 TZS. If you use two bags, your fertiliser cost has increased by 20,000 TZS. If your harvest is the same, your cost per kilo will be higher. You need to recalculate. Let's say last season your total cost was 140,000 TZS for 200 kilos, so 700 TZS per kilo. This season, with the higher fertiliser, your total cost is 160,000 TZS. If you still harvest 200 kilos, your cost per kilo is now 800 TZS. So you cannot sell at 700 TZS anymoreyou would lose money. You must aim for at least 800 TZS.

What if the market price for your produce has also changed? Maybe last season you sold tomatoes at 1,000 TZS per kilo, but this season buyers are offering only 750 TZS. If your cost is 800 TZS, you are in trouble. You have three options: find a way to reduce costs, find a different market that pays more, or switch to a different crop. For example, you could use more compost and less fertiliser to cut costs. Or you could sell directly to consumers instead of through a middleman. Or you could grow a crop that is in high demand.

Keep a simple record of price changes. In your notebook, write down the prices you pay for key inputs each season. Also write down the selling prices you receive. Compare them. If you see that costs are rising faster than selling prices, you need to act. Maybe you can negotiate with your input supplier for a discount, or buy in bulk with other farmers. The important thing is to be flexible. Don't assume that last season's numbers still work. Always recalculate your cost per kilo whenever a major cost changes. That way, you protect your profit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Costing Your Farm Produce for Profit

In African smallholder and emerging commercial farming, the difference between a farm that survives and one that collapses often comes down to one skill: knowing your true cost of production. This reference module gives you the working tools tables, checklists, and a visual framework to calculate, track, and adjust your farm costs so that every sale you make builds your business rather than eroding it.

Whether you are selling maize in Nakuru, tomatoes in Kumasi, or eggs in Lilongwe, the principle is the same: you cannot price for profit if you do not know your cost per kilo. This lesson walks through the practical mechanics of listing costs, calculating cost per unit, separating personal from farm spending, and responding when market prices shift.

1. Listing All Your Farm Costs

The first discipline is completeness. Most farmers undercount because they forget the small, recurring expenses. Your cost list should include:

  • Seeds and planting material purchased seed, cuttings, seedlings, or retained seed valued at market price.
  • Labour hired labour (land prep, weeding, harvesting) and the imputed value of family labour. Even if you do not pay cash, family labour has an opportunity cost.
  • Transport from farm to market, including fuel, hire fees, and loading/offloading charges.
  • Inputs fertilizer, pesticides, herbicides, veterinary costs, feed, water.
  • Packaging sacks, crates, jerrycans, boxes.
  • Land and equipment rent, ploughing hire, depreciation on tools.
  • Market fees cess, gate fees, licences, cooperative commissions.

2. Calculating the Cost per Kilo of Your Produce

Once your total cost is known, divide by the total harvest in kilograms. If the tomato plot above yielded 2,000 kg, your cost per kilo is:

68,300 2,000 = KES 34.15 per kg

This is your break-even price. Any sale below KES 34.15 per kg is a loss. Any sale above it contributes to profit. Note that if you harvest less than expected say 1,500 kg due to pests your cost per kilo rises to KES 45.53. This is why yield matters as much as cost control.

3. Separating Personal and Farm Expenses

One of the most common causes of farm business failure in Africa is the mixing of household and farm money. When school fees, medical bills, and farm inputs come from the same pocket without records, you cannot tell whether the farm is profitable or simply subsidising the household.

Practical rule: Open a separate farm account or use a dedicated notebook page for farm transactions only. Pay yourself a fixed monthly "salary" from the farm, and record it as a farm cost. Personal expenses are then paid from that salary not directly from farm sales.

If you cannot open a bank account, use two envelopes or two notebook sections: one labelled "FARM" and one labelled "HOME." Every shilling must go into one or the other, never both.

SAFETY WARNING: Selling Below Cost

Selling below your cost per kilo is not "doing business" it is a slow liquidation of your farm. Each below-cost sale reduces your capital, making it harder to buy inputs for the next season. In extreme cases, it leads to debt cycles, loss of land, and food insecurity. If market prices are persistently below your cost, do not sell at a loss. Instead: (1) reduce costs, (2) add value (processing, grading, packaging), (3) find alternative buyers, or (4) store and sell later. Never accept a price below cost as a permanent strategy.

4. Why Selling Below Cost Is Dangerous

Consider a farmer who sells 2,000 kg of tomatoes at KES 30/kg when cost per kilo is KES 34.15. The loss is KES 4.15 2,000 = KES 8,300. That is money removed from the farm's working capital. Next season, the farmer cannot afford fertilizer, yields drop, cost per kilo rises, and the cycle deepens. This is the debt trap that affects thousands of smallholders across sub-Saharan Africa.

Below-cost selling also distorts local markets. When one farmer dumps produce cheaply, buyers expect lower prices from everyone, dragging the whole community into loss-making sales.

5. Using a Simple Notebook to Track Costs

You do not need software. A single exercise book, ruled into columns, is enough. Create these columns:

  • Date when the expense occurred.
  • Item what you bought or paid for.
  • Category seeds, labour, transport, etc.
  • Amount (cash out) money spent.
  • Income (cash in) money received from sales.
  • Running balance farm account balance.

At the end of each week, total the "Cash Out" column. At the end of the season, total all costs and divide by total kilos sold. This is your actual cost per kilo the most important number in your farm business.

Tip: Record every transaction the same day. Memory is unreliable, and forgotten costs are the biggest cause of inaccurate costing.

6. Adjusting Costs When Prices Change

Input prices and market prices both move. When fertilizer prices rise by 30%, your cost per kilo rises. You must recalculate. When market prices fall, you must decide: can you still cover cost? If not, reduce production, switch crops, or find premium markets.

Build a simple sensitivity habit: every month, ask "What is my cost per kilo now?" and "What price am I getting?" If the gap is shrinking, act early before the season ends.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. List every cost seeds, labour (hired and family), transport, inputs, packaging, fees, and equipment.
  2. Record all transactions in a notebook the same day they happen.
  3. Separate farm money from personal money using two accounts or two envelopes.
  4. Calculate cost per kilo = total cost total kilos harvested.
  5. Never sell below cost per kilo if the market is below cost, reduce costs, add value, or store.
  6. Recalculate cost per kilo every time input prices or yields change.
  7. Pay yourself a fixed salary from the farm and record it as a farm cost.
  8. Review your notebook weekly and your cost per kilo monthly.
