1. Samming and Setting

After tanning, your hides are wet and swollen. Samming removes excess water mechanically. You can use a sammying machine, but many small tanneries in Africa use a hand-cranked wringer or even a clean, smooth log press. The goal is to reduce moisture to about 50-60% of the wet weight. If you skip samming, the next steps like splitting and shaving will be inaccurate because the hide is too spongy.

For a hand method, lay the hide on a clean concrete slab and use a squeegee or a smooth wooden plank to push water out from the center to the edges. Then fold the hide flesh-side out and press it under a heavy stone or a car jack for 30 minutes. You can also build a simple sammying frame with two rollers from an old washing machine. Turn the crank slowly, feeding the hide through. Collect the water in a bucketit can be used for soaking the next batch of raw hides.

Setting is the next step. After samming, the hide is still damp but not dripping. Setting stretches the hide and removes wrinkles. Use a setting stakea rounded wooden post about 1.5 meters tall, anchored in the ground. Drape the hide over the stake and pull it down with both hands, working from the neck to the tail. For small hides like goat or sheep, you can set them on a table with a hand roller. For cattle hides, two people are better: one holds the hide, the other pulls and stretches.

In Africa, many tanners use a 'setting board'a smooth wooden board with a slight curve. Lay the hide grain-side down and use a brass or plastic scraper to push out water and flatten the hide. Be careful not to scrape too hard or you will damage the grain. After setting, the hide should be flat and semi-dry. Hang it in the shade for 1-2 hours before moving to splitting. If you are in a hurry, you can proceed directly to splitting while the hide is still damp.

Remember: clean water and clean tools prevent stains. If you use dirty water, you will get dark spots that are hard to remove later. Also, never let the hide dry completely before splittingit becomes hard and brittle. Aim for a consistency like stiff leather. Test by pressing your thumb into the hide: it should leave a slight indent but not feel wet.

2. Splitting and Shaving

Splitting and shaving adjust the thickness of your leather. Splitting is done first, usually on a band knife splitting machine. But if you don't have one, you can use a hand splitting knife (a sharp, curved blade) and a wooden block. The idea is to cut the hide horizontally into two layers: the top grain layer and the bottom split. For most African tanneries, you want a grain layer of about 1.2 to 1.5 mm for goats, 1.5 to 2 mm for sheep, and 2 to 3 mm for cattle. The split (the underside) can be used for suede or sold as lower-grade leather.

To split by hand, soak the hide in clean water for 10 minutes to make it pliable. Lay it grain-side down on a flat board. Place a straight edge or a metal ruler along the line you want to cut. Use the splitting knife at a low angle, about 15 degrees, and push it forward with steady pressure. You may need a helper to hold the hide taut. This is slow work, but it gives you control. Practice on scrap pieces first. A common mistake is cutting too deep and ruining the grain. If you cut through the grain, you can't fix it.

Shaving comes after splitting. Shaving thins the hide to a uniform thickness and removes any remaining flesh or fat. A shaving machine has a cylinder with blades, but a hand shaving knife (a two-handled draw knife) works for small hides. Secure the hide on a shaving beama wooden beam about 2 meters long, tilted at 45 degrees. Stand behind the beam, hold the draw knife with both hands, and pull it toward you in smooth strokes. Keep the blade at a slight angle. For cattle hides, you may need a machine; hand shaving is too slow.

In Africa, many tanners use a 'shaving horse'a simple wooden frame that holds the hide. You sit on one end and use a draw knife. The key is to shave evenly. Check thickness with a gauge or a simple caliper. If you don't have a gauge, use a ruler and a coin: a 1 mm thickness is about the thickness of a 1-shilling coin. Shave from the neck to the tail, overlapping strokes. After shaving, the hide should be uniform. Rinse it in clean water to remove shavings.

Always sharpen your knife before shaving. A dull knife tears the hide. Use a whetstone and water. Also, work in a well-lit area so you can see thin spots. If you shave too thin, the leather will tear easily. If you leave it too thick, it will be stiff. Soak the hide briefly if it becomes too dry. After shaving, the hide is ready for neutralization and retanning.

3. Neutralization and Retanning

After tanning, the hide is acidic. Neutralization brings the pH up to about 5.0 to 5.5 so that dyes and fats can penetrate evenly. If you skip neutralization, your dye will be blotchy and your leather will feel harsh. You need a pH meter or pH strips. For a small tannery, use a plastic drum or a concrete pit. Fill with clean water at 30-35C. Add neutralization chemicals: sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) is cheap and effective. Use 1-2% of the hide weight. For a 10 kg hide, that's 100-200 grams. Dissolve it in warm water first, then add to the drum. Turn the hide every 15 minutes for 1 hour. Check pH: it should be around 5.0. If still acidic, add more bicarbonate.

After neutralization, rinse the hide in clean water for 10 minutes. Then retanning. Retanning improves the leather's properties: it makes it softer, fuller, and more resistant to heat and water. Common retanning agents include vegetable tannins (like mimosa or quebracho), synthetic tannins, and chrome salts. In Africa, many tanners use local mimosa extract from Acacia trees. Use 5-10% of the hide weight. For a 10 kg hide, 500-1000 grams. Dissolve in water at 40C. Add the hide and drum for 1-2 hours. You can also add 1-2% of a fatliquor (like sulfated oil) at the same time to soften the leather.

If you don't have a drum, use a pit or a large basin. Turn the hide by hand every 20 minutes. The retanning bath should be warm, not hot. Test the penetration by cutting a small piece: the color should be uniform throughout. If it's only on the surface, continue retanning. After retanning, let the hide drain for 30 minutes. Do not rinsethe retanning agents need to stay in the leather.

In Africa, some tanners use a combination of vegetable and synthetic tannins to get a lighter color. For example, 3% mimosa + 2% synthetic. This gives a good balance of softness and strength. Always wear gloves and goggles when handling chemicals. Store chemicals in a dry place. After retanning, the hide is ready for dyeing and fatliquoring. If you are making sole leather, you might skip dyeing and go straight to stuffing. But for most leathers, dyeing is next.

4. Dyeing and Fatliquoring

Dyeing gives leather its color. You can use aniline dyes (penetrating) or pigment dyes (surface). For African markets, earth tones like brown, tan, and black are popular. Use 1-3% dye based on the hide weight. For a 10 kg hide, 100-300 grams. Dissolve the dye in hot water (60C) first, then add to a drum with enough water to cover the hide (about 200% float, meaning 20 liters for 10 kg hide). Add the hide and drum for 30-60 minutes. For even color, start with the drum turning and add the dye slowly over 10 minutes. Check the color by cutting a small piece. If it's too light, add more dye. If too dark, you can't lighten it, so add dye gradually.

After dyeing, add fatliquor. Fatliquoring lubricates the leather fibers, making it soft and pliable. Common fatliquors are sulfated fish oil, neatsfoot oil, or synthetic oils. Use 5-8% of the hide weight. For a 10 kg hide, 500-800 grams. Warm the fatliquor to 50C and add to the same drum. Drum for another 30-45 minutes. The fatliquor should be fully absorbed. If you see oil floating on the surface, you used too much or the pH is off. Adjust pH to 5.0-5.5 with a little bicarbonate.

In Africa, many tanners use local oils like shea butter or palm oil. Shea butter is solid at room temperature, so melt it first. Use 5% shea butter and 3% sulfated oil for a soft, supple leather. For a more water-resistant leather, add 1-2% of a waterproofing agent like paraffin wax emulsion. Always test on a small piece before treating the whole hide.

After fatliquoring, drain the hide and hang it to dry. Do not rinse. The fatliquor needs to stay in the leather. If you are making leather for shoes, you want a firm but flexible feel. If for garments, softer. Adjust fatliquor amount accordingly. For a 10 kg hide, 500 grams fatliquor gives medium softness; 800 grams gives very soft. After drying, the leather may feel stiff. That's normalit will soften again when you do staking or milling. But if it's too stiff, you didn't use enough fatliquor. Next step is stuffing and oiling for some leathers, but many go straight to drying.

5. Stuffing and Oiling

Stuffing and oiling are additional lubrication steps, mainly for heavy leathers like sole leather or belts. Stuffing means working a mixture of oils, fats, and waxes into the leather. Oiling is similar but uses lighter oils. In Africa, traditional tanners use animal fats like beef tallow or mutton fat, mixed with beeswax and cod liver oil. A typical stuffing mixture: 50% beef tallow, 30% beeswax, 20% cod liver oil. Melt together in a pot over low heat. For 10 kg of leather, use about 1 kg of mixture.

To stuff, heat the leather slightly (30-40C) by leaving it in the sun for 10 minutes or using a warm room. Then apply the melted mixture with a brush or a rag, working it into the flesh side. Pay attention to edges and thick areas. After applying, stack the hides flesh-to-flesh and leave them overnight in a warm place. The heat helps the oils penetrate. The next day, hang the hides to dry in the shade. Do not dry in direct sun or the oils will run off.

For oiling, you can use a simple method: pour oil (like neatsfoot oil or mineral oil) into a shallow tray. Dip a cloth in the oil and rub it into the grain side. Use about 200-300 ml of oil per hide. For a 10 kg hide, 250 ml is enough. Work in circles, then hang to dry. Oiling is faster than stuffing and is good for lighter leathers like upholstery or bags. In Africa, many tanners use used cooking oil (filtered) as a cheap oil. It works, but it can go rancid. Better to use neatsfoot oil or shea olein.

After stuffing or oiling, the leather should feel supple and slightly greasy. Let it dry for 24 hours. Then you can buff the surface with a soft cloth to remove excess oil. If the leather is too greasy, wipe with a dry cloth. If it's too dry, apply more oil. Store in a cool, dry place. Stuffing is essential for sole leather because it makes it water-resistant and durable. For upper leather, you might skip stuffing and just rely on fatliquoring. But for belts, straps, and saddles, stuffing gives that traditional firm-yet-flexible feel. Always test on a scrap piece first to get the right amount.

6. Drying Techniques

Drying is criticalif done wrong, leather can become hard, brittle, or cracked. The goal is to remove water slowly and evenly. Never dry leather in direct hot sun or with a fan. In Africa, the sun is strong, so use shade or a drying shed. Hang hides on poles or racks with good air circulation. For small hides, you can hang them over a rope. For cattle hides, use a frame or a horizontal rack. The ideal drying temperature is 20-30C with 50-60% humidity. If you are in a dry area, you may need to dry in a closed room with a bucket of water to add humidity.

There are several drying methods. Air drying (hanging) is the most common. It takes 1-3 days depending on thickness. For faster drying, use a vacuum dryer or a toggle dryer, but these are expensive. A simple toggle dryer: stretch the hide on a wooden frame with nails or clips, then dry in the shade. This prevents shrinking and gives a flat leather. For small tanneries, a 'drying tunnel' made of mud bricks with a small fire at one end (not too hot) can work, but you must control the smokesmoke can darken the leather.

Another method is 'samming to dryness'after samming, you let the hide dry until it's about 80% dry, then stack it under weights overnight. This equalizes moisture. Then finish drying in the shade. For vegetable-tanned leather, you can dry it in the sun for a short time to get a darker color, but watch it closely. If the leather starts to curl at the edges, it's drying too fast. Move it to the shade.

In Africa, many tanners use a 'drying board'a wooden board with the hide stretched and nailed grain-side down. This gives a smooth grain. After drying, the leather will be stiff. You must 'condition' it by stacking it in a pile for 24 hours. The moisture inside will equalize. Then you can stake or mill it to soften. If you dry too fast, the leather will be hard and you'll need to re-wet and dry again. So patience is key. Test dryness by pressing a thumbnail into the leatherif it leaves a mark, it's still damp. If it's hard and springy, it's dry. Store dried leather in a cool, dark place away from insects.

7. Practical Reference

<h2>Practical Reference: Post-Tanning Operations for African Leather Businesses</h2>

<p>Post-tanning is where tanned hides and skins become saleable leather. In African tanneries from Ethiopia's highland sheepskin sector to Nigeria's goat and cowhide workshops, Kenya's Kariokor clusters, and South Africa's industrial tanneries the margin between profit and loss is decided here. A wet-blue or wet-white hide leaving the tanning drum is a semi-finished commodity. After samming, setting, splitting, shaving, neutralization, retanning, dyeing, fatliquoring, stuffing, and drying, that same hide can be worth two to five times more as finished upholstery, footwear upper, or garment leather.</p>

<p>This practical reference is written for tannery owners, production managers, and technicians who need repeatable outcomes, not laboratory theory. The focus is on process control, water and chemical efficiency, and the quality signals that determine whether a buyer in Addis Ababa, Casablanca, or Milan accepts your lot.</p>

<h2>Why Post-Tanning Is a Business Decision, Not Just a Chemical Step</h2>

<p>In Africa, post-tanning is often where value addition happens or is lost. Ethiopia exports millions of raw sheepskins and wet-blue, but finished leather exports command far higher unit value. Kenya's tannery sector, under the Kenya Leather Development Council, has repeatedly emphasized that upgrading to finished leather is the fastest route to jobs and foreign exchange. The same logic applies at workshop scale: a small tannery in Sokoto or Fez that masters retanning and dyeing can sell directly to footwear and bag makers instead of dumping wet-blue at commodity prices.</p>

<p>Business-first means three things: (1) control your chemical cost per square foot, (2) control your water and effluent cost, and (3) control your rejection rate. Every lesson below is framed around those three numbers.</p>

<h2>Samming and Setting</h2>

<p>Samming removes free water from the wet-blue or wet-white stock so that subsequent mechanical and chemical operations are consistent. Target moisture after samming is typically 5060% (on wet weight). Setting stretches the hide and removes wrinkles, opens the grain, and creates a flat surface for splitting and shaving. In small African tanneries, a sammying machine is often the single most under-used piece of equipment; operators skip it and go straight to shaving, which causes uneven thickness and blade wear.</p>

<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> A well-sammed hide shaves with less blade wear. Track blade change frequency against samming moisture you will see the payback within a month.</p>

<h2>Splitting and Shaving</h2>

<p>Splitting divides thick hides horizontally into a top (grain) split and a bottom (flesh) split. Shaving then brings the grain split to the exact gauge the buyer ordered for example, 1.21.4 mm for footwear upper, 0.81.0 mm for garment. Tolerance is usually 0.2 mm. In Africa, where many tanneries process mixed-origin hides, splitting and shaving are the biggest sources of waste and rework.</p>

<h2>Neutralization and Retanning</h2>

<p>After shaving, the leather is still acidic (pH 3.03.5). Neutralization brings it to pH 5.05.5 so that dyes, fatliquors, and retanning agents can penetrate evenly. Sodium formate, sodium bicarbonate, and ammonium bicarbonate are common neutralizers. Over-neutralization (pH above 6) causes grain swelling and loose grain; under-neutralization causes poor dye penetration and patchy colour.</p>

<p>Retanning rebuilds the leather's body and character. Vegetable retans (mimosa, quebracho, tara) are widely used in Africa because they are locally available and give a firm, full feel. Synthetic retans (acrylic, melamine) improve softness and light-fastness. Chrome retans improve grain tightness. A typical retan recipe might use 48% retanning agent on shaved weight, split across two or three additions.</p>

<h2>Dyeing and Fatliquoring</h2>

<p>Dyeing is done in the drum, usually at 4060C, with acid dyes for aniline finishes and pigment dyes for covered finishes. Levelness depends on neutralization pH, drum speed, float ratio, and dye addition rate. Fatliquoring follows dyeing and is the single most important step for softness, grain strength, and tear resistance. Fatliquor is typically 612% on shaved weight, added in two or three portions, with a final fixation at pH 3.54.0.</p>

<h2>Stuffing and Oiling</h2>

<p>Stuffing (or hot oiling) is used mainly for heavy leathers sole, belting, harness, and some upholstery. The leather is treated with hot fat liquors, often based on fish oil, tallow, or synthetic oils, to increase flexibility and water resistance. In African tanneries, stuffing is common in sole leather production in Sudan and Ethiopia. Temperature control matters: too hot and the grain burns; too cold and the fat does not penetrate.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fff0f0; padding:12px; margin:16px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING HOT FAT AND OIL HANDLING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#2b3a1f;">Stuffing drums operate at 6080C with hot fat liquors. Scalding and burn injuries are among the most common serious accidents in African tanneries. Always wear heat-resistant gloves, face shield, and apron when opening drums or adding hot oil. Never add water to hot oil it causes violent spitting. Ensure drums have functioning emergency stops and that the operator is never alone during stuffing. Provide a burn kit and trained first-aider on site. In many African jurisdictions, this is a legal requirement under occupational health and safety regulations.</p>
</div>

<h2>Drying Techniques</h2>

<p>Drying determines the final feel, area yield, and grain appearance. Common methods in Africa:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vacuum drying:</strong> Fast, gives flat, firm leather. Common in South Africa and Egypt. High capital cost.</li>
<li><strong>Hang drying:</strong> Low cost, widely used in Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kenya. Slow, risk of case hardening and uneven moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Toggle drying:</strong> Leather is clipped to frames and dried under tension. Gives excellent area yield and flatness. Used for high-value upholstery and footwear.</li>
<li><strong>Paste drying:</strong> Leather pasted onto glass or steel plates. Gives very flat, glossy grain. Used for high-end calf and sheepskin.</li>
</ul>

<p>Target final moisture before conditioning is 1418%. Over-drying causes cracking and poor fat distribution; under-drying causes mould during storage a serious problem in humid African coastal cities like Mombasa, Lagos, and Durban.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Samming:</strong> Check moisture is 5060% before shaving. Measure with a moisture meter, not by feel.</li>
<li><strong>Shaving:</strong> Verify gauge with a thickness gauge every 20 hides. Record blade changes.</li>
<li><strong>Neutralization:</strong> Measure pH with a calibrated meter. Target 5.05.5. Never rely on litmus paper alone.</li>
<li><strong>Retanning:</strong> Split additions. Check penetration by cutting a cross-section and inspecting the dye/retan line.</li>
<li><strong>Dyeing:</strong> Control float ratio, temperature, and addition rate. Poor levelness is usually a neutralization problem, not a dye problem.</li>
<li><strong>Fatliquoring:</strong> Use 612% on shaved weight, two-stage addition, fix at pH 3.54.0. Check softness after 24 hours, not immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Drying:</strong> Target 1418% moisture. Avoid case hardening. Store dried leather in a dry, ventilated area.</li>
<li><strong>Cost control:</strong> Record chemical, water, and energy use per square foot every batch. Review weekly.</li>
</ol>
