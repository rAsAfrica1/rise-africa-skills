1. Principles of Wheat Cleaning

Wheat arrives at the mill with more than just grain. It carries stones, sand, straw, chaff, weed seeds, broken kernels, and sometimes metal or glass. If these impurities go into the mill, they damage equipment, wear out rolls, and end up in your flour. Cleaning is not optionalit is the first step to safe, quality flour. In Africa, wheat is often harvested by hand or small combines, so it may contain more foreign matter than wheat from large farms. Your job is to remove everything that is not wheat.

The cleaning process uses differences in size, shape, density, and magnetic properties. Screens separate by size: small holes let sand fall through, large holes let bigger trash stay on top. Aspirators use air to lift light materials like chaff and dust. Destoners use a vibrating deck and air to separate heavy stones from wheat. Magnetic separators catch iron pieces like nails or wire. Each machine targets a specific impurity.

Start by taking a sample of incoming wheat. Weigh 1 kg and hand-pick all foreign matter. If impurities are more than 2% by weight, you need extra cleaning steps. Common weeds in Africa include wild oats, corn cockle, and ryegrass. These are similar in size to wheat, so they need specific screens or gravity separators. Also check for moisture: if wheat is above 14% moisture, it may clog screens. Dry it to below 13% before cleaning.

A typical cleaning flow in a small African mill: receiving pit coarse screen (removes straw, sticks) aspirator (removes dust, chaff) destoner (removes stones) magnetic separator (removes metal) fine screen (removes small seeds) to tempering. You can combine machines, but never skip the destonerstones break millstones and roller mills. Inspect screens daily for holes or wear. Replace torn screens immediately.

Keep records: how much wheat you cleaned, how much waste removed, and what types of impurities. This helps you adjust settings and train staff. Clean wheat means better flour yield, less downtime, and happier customers. Remember: cleaning protects your mill and your reputation.

2. Cleaning Equipment: Separators, Aspirators, and Destoners

To clean wheat well, you need the right machines. This lesson covers three essential types: separators, aspirators, and destoners. They work together to remove different impurities. You can buy them new or build simple versions locally. Many African mills start with a rotary screen separator, a simple aspirator, and a gravity destoner.

Separators use screens to sort by size. A common type is the rotary drum separator: a rotating cylinder with holes. Wheat falls through holes; larger trash stays inside and exits at the end. For small seeds like wild oats, use a screen with holes slightly smaller than wheatwheat stays on top, seeds fall through. In Africa, you can make a vibrating screen with a motor and eccentric weight. Set the screen at a 1015 degree slope. Feed wheat at a steady rateabout 1 ton per hour per meter of screen width. Shake vigorously to keep holes clear.

Aspirators use air to lift light materials. A simple aspirator is a vertical pipe with an upward air stream. Wheat falls down; chaff, dust, and light seeds are blown up and out. Adjust air speed: too slow and trash stays; too fast and good wheat is blown away. Test by catching output: you want trash up, wheat down. For a 1-ton/hour flow, use a fan moving about 1015 cubic meters of air per minute. In dusty African conditions, clean the air filter daily.

Destoners separate by density. A destoner is a vibrating deck with a porous surface. Air blows up through the deck. Heavy stones stay on the deck and move uphill; lighter wheat floats and moves downhill. Set the deck angle at 510 degrees and vibration speed at 300500 strokes per minute. Feed wheat evenly. If stones carry over with wheat, increase air flow or reduce feed rate. If wheat goes with stones, reduce air flow or increase deck angle. Check the stone discharge every hourstones should be dry and free of wheat.

Maintain equipment: grease bearings weekly, check belts and screens daily, and keep motors dry. In humid African climates, rust is a problempaint steel parts and store spare screens indoors. Train operators to listen for unusual noises and stop the machine if something breaks. A well-maintained cleaner saves money and produces better flour.

3. Tempering and Conditioning Methods

Tempering, also called conditioning, means adding water to wheat and letting it rest before milling. This makes the bran tough and the endosperm soft, so they separate easily during milling. Without tempering, bran shatters into flour, giving dark color and poor baking quality. In Africa, where wheat may be dry and hard, tempering is critical for good flour.

First, know your wheat. Hard wheat (like most bread wheat) needs more water and longer rest than soft wheat. Target moisture: hard wheat 15.516.5%, soft wheat 14.515.5%. If wheat starts at 10% moisture, you need to add about 56% water. For 100 kg wheat, add 56 liters of clean water. Use a sprayer or mixing screw to wet wheat evenly. Let it rest in a bin for 1224 hours for hard wheat, 612 hours for soft wheat. In hot African climates, rest time may be shortercheck moisture after 6 hours.

There are two main methods: cold tempering and warm tempering. Cold tempering uses room-temperature water. Warm tempering heats water to 4050C; this speeds water penetration, so rest time drops to 48 hours. Warm tempering is useful in cool highland areas like Ethiopia or Kenya. But never exceed 50Ctoo hot and you damage gluten. For small mills, cold tempering is simpler and safer.

Add water in stages if wheat is very dry. First add half the water, mix, rest 2 hours, then add the rest. This prevents surface wetness. Use a moisture meter to check after rest. If moisture is low, add more water and rest another 2 hours. If too wet, mix with dry wheat or let it dry in thin layers.

Conditioning also includes adding heat or steam in large mills, but for African small mills, water alone works. After tempering, wheat should be cool and dry to touch. Mill immediatelydo not let tempered wheat sit for days, or it may mold. Record water added, rest time, and final moisture. Adjust next batch based on flour quality. Good tempering gives higher yield, whiter flour, and less energy use in milling.

4. Moisture Measurement and Control

Moisture is the most important number in wheat cleaning and conditioning. Too dry, and bran shatters, flour is dark, and milling is hard. Too wet, and wheat sticks to rollers, molds, and yields poor flour. You need to measure moisture accurately and control it. In Africa, where humidity varies from desert to rainforest, you must check moisture daily.

Use a moisture meter. The best is an electronic meter with a probe that you push into a wheat sample. They cost $100$300. Cheaper options: a drying oven (weigh 10 g wheat, dry at 130C for 1 hour, reweighloss is moisture). Or a simple salt test: mix 10 g wheat with 20 g dry salt, shake, and if salt clumps, moisture is above 14%. For accuracy, calibrate your meter monthly with a known sample.

Take samples from different parts of the bintop, middle, bottom. Moisture varies. Test at least 3 samples and average. Before cleaning, test incoming wheat. If above 14%, dry it first. Spread wheat in thin layers (5 cm) in sun for 24 hours, stir every 30 minutes. In humid areas, use a mechanical dryer with low heat (4050C). Never dry above 60Cit damages gluten.

During tempering, calculate water to add: (Target moisture Current moisture) Weight of wheat / (100 Target moisture). Example: 100 kg wheat at 12% moisture, target 16%. Water = (16-12) 100 / (100-16) = 4 100 / 84 = 4.76 liters. Add this amount. After rest, measure again. If moisture is 15% instead of 16%, add more water: (16-15) 100 / (100-16) = 1.19 liters. Mix and rest 2 hours.

Control moisture in the mill: keep bags sealed, store wheat in dry place, and avoid milling in rain. In coastal Africa, use dehumidifiers or air conditioners in storage. Train workers to record moisture every batch. A simple notebook works. If moisture is off by 1%, flour yield drops by 23%. So measure, adjust, and measure again. Good moisture control means consistent flour and happy bakers.

5. Impact of Conditioning on Milling Performance

Conditioning directly affects how well your mill runs and how much flour you get. Properly conditioned wheat mills faster, uses less power, and gives higher yield of clean flour. Poor conditioning causes roller wear, clogged screens, and dark flour. In Africa, where energy costs are high, good conditioning saves money.

When wheat is tempered right, bran becomes leathery and endosperm becomes soft. The roller mill breaks the wheat cleanly: bran stays in large flakes, endosperm crushes into flour. You get less bran powder in flour, so flour is whiter and has better baking quality. If wheat is too dry, bran shatters into tiny pieces that mix with flouryour flour ash content rises. Ash is a measure of bran contamination; target below 0.6% for white flour. If too wet, wheat sticks to rollers, causing uneven grinding and blockages.

Conditioning also affects sifting. Dry bran sifts easily; wet bran clogs screens. In a typical African mill, a 1% increase in wheat moisture (within target range) can increase flour yield by 12%. But over 17% moisture, yield drops because wheat gums up. Power consumption: milling dry wheat uses more energy because it is harder. Proper conditioning can reduce energy use by 1015%.

Test your conditioning: after tempering, take 100 g wheat and mill it in a small laboratory mill. Sift and weigh flour. Calculate yield: flour weight / wheat weight 100. Good yield for hard wheat is 7275%. Also check bran: it should come off in large pieces, not powder. If bran is powdery, wheat was too dryadd more water and rest longer. If bran is sticky and clogs, too wetreduce water or rest time.

Adjust conditioning for different wheat types. Hard wheat needs more water and longer rest. Soft wheat needs less. In hot climates, rest time is shorter because water absorbs faster. Keep records: wheat type, moisture before and after, rest time, mill settings, yield, and flour ash. After a few batches, you will know the best recipe for your wheat. Good conditioning is the difference between a profitable mill and a struggling one.

6. Troubleshooting Cleaning and Conditioning Issues

Even with good equipment and methods, problems happen. This lesson helps you diagnose and fix common issues in wheat cleaning and conditioning. Use a systematic approach: check wheat first, then machines, then settings. Keep a logbook of problems and solutions.

Problem: Stones in cleaned wheat. Cause: destoner not working. Check: air flow (increase if low), deck angle (adjust), feed rate (reduce if too high), screen holes (clean if blocked). In Africa, dusty air clogs destoner screensclean daily. If stones still pass, install a second destoner or a gravity separator.

Problem: Metal pieces in wheat. Cause: magnetic separator weak or missing. Check: magnet strength (test with a nail), clean metal particles off magnet daily. If magnet is weak, replace it. Also install a metal detector before the mill.

Problem: Wheat too wet after tempering. Cause: too much water added or short rest. Check: moisture meter calibration. If meter is wrong, you add wrong water. Recalibrate. If water was correct, extend rest time or add dry wheat to blend. Never mill wet wheatit clogs rollers.

Problem: Wheat too dry after tempering. Cause: not enough water or long storage. Check: initial moisture (maybe lower than thought). Add more water, mix, rest 2 hours. If in dry climate, cover bins to prevent evaporation.

Problem: Flour dark with bran specks. Cause: poor conditioning (bran shattered) or worn rollers. Check: wheat moisture (should be 15.516.5% for hard wheat). If low, add water and rest. Check roller gapsif too tight, bran breaks. Adjust to recommended setting.

Problem: Mill clogging. Cause: wheat too wet or screens torn. Check: moisture (if >17%, dry wheat). Check screens for holes or sticky buildupclean or replace. Reduce feed rate if motor overloads.

Problem: Low flour yield. Cause: many factorspoor cleaning (stones damage rolls), wrong moisture, worn rolls. Check each step. Start with cleaning: if impurities >2%, improve cleaning. Then check moisture. Then check roll gaps and sharpness. Keep records to spot trends. In Africa, power fluctuations can affect motorsuse stabilizers. Train staff to report problems early. Fix small issues before they become big breakdowns.

<h2>Practical Reference: Wheat Cleaning and Conditioning</h2>

<p>This practical reference is written for mill managers, shift supervisors, and quality officers running small to medium flour mills across Africa from the Rift Valley wheat belts of Kenya and Tanzania to the irrigated schemes of Sudan, Ethiopia, and South Africa's Free State. It assumes you already understand the principles; here we focus on what to check, what to measure, and what to do when things go wrong. Every hour of downtime or every point of extraction rate lost is money. The goal of this module is to help you protect both.</p>

<p>Wheat arriving at an African mill is rarely clean. It carries stones, sand, chaff, broken kernels, ergot, insect fragments, metal, and critically it usually arrives too dry or too variable in moisture for efficient milling. Cleaning removes what shouldn't be there; conditioning (tempering) adds water and time so the bran toughens and the endosperm softens. Get both right and you gain extraction rate, reduce ash, protect your rolls, and cut energy cost per tonne of flour. Get them wrong and you lose yield, damage equipment, and risk rejected deliveries.</p>

<h3>Moisture Measurement and Control</h3>

<p>You cannot manage what you don't measure. In African mills, the most common cause of inconsistent milling is variable incoming moisture combined with a "one-size-fits-all" tempering routine. Invest in at least one reliable moisture meter (capacitance or NIR) and calibrate it weekly against oven-dry reference samples. If you cannot afford an NIR unit, a calibrated capacitance meter plus a simple drying oven for verification is enough.</p>

<p>Measure moisture at three points: (1) at intake, per truck or per lot; (2) after tempering, before first break; (3) on finished flour. Record everything in a logbook or spreadsheet. The water addition calculation is straightforward:</p>

<p style="background:#f5f5f5; padding:10px; border-left:4px solid #2e7d32; font-family:monospace;">Water to add (L/tonne) = [(Target % Current %) (100 Target %)] 1000</p>

<p>Example: wheat at 11% moisture, target 16% for hard wheat. Water = [(16 11) (100 16)] 1000 = (5 84) 1000 = <strong>59.5 litres per tonne</strong>. Add this via a calibrated flow meter or dosing pump never estimate by eye. In hot, dry African conditions, evaporation losses during tempering can be 0.51.0%, so slightly over-target and verify before milling.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Dust Explosion and Confined Space Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">Wheat dust is combustible. In enclosed cleaning and tempering areas, dust concentrations can reach explosive levels. <strong>Never</strong> use compressed air to blow down equipment in enclosed spaces; use approved vacuum systems or wet methods. Ensure all bucket elevators, bins, and ducts are bonded and earthed to prevent static ignition. Tempering bins and silos are <strong>confined spaces</strong> entry requires a permit, atmospheric testing (oxygen, CO, HS), a standby observer, and a rescue plan. In many African mills, bin entry has caused fatal engulfment and asphyxiation. If a bin bridges or blocks, use external vibrators or rods from outside <strong>do not enter</strong> without full confined-space protocol. Follow your national occupational safety regulations (e.g., Kenya OSHA 2007, South Africa OHS Act 85 of 1993).</p>

<h3>Impact of Conditioning on Milling Performance</h3>

<p>Conditioning is where the miller's skill directly converts to money. Properly tempered wheat gives:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Higher extraction rate:</strong> 13% more flour per tonne of wheat. On a 100 t/day mill, that's 13 tonnes of extra flour daily.</li>
<li><strong>Lower ash:</strong> Cleaner separation of bran from endosperm means better flour grade and higher selling price.</li>
<li><strong>Lower energy cost:</strong> Properly conditioned wheat requires less grinding energy softer endosperm, tougher bran.</li>
<li><strong>Longer roll life:</strong> Less abrasive bran and fewer stones mean less wear on roll chills.</li>
<li><strong>Consistent flour quality:</strong> Stable moisture means stable baking performance critical for bakery customers who demand repeatability.</li>
</ul>

<p>In the African context, where wheat is often imported or sourced from variable local harvests, blending is your most powerful tool. Blend high-moisture and low-moisture lots to a target before tempering. This reduces the range of adjustment needed and stabilises the mill. Keep a simple blending calculator in your quality office even a spreadsheet is enough.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Test moisture at intake</strong> for every truck or lot. Record it. Never assume.</li>
<li><strong>Clean screens daily</strong> check for holes, wear, and blockages. Replace worn screens immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Verify destoner airflow</strong> at the start of each shift using the manufacturer's gauge or a simple pitot check.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate water addition</strong> using the formula never estimate. Use a calibrated flow meter.</li>
<li><strong>Respect rest time:</strong> 1624 h for hard wheat, 816 h for soft. Set timers; don't guess.</li>
<li><strong>Measure moisture again</strong> after tempering and before first break. Adjust if outside target by more than 0.3%.</li>
<li><strong>Log everything</strong> intake moisture, water added, rest time, post-temper moisture, extraction rate. Review weekly for trends.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect magnets and metal detectors</strong> every shift. A single metal fragment can destroy a roll and cost thousands in downtime.</li>
</ol>
