7. Harvest, Processing and Selling

You have raised the snails. Now you must turn them into income. This module covers how to harvest without losing future stock, how to process and package, who buys, how much to charge, and how to build a customer base that pays premium prices.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 7.1 — Harvest timing

Harvest timing determines both price and quality. Too early and the snails are small; too late and they are tough and past prime.

StageAgeWeightBest for
Juvenile6–9 months40–70 gGrowing out more — do not harvest
Young adult10–12 months80–110 gBasic market
Prime13–15 months120–160 gRestaurants, premium market
Mature16–18 months160–220 gTop price — giant species can go higher
Oversize20+ months220+ gTough meat, lower price. Avoid.

Harvest rules:

  • Harvest only the largest 50% of the pen. Keep the rest for breeding.
  • Harvest in the cool of the morning. Snails are less stressed.
  • Do not harvest a pen you need for breeding. Dedicate separate pens to breeding and to harvest.
  • Do not harvest during the rainy season if you can avoid it. Snails eat and grow fastest then. Harvest in the dry season when prices are highest.
The 50% harvest rule: Every harvest, take only 50% of the ready snails. The other 50% either continue growing or become breeding stock. This single rule keeps your farm productive for decades — the snails you do not harvest today produce 100× their number next year.
Practical exercise 7.1: Weigh 50 adult snails. Rank by size. Mark the top 25 for harvest, the bottom 25 for future breeding. Log both groups.

Lesson 7.2 — Harvesting and cleaning

Harvesting snails for market:

  1. Select snails. Choose the largest, healthiest.
  2. Starve for 2 days. Place snails in a clean container with no food. This purges the digestive tract — much better taste.
  3. Rinse in clean water. Remove soil and slime.
  4. Blanch in boiling water for 2–3 minutes. This loosens the meat from the shell.
  5. Extract the meat. Use a small fork or hook to pull the meat out of the shell.
  6. Remove the intestinal tract. Cut open the meat and remove the dark digestive organs.
  7. Rinse and package. Rinse clean meat again. Package for sale.

For live sales (restaurants prefer live snails):

  1. Starve 2 days.
  2. Rinse the shells thoroughly.
  3. Dry slightly, then pack in breathable containers.
  4. Add a small amount of fresh leaves for transport.
  5. Deliver within 24 hours.
Do not harvest snails for the meat market unless you can process immediately. Snail meat spoils quickly — within 4–6 hours at room temperature. If you cannot process within 6 hours, sell live instead.
Practical exercise 7.2: Process 10 snails for meat. Time the entire process from selection to packaged meat. Note the yield per snail (usually 40–50% of live weight). Adjust your pricing accordingly.

Lesson 7.3 — Packaging and preservation

Snail meat can be sold fresh, frozen, smoked, dried, or as live snails.

FormatShelf lifePriceBest for
Live snails3–7 days (in shade)BaseRestaurants, local market
Fresh meat (refrigerated)2–3 days+10–20%Local restaurants
Frozen meat3–6 months+20–30%Export, diaspora
Smoked meat2–4 weeks+30–40%Local market, non-refrigerated
Dried meat3–6 months+20–30%Export, non-refrigerated
Processed (in sauce, canned)6–12 months+40–60%Retail, export

Packaging materials:

  • Live: Woven baskets, mesh bags, cardboard boxes with holes.
  • Fresh meat: Plastic containers, vacuum bags, cling film.
  • Frozen: Vacuum bags, sealed freezer containers.
  • Smoked/dried: Sealed plastic bags, vacuum bags, glass jars.

Labelling (for any non-live product):

  • Product name (e.g., "Fresh Snail Meat")
  • Net weight
  • Date of processing
  • Best before date
  • Your business name and contact
  • Storage instructions
Practical exercise 7.3: Package 10 live snails for a restaurant test. Package 2 kg of meat for the freezer. Label both. Take photos for marketing.

Lesson 7.4 — Finding buyers and pricing

BuyerVolumePrice per kg (typical)
Local market traderWeekly, 5–20 kg$6–$12
Restaurant (specialty)Weekly, 5–30 kg$10–$20
Hotel (fine dining)Weekly, 10–40 kg$12–$25
Diaspora shop (local)Weekly, 20–100 kg$15–$25
Exporter (UK, US, EU)Monthly, 100–500 kg$20–$40

How to find buyers:

  • Restaurants. Visit personally. Bring samples. Offer to supply small quantities for a trial month.
  • Hotels. Talk to the food and beverage manager. They often source from multiple suppliers.
  • Diaspora shops. WhatsApp groups, Facebook communities, ethnic groceries in major cities.
  • Exporters. Search for African food exporters in your region. Many are looking for reliable snail supply.
  • Online. Facebook Marketplace, Jumia, Etsy (for dried/smoked).
The restaurant rule: One restaurant relationship can be worth $500–$2,000 per month. Restaurants pay premium prices and order consistently. Spend your first 6 months building 2–3 restaurant relationships before looking at exports.
Practical exercise 7.4: Visit 5 restaurants this week. Bring samples. Ask about their snail supply needs and typical price. One "yes" is enough to start.

Lesson 7.5 — Building a repeat customer base

Snail farming is a slow business — the customer base must be too. Restaurants, hotels, and exporters want reliability, not one-off sales.

Repeat customer practices:

  • Deliver on time, every time. Restaurants cannot run out of an ingredient.
  • Consistent quality. Same size, same freshness, same packaging.
  • Fast response. Answer calls and messages within 5 minutes.
  • Fair pricing. Same customer, same price, every order.
  • Flexible on quantity. Slow weeks mean smaller orders. Accept and plan.
  • Extra value. Free cleaning on live snails. Free recipe cards. Free samples of new products.

Building the reputation:

  • Get testimonials from restaurants. Ask for a small written note or permission to use their name.
  • Photograph your deliveries, your pen, your processing. Use as marketing material.
  • Be the farmer who delivers reliably. In a market where most suppliers are inconsistent, reliability is the winning edge.
  • Contact repeat customers every 2 weeks. Even if they have not ordered, check in.
Practical exercise 7.5: Set up a customer list. Every buyer, every delivery, every payment. After 6 months, track your repeat rate. Aim for 60%+.

Lesson 7.6 — Scaling the snail farm

Once your farm is stable and profitable, three growth paths are available.

PathInvestmentTime to payoffRisk
Add pens, increase stock$300–$1,50012–18 monthsLow
Add processing (dried, smoked, packaged)$200–$1,0003–6 monthsMedium
Add export agreements$500–$3,000 (compliance, cold chain)6–12 monthsMedium-high
Train and supply other farmers (spawn/hatchling sales)$0–$2001–3 monthsLow

The safest growth path:

  1. Year 1: Master snail farming with 500 snails. Focus on survival rate.
  2. Year 2: Scale to 2,000 snails. Add restaurant customers. Start processing.
  3. Year 3: Scale to 5,000+ snails. Add export or training revenue.
  4. Year 4+: Consider a training or breeding stock business alongside meat sales.
The snail farming exit: Once you have 3+ years of experience, you become valuable not just for your snails but for your knowledge. Selling breeding stock, training new farmers, or consulting pays very well and has no additional production cost.
Practical exercise 7.6 — Capstone: Write your one-page snail farming business plan. Current stock, next 12-month target, growth path, expected revenue, and one specific action for each of the next 4 quarters. Post it near your pen. Review quarterly.
