Lesson 1

Why Sweet Potato Is Africa's Underrated Cash Crop

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Explain why sweet potato produces more food per hectare per day than most cereals
  • Understand the 3-4 month cycle as a commercial advantage
  • Recognise why it is sold as both food and fodder

Sweet potato produces more calories, more vitamin A and more biomass per hectare per day than maize or cassava. It finishes in 3 to 4 months. It grows on soils too poor for maize. And unlike almost any other root crop, it has a second commercial product — the vines — which sell as livestock fodder. There is no other crop in Africa with this combination.

The reason sweet potato has historically been treated as a subsistence crop is not agronomic. It is market. Farmers grew it for household consumption because there was no reliable buyer. That has changed in the last 15 years: the orange-fleshed sweet potato (OFSP) has been promoted for vitamin A, and urban markets now pay real money for it. The market exists. The supply does not.

Lesson 2

White vs Orange-Fleshed — the Vitamin A Market

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand the difference between white and orange-fleshed sweet potato
  • Know why the orange varieties sell for a premium
  • Identify the institutional buyers for OFSP

White-fleshed sweet potato is the traditional African crop. It is high in starch, low in sugar, and stores well. It is the preferred type for many household uses and for processing into flour. Prices are moderate and the market is stable.

Orange-fleshed sweet potato (OFSP) is rich in beta-carotene — the precursor to vitamin A. It is sweeter, softer and does not store as long as white. But it commands a 20–60% price premium in urban markets, and a much larger premium from institutional buyers — health programs, NGOs, schools, and food-security projects that use OFSP to combat vitamin A deficiency.

Vitamin A deficiency remains a serious public-health problem in many African countries. OFSP is a proven, low-cost vehicle for delivering it. This has created a partially subsidised market: government programs and NGOs buy OFSP vines, roots and processed products at guaranteed prices to distribute them through schools, clinics and community programs.

Lesson 3

Varieties by Use — Table, Processing, Fodder

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish table, processing, and dual-purpose varieties
  • Choose varieties for your market, not for a catalogue
  • Understand the trade-off between root yield and vine yield

Table varieties

Chosen for appearance, taste, texture and shape. Sweet, smooth skin, uniform shape. Common in Africa: Ejumula, NASPOT series, Kabode, Vita (OFSP). These are the fresh-market varieties.

Processing varieties

Chosen for dry matter content — high dry matter is needed for good flour, chips, or crisps. The best varieties are not always the sweetest. Local white varieties are often the best for processing into flour because of their texture and starch content.

Fodder varieties

Some varieties are grown mainly for their vines, which are high-protein livestock feed. Root yield is lower but vine yield is high. Fodder varieties are useful in a mixed livestock farm where the vines are worth more than the roots.

Dual-purpose varieties

Most improved OFSP varieties are dual-purpose: acceptable root yield and good vine production. For most smallholders, dual-purpose is the right choice — you get roots for market and vines for fodder, and if one market fails you still have the other.

The root-vine trade-off

There is a real trade-off. Heavy vine harvesting reduces root yield. If you are growing for the fresh market, harvest vines sparingly and only at the end of the season. If you are growing for fodder, accept a lower root yield. Decide before you plant.

Lesson 4

Yield Reality — What You Can Realistically Grow

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Know the yield range for African conditions
  • Identify the four factors that close the gap
  • Set a realistic first-season target

African average sweet potato yield sits at 5–8 t/ha. Achievable yield with improved varieties, quality vines, basic fertility, and timely weeding is 15–30 t/ha. Under irrigation and good management, yields above 40 t/ha are recorded.

Four factors that close the gap

  1. Clean vines. Virus-infected vines produce 50–80% less yield. This is the single biggest factor. Buy vines only from certified clean-vine producers.
  2. Ridges or mounds. Sweet potato roots need loose, deep soil to swell properly. Flat planting produces smaller, more misshapen roots.
  3. Variety. Improved varieties available through national research programs yield 2–3x local landraces.
  4. Weeding. Two weedings in the first 6 weeks. After 6 weeks the vine canopy suppresses most weeds.

Lesson 5

The Three Markets for Sweet Potato

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Name the three main markets for sweet potato
  • Match variety and grade to the correct buyer
  • Understand the fodder market as a second income stream

Market 1 — Fresh table market

Households, market vendors, supermarkets and roadside stalls. Buy on appearance: uniform, smooth, medium-sized. Prices range from $0.20–0.60/kg depending on season and region. This is the most common market and the easiest to sell into.

Market 2 — Processing market

Flour, chips, crisps, puree. Buyers include food processors and the growing snack industry. Prices are 20–40% above fresh market for good-quality processing-grade roots, but require volume and consistency. Some buyers prefer larger or irregular roots (cheaper) rather than premium table grade.

Market 3 — Institutional / vitamin A market

The OFSP market with the highest prices. NGOs, government health and nutrition programs, and school feeding schemes buy OFSP roots and vines at guaranteed prices. Payment can be slow (30–90 days) but the price premium is significant. Contact your district nutrition office or an NGO active in the area for the current OFSP purchase program.

Market 4 — Fodder (vine) market

Livestock feed buyers — dairy farmers, piggeries, poultry — buy sweet potato vines as protein-rich fodder. Price $20–60 per tonne fresh. This is often the highest-margin part of the crop when fodder demand is strong. Many sweet potato farmers overlook this market entirely.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. How long does a sweet potato cycle take?

Correct: 90–120 days. Fast cycle is one of sweet potato's biggest commercial advantages.

2. Why does orange-fleshed sweet potato command a higher price?

Correct: beta-carotene. Health programs, NGOs and urban consumers pay a premium for it.

3. What is the biggest factor cutting African sweet potato yields?

Correct: virus-infected vines. Cause 50–80% yield loss. Buy clean vines only.

4. What is the second commercial product from sweet potato?

Correct: vines as fodder. $20–60/t fresh, and often the highest-margin part of the crop.

5. Which market pays the highest premium for OFSP?

Correct: NGO / government nutrition programs. 50–100% premium but slow payment terms.
