3. The Home Laboratory Setup

You do not need a million-dollar lab. You need a clean, small, controlled space — and the right technique. This module shows you how to build a working spawn lab for as little as $200, and how to run it like a professional.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Room design — airflow, surfaces, contamination control

Every contamination starts somewhere. 90% of the time, it starts in the lab room. If the room is dirty, cluttered, or draughty, no amount of sterile technique will save you.

A good lab room has six characteristics:

  • Small and dedicated. 2×3 m is plenty. The smaller the room, the easier to control. Never a room that also serves as a kitchen, laundry, or bedroom.
  • Sealed. No draughts from windows, doors, or ceiling gaps. Air movement carries contamination. If you can feel a breeze, seal it.
  • Smooth surfaces. Floors, walls, ceiling, and table should all be non-porous and easy to wipe down. Vinyl flooring, tile, or epoxy paint are ideal. Bare wood and raw cement harbour spores.
  • Washable. Everything in the room must be cleanable with alcohol or diluted bleach. No fabric curtains, no carpets.
  • Controlled access. Nobody walks through your lab except you. No children, no pets, no visitors.
  • No draught-generating equipment. If you have a fan or air conditioner, it should be off during work.

The three enemies of sterile work: airborne spores, contact contamination, and your own skin/hair/clothing. Your room design should reduce all three before you even start.

Rule: If you would not eat off the floor, you cannot grow mushrooms in that room. Sterile means sterile — not "pretty clean".
Practical exercise 3.1: Identify your lab room. Measure it. List every source of draught, dust, or contamination. Plan how you will seal, clean, and dedicate it. If you do not have a dedicated room, plan to build a temporary one from PVC sheeting in a corner.

Lesson 3.2 — Laminar flow vs still-air box — the budget option

For sterile transfers (moving mycelium from one container to another), you need either a laminar flow hood or a still-air box. Here is the difference.

FeatureLaminar flow hoodStill-air box
Cost$500–$3,000$20–$80
How it worksBlows filtered air across the work areaEnclosed box that traps air — you work inside
Contamination rateVery low (~1%)Low (~5–10% with good technique)
ComfortComfortable — you see through a glass shieldCramped — you work through arm holes
Skill requiredBasicBasic, but technique matters more
Best forCommercial production, 50+ transfers/dayStartup phase, 5–20 transfers/session

For a start-up, build a still-air box. It costs almost nothing and gets you to production. You can upgrade to a laminar flow hood when revenue justifies it.

How to build a still-air box:

  1. Get a large clear plastic storage bin (60–100 litres) with a lid.
  2. Cut two circular holes in the side facing you — big enough for your hands and forearms (10 cm diameter).
  3. Cut a rectangular viewing window in the lid, cover with plastic or glass.
  4. Seal all edges with silicone or tape. The box must be airtight except through the arm holes.
  5. Before use, wipe the inside with 70% alcohol and let it sit 10 minutes. Then work inside with gloved hands.
Why still-air works: Spores and bacteria settle downward. Inside a sealed box, the air is still — particles fall onto the bottom, not onto your work. Combined with an alcohol flame near your hands, you achieve 90–95% clean transfers without a $2,000 hood.
Practical exercise 3.2: Build your still-air box this week. Total materials cost under $80. Test it by leaving an open agar plate inside for 10 minutes, then incubating for 3 days. If fewer than 2 colonies grow, your box works.

Lesson 3.3 — Pressure cooker as autoclave — the 15 PSI method

You must sterilise your grain, agar, and tools before every use. A pressure cooker is the cheapest way to achieve the temperatures required (121°C). Regular boiling at 100°C is NOT enough for grain.

What a pressure cooker does: Under 15 PSI (pounds per square inch) of pressure, water boils at 121°C instead of 100°C. That extra 21°C is what kills bacterial endospores and fungal spores, which survive boiling.

Required times:

MaterialPressureTime
Agar plates or slants (small jars)15 PSI20 minutes
Grain jars (500 ml)15 PSI45–60 minutes
Grain bags (2 kg)15 PSI90–120 minutes
Substrate bags for fruiting (larger)15 PSI2–3 hours
Metal tools (scalpel, forceps)15 PSI15 minutes OR flame-sterilise

Buy the largest pressure cooker you can afford (10–20 litres). Smaller cookers force you to run more batches, which wastes fuel and time. A good quality 15-litre cooker costs $60–$150 and lasts for years.

Do not shortcut sterilisation times. Undercooked grain jars look fine, but as soon as you inoculate them, contamination explodes. Do it right the first time or you will lose the entire batch and a week of production.
Practical exercise 3.3: Buy or borrow a pressure cooker. Test it: run it with water at 15 PSI for 20 minutes. Note the time it takes to reach pressure on your heat source. This is your cycle time from now on.

Lesson 3.4 — Alcohol lamps, gloves, and sterile technique

Technique is what saves you when equipment is minimal. Here are the rules professionals follow.

Before any transfer:

  • Shower or wash thoroughly. No perfumes, no lotions.
  • Wear clean clothes. Ideally a lab coat or a clean long-sleeved shirt.
  • Tie long hair back or cover with a cap.
  • Put on nitrile or latex gloves. Wipe with 70% alcohol.
  • Wipe down the still-air box interior and all surfaces with 70% alcohol.
  • Set up an alcohol lamp (or a small spirit burner) inside the box.

During transfers:

  • Work quickly but deliberately. Slow, sweeping movements stir up air and contaminate.
  • Keep your hands and tools between you and the flame. The rising heat creates a small sterile zone.
  • Flame-sterilise any metal tool (scalpel, forceps, needle) until red-hot, then let it cool for 5 seconds before touching culture.
  • Never speak, cough, or breathe directly onto the work. Wear a mask if possible.
  • Open culture containers only for the minimum time. Every second open is a second of exposure.

After transfers:

  • Seal everything immediately.
  • Clean the still-air box again with alcohol.
  • Log what you transferred, when, and to what. Date each container.
Move slowly. The single biggest beginner mistake is rushing. Fast movements create air currents that pull contamination onto your work. Slow, smooth, calm — like a surgeon, not a chef.
Practical exercise 3.4: Do a "dry run" — set up your still-air box, light the alcohol lamp, and practise opening and closing jars inside it. Do this 10 times before doing a real transfer.

Lesson 3.5 — Storage for cultures and Petri dishes

You need somewhere to keep cultures, agar plates, and spawn while they grow. This storage needs specific conditions.

WhatTemperatureLightWhere
Agar plates (incubating)22–26°CDarkIncubation box or cupboard
Agar slants (long-term)4–8°CDarkRefrigerator (dedicated for cultures)
Grain spawn (colonising)22–28°CDarkIncubation cupboard
Finished spawn (ready to sell)4–8°C or 15°CDarkRefrigerator or cool room

The incubation cupboard should be a closed, insulated box (or just a clean cupboard). You do not need a fancy incubator — a well-insulated cupboard with a thermometer works fine in most of Africa. If your room temperature is 25–30°C, that is often perfect for oyster mycelium.

For long-term strain storage, you must have a dedicated refrigerator. This should be a small one, used only for cultures — never food. Fridge temperature should be 4–8°C, checked with a thermometer every week.

Do not keep cultures in the same fridge as food. Cross-contamination from food spoilage organisms will destroy your strain library. Buy a small used fridge for this purpose only — a $50 used fridge is enough.
Practical exercise 3.5: Set up an incubation cupboard and (if you can afford it) a dedicated refrigerator. If you cannot afford a fridge yet, use a sealed box in the coolest corner of your house, and accept that long-term storage is not yet possible.

Lesson 3.6 — Budget breakdown — $200 vs $2,000 setups

Two viable labs. Pick what you can afford, and grow into the next tier.

The budget setup produces 10–20 kg of spawn per month. The pro setup produces 150–300 kg per month. Both are viable businesses — the budget setup is your starting point, the pro setup is your growth target.

You can reach the pro setup in two ways: save revenue from the budget setup for 12–24 months, or take a small loan once the budget setup is profitable. Do not borrow to buy the pro setup before the budget setup has proven it can sell everything it produces.

Buy in stages: Pressure cooker first. Then grain jars and agar. Then the still-air box. Then a fridge for cultures. Only when you are producing 20+ kg per month do you consider the laminar flow hood and autoclave.
Practical exercise 3.6: Write down your current lab inventory. Circle what you already have. Write what you still need. Set a budget for the next 3 months of purchases. Do not buy everything at once — build up over time.
