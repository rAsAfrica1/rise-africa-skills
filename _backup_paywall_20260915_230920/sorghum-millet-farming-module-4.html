Lesson 1

Quelea and Why Bird Damage Is a Social Problem

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand quelea and other grain-eating birds as a flock problem, not a farm problem
  • Quantify the real economic risk per season
  • See why individual action fails where community action works

A single flock of quelea — the small red-billed weaver bird of African savannas — can number in the millions. When it settles on a sorghum field at the milk or dough stage it strips the panicles in a morning. Losses of 30–100% of the harvest on unprotected plots are documented in the Sahel, East Africa and southern Africa.

The reason bird defence is a social problem, not an individual one, is simple: birds move. A farmer who scares birds off at 8 a.m. sends them to the neighbour's field at 9 a.m. That neighbour scares them back at 10 a.m. Nothing is achieved except exhaustion. Collective, synchronised action — the whole village at once — is the only tactic that works.

Lesson 2

The Stacked Bird Defence

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Stack four bird-control lines at once
  • Choose a tannin grain or a bird-resistant variety as the structural defence
  • Time the harvest to shrink the exposed window

Four tactics, used together, cut bird loss from 50–100% to 5–15%.

1. Synchronised planting

If one field ripens three weeks before its neighbours, it feeds the entire flock. If the whole village's sorghum ripens at the same time, the total grain available per bird per day drops, and the flock moves on more quickly. Village-level planning of the planting date is the single most powerful social tactic.

2. Tannin grain or bird-resistant variety

Brown, high-tannin sorghum is unpalatable to birds. It is also less attractive to the food market, so a mixed strategy is common: white sorghum for the food and malting market in the low-risk perimeter, brown sorghum in the centre of the village fields where birds concentrate.

3. Active scaring with labour

The honest, low-tech solution: two people per hectare through the damage window, using slings, whistles, flags, drums, metallic noise, reflective tape. Two weeks of active scaring costs a labourer's wages and saves the crop. Aerial sprays and explosives are not permitted in most countries, and community-organized scaring by national plant protection services may be available in outbreak years.

4. Timing

Harvest as soon as the grain reaches hard dough — 5–10 days earlier than you would for maximum grain weight — if quelea are present. The 3–5% loss of grain weight from early harvest is far less than the 30–100% from birds. The decision is arithmetic, not preference.

Lesson 3

Stem Borers and Head Miners

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify stem borer damage (dead heart) and millet head miner damage
  • Use cultural, biological and chemical control in the right order
  • Time any insecticide spray correctly

Stem borers (Busseola, Sesamia, Chilo)

The larva tunnels into the stem. Vegetative-stage attack produces dead heart — the central shoot dies and the plant produces no head. Flowering-stage attack produces white head — the panicle fills with empty grain.

Control in order of priority:

  1. Destroy crop residues after harvest — borer larvae overwinter in stubble
  2. Rotate with a legume — breaks the borer cycle
  3. Encourage natural enemies — Trichogramma wasps, earwigs, spiders
  4. Biological — Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) spray at 10–14 day intervals during vegetative growth, if pressure is high
  5. Chemical — only as last resort, only when scouting confirms above-threshold pressure. Spray into the leaf whorl at 3–5 weeks after emergence. Respect pre-harvest interval.

Millet head miner (Heliocheilus albipunctella)

The most important pearl millet pest in the Sahel. The larva mines the developing panicle, webbing the grain together. Damage up to 40% is recorded. The most practical control is early-maturing varieties that escape the pest's peak flight, plus hand-collection of larvae during scouting.

Lesson 4

Smuts, Ergot and Grain Moulds

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Identify covered smut, loose smut, ergot and grain moulds
  • Use seed treatment, rotation and timing to reduce losses
  • Understand the food safety issue with ergot and moulds

Smuts

Sorghum covered smut and loose smut replace the grain in the panicle with black spore masses. Infection is seed-borne or soil-borne; seed treatment with a triazole or carboxin fungicide is the standard prevention. Rotate away from sorghum for one season in infested fields.

Ergot (Claviceps)

Ergot replaces individual grains with dark, hard sclerotia. It is a food-safety issue, not just a yield loss. Ergot alkaloids cause ergotism in humans and livestock. Sorghum and pearl millet are both susceptible. Cool, wet weather at flowering raises risk.

Control: use clean seed, avoid late planting into cool wet conditions, rotate, and — critically — do not eat or sell grain with visible sclerotia. Separate and destroy sclerotia during threshing. If a large share of grain is affected, do not sell for food use.

Grain moulds (Fusarium, Curvularia, Aspergillus)

Wet weather at grain fill and harvest, plus delayed harvest, produces discoloured grain and mycotoxins — including aflatoxin from Aspergillus. The commercial risk is real: some grain buyers screen for aflatoxin and reject contaminated loads.

Control: harvest as soon as grain reaches maturity, dry to 12–13% moisture quickly, store hermetically. Do not let grain sit wet in a heap for days.

Lesson 5

Insect Storage Pests

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify the main storage pests of sorghum and millet
  • Compare chemical, hermetic and traditional storage methods
  • Choose the right storage method for your farm

Main storage pests

  • Lesser grain borer (Prostephanus truncatus) — worst pest on stored maize but also attacks sorghum; produces heavy dust
  • Rice weevil (Sitophilus oryzae) — the most common; bores into grain
  • Angoumois grain moth (Sitotroga cerealella) — larvae feed inside the grain
  • Red flour beetle (Tribolium) — secondary; attacks damaged grain and flour
  • Rats and mice — significant losses in traditional stores

Storage methods compared

  1. Traditional granary — mud or thatch store. Cheap. Vulnerable to rodents and weather. Losses 10–30% over 6 months.
  2. Hermetic bags (PICS, GrainPro) — airtight plastic liners in a woven bag. Cost ~$3–8 per 100 kg bag. Kills insects without chemicals. Losses <2% over 12 months. Best option for most smallholders who need chemical-free storage.
  3. Metal silos — $300–800 for a 500 kg silo. Excellent rodent resistance and long life. Higher capital cost.
  4. Chemical protectants (Actellic dust, etc.) — effective but with residue issues. Not compatible with organic markets. Follow label rates exactly.
  5. Cold storage — rarely economic for sorghum and millet at smallholder scale.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. Why is bird damage best fought at village level?

Correct: farmers just move birds around. Synchronised action is the only effective tactic.

2. What is a structural defence against birds?

Correct: tannin sorghum. Birds find high-tannin grain unpalatable.

3. What does "dead heart" indicate?

Correct: stem borer during vegetative stage. The central shoot dies and no head forms.

4. Why is ergot a food-safety concern?

Correct: toxic alkaloids. Do not eat or sell grain with visible sclerotia.

5. What is the cheapest defensible long-term storage method for most smallholders?

Correct: hermetic bags. $3–8 per 100 kg, kills insects without chemicals, losses under 2%.

Module 4 capstone

Prepare a season-long pest and disease plan for your sorghum and millet fields. Include: (1) whether quelea are a problem in your area and the date range of the risk; (2) your bird-scaring plan (who, when, how many people); (3) whether you will use a bird-resistant variety and why; (4) your stem borer scouting schedule; (5) your ergot and grain mould prevention plan; (6) your storage choice for the harvested grain.
