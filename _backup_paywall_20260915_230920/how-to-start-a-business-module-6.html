6. Step 6 — Operate and Improve

The first 90 days are hard. The next 12 months are what make or break you. This module is about the unglamorous work that separates a surviving business from a growing one — daily routines, records, customer service, fixing what breaks, and running a proper 3-month review.

Module 6 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 6.1 — Daily operations — routines that scale

Successful small businesses run on routines. Not the grind of 16-hour days — the discipline of doing the same four or five things every single day, so that nothing important gets forgotten.

A typical daily routine for a small business has five parts:

TimeActivityPurpose
MorningCash count and float checkKnow your starting position
MorningStock check and prepNothing missing for the day
All dayServe customers, log every saleRevenue and records
AfternoonFollow up on enquiries and ordersConvert leads to sales
EveningCash-up, record expenses, plan tomorrowReconcile and prepare

The evening routine is the one most owners skip — and it is the most important. Fifteen minutes at the end of the day to count cash, record expenses, and write three priorities for tomorrow is what turns chaos into a business.

Why routines matter more than talent: talent is inconsistent. A routine is consistent. You will have good days and bad days. The routine ensures the same standard of service on both. Over a year, this consistency is what builds a reputation.

Practical exercise 6.1: Write your daily routine. Morning, day, evening. Post it where you work. Follow it for 7 days without deviation. Adjust after a week, not before.

Lesson 6.2 — Records — sales, expenses, profits

If you cannot answer "How much did I make last month?" in 30 seconds, you do not have a business — you have a hobby. Records are what turn activity into numbers, and numbers into decisions.

You need three records, kept daily:

1 — Sales book. Every single sale. Date, what was sold, amount, payment method (cash/mobile/bank). One line per sale. Can be a paper notebook or a WhatsApp self-message log. What matters is that it is complete and daily.

2 — Expense book. Every single expense, no matter how small. Date, what was bought, amount, who was paid. A $0.30 airtime top-up is a real expense. So is the taxi to deliver goods. If you do not record it, you will not know your real profit.

3 — Monthly summary. At the end of each month, add up:

  • Total sales (revenue)
  • Total direct costs (materials, stock)
  • Total operating costs (rent, staff, transport, electricity)
  • Gross profit = Revenue − Direct costs
  • Net profit = Gross − Operating costs
MonthRevenueDirect costsOperatingNet profit
Jan$1,240$620$380$240
Feb$1,580$780$400$400
Mar$1,410$700$410$300

Once you track this for three months, you can see trends. Revenue up but net flat means costs are eating the growth. Revenue flat but net up means you have cut costs effectively. Every number tells a story.

The daily-five-minutes rule: Five minutes a day to write sales and expenses. It is the difference between knowing your profit and guessing it. Guessing businesses lose money quietly until it is too late.
Practical exercise 6.2: Open a sales book and an expense book today. Record every transaction for 30 days. At the end of the month, produce your first monthly summary. This number is your starting line.

Lesson 6.3 — Customer service — the thing that wins repeat business

In small business, getting a new customer costs 10 times more than keeping an existing one. Customer service is not soft — it is the cheapest growth strategy there is.

Four things matter more than anything else:

1 — Speed of response. In Africa's informal economy, the business that replies within 5 minutes wins 80% of the sales it competes for. Slow replies lose sales to faster competitors. If you can only promise one thing, promise speed.

2 — Consistency of quality. Every customer should get the same product, every time. A customer who buys a great loaf on Monday and a poor one on Friday stops coming. Consistency beats brilliance.

3 — Honesty about mistakes. When you get it wrong — and you will — say so first, before the customer complains. "I am sorry, I made a mistake" from the owner is worth more than a discount. Customers forgive honest mistakes. They do not forgive excuses or silence.

4 — Going slightly beyond. Small unexpected acts of service — putting the delivery inside the gate, remembering a name, following up after a purchase, throwing in an extra when you can — create loyalty far beyond what price can buy.

One bad review travels faster than ten good ones. In WhatsApp groups and community forums, a single complaint can reach hundreds of people in a day. Handle every complaint as though it will be published — because in Africa's digital economy, it may well be.
Practical exercise 6.3: For one week, time your replies to every enquiry. Aim for under 5 minutes during working hours. At the end of the week, count how many converted to sales. That number is your "speed-of-reply" conversion rate — improve it every month.

Lesson 6.4 — Fixing what breaks — fast

Things will break. Equipment fails, suppliers disappear, staff quit, customers complain, weather changes. How fast you fix these is what determines whether a small problem stays small or becomes a crisis.

The three-step fix process:

  1. Isolate — what exactly is broken? Do not panic. Spend five minutes identifying the actual issue. Is it the machine, the supplier, or a single order? "Business is bad" is not a problem statement. "I lost 3 customers this week because deliveries were late" is.
  2. Fix the minimum. What is the fastest, cheapest change that gets the business back to normal? Not the ideal fix — the necessary one. If your oven breaks, do not spend 3 days choosing a new one. Borrow a neighbour's oven and keep selling while you decide.
  3. Then do the proper fix. Once the emergency is handled, plan the real solution. New equipment, new supplier, new staff. But only after the business is safe.

Common things that break and standard fixes:

What breaksQuick fixProper fix
Key machineBorrow or rent one temporarilyBuy a backup or a newer model
Supplier failsBuy from a market seller at higher cost for nowQualify two or three backup suppliers
Staff quitsCover the work yourself for 1–2 weeksHire carefully, train a backup
Cash crunchPush sales, delay non-essential paymentsBuild a 2-month reserve over time
Customer complaintAcknowledge, replace or refund immediatelyFix the process that caused it
Practical exercise 6.4: Think about the 3 most likely things to break in your business in the next 6 months. Write down the quick fix and the proper fix for each. Keep this list. When something breaks, use it — do not improvise under pressure.

Lesson 6.5 — The 3-month review — what is working, what is not

Every 90 days, step back and review. Not the daily numbers — the big picture. The review is what keeps a business from drifting into failure while the owner is too busy to notice.

Ask six questions, in writing:

  1. Revenue trend. Is revenue up, flat, or down compared to 90 days ago? By how much?
  2. Profit trend. Is net profit up, flat, or down? The same percentage as revenue, or different? If different, why?
  3. Customer trend. Are you gaining, keeping, or losing customers? What is your repeat rate?
  4. What is working? Which products, days, channels, or customer types are most profitable? Double down on these.
  5. What is not working? Which products, days, channels, or customers lose money? Cut these.
  6. What is one thing to change? Not ten things. One. The single change with the biggest impact for the next 90 days.

Most businesses drift because the owner never stops to ask these questions. They run on autopilot for two years and then discover the market has moved and they have not.

The one-change rule: Every 90 days, change one thing. Not ten. One. This is how improvement compounds without overwhelming you. Twelve months of one good change per quarter is a completely different business.
Practical exercise 6.5: Set a calendar reminder for 90 days from today. Do the six-question review on paper. Pick your one change. Write it down and start it on day 91.

Lesson 6.6 — Raising prices without losing clients

At some point, you will need to raise prices. Inputs have gone up, your costs have risen, or you have improved your product. The fear is that customers will leave. Some will. Most will not — if you do it right.

Four rules for raising prices safely:

1 — Raise by 5–15%, not 50%. Small, regular increases every 6–12 months are invisible to most customers. Big sudden jumps get noticed and trigger switching.

2 — Give notice. Tell your best customers a month in advance. "From 1 March, prices go up by 10%. Nothing changes about the product or service." This gives them time to accept the change and shows respect.

3 — Bundle a small improvement. If you are raising prices, add something. A new flavour, faster delivery, better packaging, a loyalty stamp. Customers accept a price increase much better when they see what they are getting for it.

4 — Hold the line for regulars. If you have been selling to someone weekly for a year, offer them the old price for one more month, or a 5% loyalty discount on the new price. Loyalty discount is a marketing asset — it keeps them and makes them feel valued.

Old priceNew priceIncreaseCustomer reaction (typical)
$1.00$1.1010%Most stay, ~5% leave
$1.00$1.2020%~15% leave, must add value
$1.00$1.5050%~40% leave unless product is upgraded
Never apologise for raising prices. Say "our costs have increased and we are adjusting our prices" once, calmly. Do not over-explain or apologise repeatedly. Confidence is contagious — if you believe the price is fair, customers will accept it.
Practical exercise 6.6: Review your current prices against your current costs. If your margin has dropped below target, plan your next price increase. Announce it 30 days ahead. Include one small improvement. Track how many customers stay.
