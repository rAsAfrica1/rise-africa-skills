Lesson 1

Knowing When to Harvest — Hard Dough and the Quelea Trade-off

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Judge the correct maturity stage for harvest
  • Decide between early harvest and maximum grain weight
  • Understand the moisture target at harvest

Sorghum and millet pass through three late stages: soft dough (grain can be squeezed between fingers and pops), hard dough (grain dents under a thumbnail but is not brittle), and physiological maturity (grain is at maximum dry weight, black layer has formed at the base of the grain).

The economically correct harvest stage is hard dough to physiological maturity. Harvesting at soft dough loses 10–20% of grain weight. Harvesting past maturity exposes the crop to shattering and to bird damage.

Where quelea are present, harvest at hard dough — 5–10 days before maximum grain weight — because the 3–5% weight loss is far less than the 30–100% loss to birds. Where quelea are absent, wait for physiological maturity.

Lesson 2

Harvesting, Threshing and the Stover Second Harvest

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Harvest panicles cleanly and dry them before threshing
  • Value the stover as a second crop, not waste
  • Thresh without contaminating grain

Harvesting

Cut panicles with a knife or sickle, leaving the stalk standing if you intend to harvest stover separately. Alternatively cut the whole plant at ground level and stack sheaves to dry in the field for 5–10 days. Head-first harvest is preferred for sorghum to reduce soil contamination and improve drying.

Drying before threshing

Panicles must be sun-dried to 12–13% grain moisture before threshing — usually 5–7 days of sun on a tarpaulin or hard floor. Testing: bite a grain; if it cracks (not squashes) and no moisture is felt, it is dry enough.

Threshing

Hand-threshing by beating with sticks is common. Mechanical threshers give better grain quality but must be adjusted to avoid cracking. Thresh on a tarpaulin — not bare soil — to prevent stone contamination. Winnow to remove chaff and light material.

Stover — the second crop

The stalks and leaves left after harvest are not waste. Sorghum and millet stover is quality livestock feed, especially if treated with urea. It is also used for thatching, fencing, fuel and compost. Valuing stover as part of the crop's total return changes the economics: a well-managed sorghum crop yields 2 t/ha grain plus 3–5 t/ha stover. At local fodder prices, the stover can add 20–40% to the crop's total value.

Lesson 3

Drying and Hermetic Storage

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Dry grain to the correct moisture for safe storage
  • Choose between hermetic bags, silos and chemical methods
  • Understand storage loss economics

Drying to target

Sorghum and millet store safely at 12–13% moisture. Above 14%, mould and aflatoxin risk rises sharply. Above 16%, the grain will heat and rot within weeks.

Drying methods

  • Sun drying on tarpaulin or concrete — cheapest, works if done carefully (no hot concrete, thin layer, turned regularly)
  • Solar dryer — faster, better control, protects from rain and dust
  • Mechanical dryer — commercial scale

Storage methods compared

See Module 4 Lesson 5 for the full comparison. Key economics: hermetic bags cost $3–8 per 100 kg and cut losses from 10–30% to under 2%. For 1 tonne of grain, that is $30–80 spent to save 80–280 kg of grain. The value of 80–280 kg at $0.30–0.50/kg is $24–140. The bag pays for itself in one season.

Storage discipline

  • Use bags or silos that are clean and dry
  • Never mix new grain with old
  • Stack bags on pallets, not on the ground
  • Keep away from walls
  • Check monthly for insect activity, rodent damage, and moisture
  • Hermetic bags must be properly sealed — a single tear defeats the purpose
Lesson 4

Processing — Flour, Instant Foods, Malting

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Choose between selling raw grain, flour, or a further-processed product
  • Understand the malting process for sorghum
  • Calculate the margin multiplier at each processing step

Raw grain

Baseline product. Price $0.20–0.50/kg depending on region and market. No processing margin.

Flour

Sorghum and millet flour sell at 1.5–3x the raw grain price. Small hammer mills (locally fabricated, $200–1,500) let a farmer or a co-op mill grain for themselves and for a fee. Value addition is the milling fee plus a share of the retail price.

Instant porridge and composite flours

A growing urban market pays well for pre-cooked, fortified instant porridge — traditional West African "ogi" or "koko", or the East African "uji". Products are sold in 500 g or 1 kg packs at 4–8x the raw grain price. Requires food safety compliance, packaging and a retail channel.

Malting

The highest-value transformation. Malting is the controlled germination and drying of grain. Sorghum malting supplies the brewing industry (both commercial and traditional), and the malting process itself is a micro-business. Steps:

  1. Steeping — soak clean white low-tannin sorghum in water for 24–36 hours
  2. Germination — spread in a thin layer, keep moist, allow 3–5 days of sprouting. The grain modifies its own starch into sugar.
  3. Kilning — dry and roast gently to stop the process at the right colour and flavour. Temperature control is critical.
  4. De-rooting and cleaning — remove rootlets and any defective grain
  5. Packing — for brewer, miller, or retail

Malting is a skill. It requires clean white low-tannin sorghum (not brown), controlled temperature and humidity, and attention to hygiene. A well-run malting operation sells to breweries at $0.40–0.80/kg for the finished malt, versus $0.25–0.40/kg for raw grain. The margin multiplier is 1.5–3x.

Lesson 5

By-Products and the Full Value Chain

~9 min
In this lesson
  • List the by-products of sorghum and millet processing
  • Value the stover, bran, brewers' grains as income streams
  • See the whole crop as a value chain, not a single product

A sorghum crop produces more than grain. A farmer who captures the by-products captures 20–40% more revenue from the same field.

  • Stover — livestock feed, fuel, thatch, compost. $30–100/t depending on region
  • Bran — from milling, sells as animal feed at $0.10–0.25/kg
  • Brewers' spent grain — from malting or brewing, high-protein feed, $0.10–0.20/kg
  • Malting rootlets — high-protein feed additive
  • Waste water from steeping — used as liquid fertiliser in some systems

A 1-hectare sorghum crop, fully processed and marketed, can generate:

  • 2 t grain → $500–1,000 depending on market
  • 3–5 t stover → $100–400 in feed value
  • 500–700 kg bran (from milling 2 t) → $60–150
  • Optional malt margin on 1 t → $200–400

Total realistic crop value: $700–1,700/ha. The grain alone is $500–1,000. The difference is captured by farmers who think of their crop as a value chain.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. What moisture level is correct for safe storage?

Correct: 12–13%. Above 14% mould risk rises sharply; above 16% grain will heat and rot.

2. If quelea birds are present, when should you harvest?

Correct: hard dough, 5–10 days early. 3–5% weight loss is better than 30–100% bird loss.

3. What is the approximate price premium for sorghum flour over raw grain?

Correct: 1.5–3x. Milling margin.

4. What type of sorghum should be used for malting?

Correct: white, low-tannin sorghum. Tannins interfere with germination and beer flavour.

5. What is the value of stover from a good sorghum crop?

Correct: $30–100/t as feed. Stover is a real second crop.

Module 5 capstone

Write a one-page post-harvest plan for your sorghum or millet crop. Include: (1) your harvest date or window; (2) your drying method and moisture target; (3) your storage choice and cost; (4) whether you will sell raw, mill, or malt; (5) your plan for the stover; (6) the expected revenue from grain plus by-products. Compare that total to what you expected when you sowed the crop.
