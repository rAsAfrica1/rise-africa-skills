4. Feeding and Nutrition

Snails are cheap to feed — most of their diet grows wild in Africa. But feeding them correctly is what determines whether they grow fast or stay small. This module covers what to feed, how much, when, and the calcium that makes the difference between thin shells and profitable snails.

Module 4 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 4.1 — Leafy greens — the base of the diet

Snails are herbivores with a strong preference for tender, calcium-rich leaves. Leafy greens should make up 60–70% of their diet.

LeafCalciumNotes
Pawpaw (papaya) leavesHighExcellent staple. Grow pawpaw trees near your pen.
Cocoyam leavesHighAvailable in most of West/Central Africa
Cabbage leaves (outer)MediumCheap from markets, snails love them
LettuceMediumFavourite food, but more expensive
SpinachMediumGood nutrition, widely available
Moringa leavesVery highExcellent if you have moringa trees
Cassava leavesHighDo not use raw — slight cyanide risk. Use only if cooked and cooled.
Sweet potato leavesHighGood staple, grows easily
Amaranth leavesHighCommon African vegetable

What to avoid:

  • Pesticide-treated leaves. Kills snails. Never feed leaves from sprayed gardens.
  • Spicy or aromatic leaves — onions, garlic, chilli. Snails refuse them.
  • Tough, fibrous leaves — snails prefer tender.
  • Wet or rotten leaves — causes mould.
The free-food rule: Pawpaw leaves alone can sustain a snail farm at almost zero feed cost. Plant 5 pawpaw trees near your pen. They provide leaves year-round with no cost after the first year.
Practical exercise 4.1: Identify 5 leafy sources near you. Test with your snails — place 3 small piles of different leaves and see which they eat first. This tells you their preference and your free feed sources.

Lesson 4.2 — Fruits — for growth and palatability

Fruits are snacks, not staples — but snails love them and they speed growth. Use fruits as 20–30% of the diet.

FruitNotes
PawpawTop favourite. Soft flesh, high sugar, easy.
MangoSeasonal favourite. Ripe, soft flesh.
BananaCheap, available year-round.
Watermelon rindFree from markets. Snails eat the rind.
TomatoOver-ripe tomatoes rejected at market are ideal.
AvocadoHigh fat — excellent for fast growth, but expensive.
CucumberWater-rich, good in hot weather.
Passion fruit pulpExtract from reject fruits, highly attractive.

Rules for fruits:

  • Ripe or over-ripe only. Unripe fruit is refused.
  • Wash all fruits. Remove pesticide residue.
  • Remove uneaten fruit daily. Rotting fruit attracts pests and disease.
  • Cut into small pieces. Snails have small mouths — chunks of 2–3 cm are ideal.
Practical exercise 4.2: Visit your local market at closing time. Ask for over-ripe or rejected fruits — often free. Bring home and feed to snails. Test which fruits are most accepted.

Lesson 4.3 — Calcium sources — the shell-building mineral

Without calcium, snails cannot build strong shells. Growth stalls, shells thin, and mortality rises. Calcium is non-negotiable.

SourceNotes
Crushed eggshellsFree from kitchens, markets, hatcheries. Bake to sterilise, crush fine.
Limestone powderBuy from agricultural suppliers. Cheap in bulk.
Oyster shell (ground)Sold as poultry feed supplement. Excellent source.
Cuttlebone (ground)Bought from pet shops. Good but small quantities.
Wood ashFree if you have fires. Contains calcium and potash.
Dolomite powderAgricultural limestone. Cheap in bulk.
GypsumAvailable where cement is sold. Use sparingly.

How to feed calcium:

  • Mix into soil. One cup of crushed eggshell per 10 litres of bedding.
  • Sprinkle on top of food. A light dusting on fruits and leaves.
  • Separate calcium tray. Place a small dish of crushed shells in the pen. Snails self-regulate.
  • Weekly replenishment. Snails consume calcium continuously. Refresh weekly.
The calcium rule: If you do only one thing for your snails' health, provide calcium. It is the single biggest factor in shell quality, growth rate, and survival. Cheap eggshells are the best free source.
Practical exercise 4.3: Collect eggshells from your kitchen for one week. Bake in the oven at 200°C for 10 minutes to sterilise. Crush fine. Add one cup per 10 litres of bedding. This is your first calcium supply.

Lesson 4.4 — Commercial feed and supplements

You do not need commercial feed for snails. But for faster growth or larger-scale operations, supplements help.

SupplementPurposeCost
Poultry layer mashProtein boost, faster growthLow
Fish mealHigh protein, especially for hatchlingsMedium
Groundnut cakeProtein, energyLow
Soya mealProteinLow
Dried moringa leaf powderProtein + calcium + mineralsFree if you have moringa
Agricultural limeBulk calciumVery low

How to use supplements:

  • Sprinkle 1–2 teaspoons per 100 snails, 2–3× per week. More is wasted and can attract pests.
  • Do not replace fresh food with supplements. Supplements are additions, not substitutes.
  • Hatchlings benefit most. A protein-rich diet in their first 2 months dramatically improves survival.
  • Do not over-supplement. Snails are sensitive to excess. Small quantities, frequent.
Practical exercise 4.4: Buy 1 kg of poultry layer mash. Feed 1 teaspoon per 100 snails twice per week. Compare growth of this group to a group without the supplement over 60 days.

Lesson 4.5 — Feeding schedule and quantities

Snails are nocturnal feeders. Most of their eating happens at night or early morning.

StageFeeding frequencyQuantity
Hatchlings (0–30 days)DailyFinely chopped leaves, more than they can eat
Juveniles (1–6 months)Every other dayLeaves to cover 20% of pen area
Adults (6+ months)Every other dayLeaves + fruits to cover 25% of pen area
Breeding adultsDailyExtra calcium, richer fruits

Feeding routine:

  1. Feed in the late afternoon. Snails are most active at night.
  2. Remove old food before adding new. Prevents mould and pests.
  3. Scatter food evenly. Not in one pile — every snail should have access.
  4. Water after feeding. Spray lightly. Moisture activates feeding.
  5. Check the next morning. If food is untouched, something is wrong (temperature, humidity, disease).
Never leave food more than 48 hours. Rotting food attracts ants, mites, and mould. Remove any uneaten food at the next feeding. Snails will eat more of fresh food than of stale.
Practical exercise 4.5: Set a feeding schedule. Note the quantity fed, what was consumed, and how many snails. Adjust after 2 weeks based on consumption.

Lesson 4.6 — Water and humidity management

Snails do not drink water directly. They absorb moisture through their skin and from their food. Humidity is their water supply.

Humidity targets:

  • 70–85% — ideal for all stages
  • Below 60% — snails retract into shells, stop eating, growth halts
  • Above 90% — mould growth, respiratory problems

How to maintain humidity:

  • Spray the pen once or twice daily. Use a watering can or spray bottle. Light mist, not soaking.
  • Early morning or late afternoon. Never midday when heat evaporates the water instantly.
  • Shade the pen. Direct sun drops humidity fast.
  • Add water-holding material. Sphagnum moss or coconut coir helps maintain moisture.
  • Test with your hand. The bedding should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
WeatherSpray frequency
Hot and dry2–3× per day
Moderate1–2× per day
Rainy or humidEvery other day, or skip
ColdOnce per day, light spray
The moisture rule: Damp, not wet. Bedding that drips water when squeezed is too wet — snails can drown. Bedding that crumbles is too dry. Aim for the texture of a wrung-out sponge.
Practical exercise 4.6: Buy a hygrometer ($10–$20). Place it inside your pen. Log humidity twice daily for 2 weeks. Adjust your spraying until you consistently hold 70–85%.
