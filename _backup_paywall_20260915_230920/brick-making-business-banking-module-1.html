1. The Brick Making Business Model

Brick making is one of the most reliable construction businesses in Africa. Demand never stops — homes, shops, schools and factories all need blocks. But the businesses that make real money are the ones who understand the numbers. This module covers the market, the two business models, and the real economics.

Module 1 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 1.1 — Types of bricks — clay, cement, fly ash

Three types of building bricks dominate the African market. Each uses different raw materials, has different production methods, and serves a different customer.

TypeRaw materialsProductionBest for
Clay bricks (fired)Clay, water, kiln fuelMould, dry, fire in kilnTraditional housing, decorative walls
Cement blocks (concrete)Cement, sand, waterMix, mould, cureModern housing, commercial walls
Fly ash bricksFly ash, lime, sand, gypsumMix, press, cureLow-cost housing, industrial buyers

In most African countries, cement blocks dominate — no kiln needed, faster to make, and the standard for modern construction. Clay bricks are a smaller traditional market. Fly ash bricks need access to a coal power station for the ash.

For a start-up, cement blocks are the practical choice: largest market, lowest startup cost, simplest production. You can expand into clay or fly ash later.

Practical exercise 1.1: Visit 3 hardware shops in your area. Ask: what block sizes do you sell most? What is the current price per block? Which sells faster — cement or clay? This is your first market research.

Lesson 1.2 — The demand cycle

Construction follows seasons. In most of Southern and East Africa, activity peaks in the dry months and slows during heavy rains and the year-end holidays.

SeasonDemandTypical months
Peak constructionVery highApril–September (dry season)
ModerateMediumOct–Nov, Feb–Mar
Off-seasonLowDec–Jan, peak rains

Businesses that succeed plan around this. Two strategies:

  • Stockpile in off-season. Cure 20,000–50,000 blocks during the slow months — they age well and sell at full price in the dry season.
  • Diversify product. Add pavers, kerbstones, or decorative blocks for off-season sales.
Cash flow rule: Save 30% of peak-season profit as an off-season reserve. If you spend everything in peak, the slow months will break you.
Practical exercise 1.2: Ask 5 local contractors: which months are busiest? Which are slowest? Their answers become your production calendar.

Lesson 1.4 — Volume vs premium bricks

Two business models. Most successful yards run both.

Model A — Volume blocks. Standard size, mass produced, low price ($0.70–$0.90). Customer: contractors, government housing, commercial developers. Output: 5,000–50,000 per month. Margin: 30–40%.

Model B — Premium blocks. Decorative, coloured, patterned, non-standard sizes. Price $1.50–$4.00 each. Customer: homeowners, architects, specialty builders. Output: 500–3,000 per month. Margin: 50–70%.

ModelVolume/monthPrice/blockMonthly revenueMargin

Practical exercise 1.4: Pick your starting model. If volume, find 2 contractors ready to buy. If premium, design 3 premium block styles and price them for a local market.

Lesson 1.5 — Site selection

Location is the second biggest cost after materials. A bad site adds 20–30% to your per-block cost.

  • Water access. Brick making uses 60–80 litres per 100 blocks plus curing water. Site near a borehole, dam, or river.
  • Sand source nearby. Sand is heavy. Anything more than 10 km adds real cost.
  • Cement supplier nearby. Cement is 40–50% of your cost. Be close to a wholesaler.
  • Market access. On a main road or within 10 km of active construction.
  • Space. 200–500 m² for a small yard: cement store, sand pile, mixing area, moulding area, curing area, stockpile.
  • Security. Bricks attract thieves once stock builds up. Fence the yard.
  • All-weather access road. Trucks must reach your yard even in the rains.
The location rule: Never compromise on water. A site with good market access and no water is worse than the reverse. Water is the constraint that kills brick businesses.
Practical exercise 1.5: Find 3 candidate sites. For each, list water source, sand source, cement supplier, road access, size, and rent. Score each 1–5. Pick the highest.

Monthly operating cost (5,000 blocks): cement $1,100, sand $165, water $30, labour $160, rent $100, transport $150, misc $100 = $1,805. Revenue at 5,000 × $0.85 = $4,250. Monthly gross profit = $2,445.

Break-even = about 2,124 blocks per month. Very achievable within 3 months of steady operation.

Realistic income:

  • Month 1–2: $0–$500 profit — learning curve
  • Month 3–6: $1,000–$2,500 per month — steady small operation
  • Month 6–12: $2,500–$5,000 per month — established local supplier
  • Year 2+: $5,000+ per month if you scale to 15,000–20,000 blocks
Practical exercise 1.6: Build the same table with your local prices. Write your break-even volume on the wall of your future yard.
