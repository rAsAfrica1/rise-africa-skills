4. Step 4 — Set Up the Legal and Admin Basics

You do not need a lawyer to register a business in Africa. But you do need to do it right — because banks, corporate clients, and government buyers all check. This module walks through registration, banking, tax, mobile money, licences, and insurance in plain language.

Module 4 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 4.1 — Registering a business in your country — the actual steps

Registering your business is easier and cheaper than most people think. In most African countries it costs between $20 and $200, and takes 3–10 working days when done online. The four common legal structures are:

StructureBest forLiabilityCost
Sole proprietor / sole traderOne-person businesses under $5k revenuePersonal liability — your house is on the lineCheapest, sometimes free
PartnershipTwo or more people sharing ownershipUnlimited joint liabilityLow
Private Limited Company (Ltd/Pvt)Any business that wants contracts, bank accounts, or investmentLimited to what you put inModerate
Cooperative / TrustGroup-owned, social enterprise, NGO-adjacentMembers' liability limitedLow

Most small businesses in Africa register as Private Limited Companies. It costs a bit more but it opens doors — banks will give you a business account, corporate clients will sign contracts, and if things go wrong, your personal assets are protected.

The generic registration process in most countries:

  1. Name search and reservation — online portal at the companies registry (e.g., ZIMRA, CIPRO, eCitizen, NAFDAC, RGD). Costs $5–$30. Takes 1–2 days.
  2. File incorporation documents — Memorandum and Articles of Association, directors' details, share structure, registered address. Costs $50–$200. Takes 3–7 days.
  3. Receive Certificate of Incorporation — a legal document that proves your business exists.
  4. Tax registration — register with the revenue authority for a Tax Identification Number (TIN) or equivalent. Usually free.
  5. Industry licences — depending on your sector: food handling, health, environmental, import/export. Separate applications.
  6. Municipal / local permits — business licence from your local council. Costs $20–$100 per year.
Do not pay agents more than $200 total. Every country has an online registration portal. If someone quotes $500 for "incorporation", say no and use the portal yourself, or find a registered accountant who charges fairly. Rip-offs in this space are common.
Practical exercise 4.1: Go to your country's companies registry website. Find the exact fee, required documents, and processing time for registering a Private Limited Company. Write them down. If you have the money ready, register this week.

Lesson 4.2 — Business bank account vs personal — why it matters

Keeping your business money and personal money in the same account is the single most common financial mistake small business owners make. It hides your real profit, makes tax filing impossible, and prevents you from ever proving your business to a bank or investor.

Open a dedicated business account as soon as you are registered. A business account is different from a personal one in four key ways:

  • It is opened in the business name, using the Certificate of Incorporation.
  • It accepts payments to the business name — customers can pay by cheque, transfer, or card into "Your Business Pvt Ltd".
  • It supports separate bookkeeping — expenses and revenue flow through it distinctly.
  • It builds a banking history — after 6–12 months of clean transactions, you qualify for overdrafts, merchant facilities, and loans.

Do not wait until you "have enough money" to open a business account. Open it on day one. Even with $50, a clean business account history is worth more than the money in it.

Banking costs to watch:

Fee typeTypical costWatch out for
Monthly account fee$2–$15Some banks charge much more for "premium" business accounts
Transaction fees$0.20–$1 eachCheaper on high-volume accounts
Cash deposit fees0.5–2% of amountIf your business is cash-heavy, this adds up fast
Minimum balance$0–$500Fees apply if you dip below — read the fine print
Never mix personal and business. If you take money out for personal use, record it as "owner's drawings" — not as an expense. If you put personal money in, record it as "capital contribution". Clean records are what a bank looks at when deciding whether to lend to you.
Practical exercise 4.2: Visit two banks this week. Ask: what does a business account cost per month, what is the minimum balance, and what documents do you need? Compare and pick one. Open it.

Lesson 4.3 — Tax registration and what you must file

Tax is not optional, but it is also not the enemy. Paying tax correctly makes you a legitimate business — eligible for government contracts, corporate clients, and credit. Not paying it eventually catches up with penalties, interest, and bank account freezes.

The main business taxes in most African countries:

TaxWhat it isWho pays
Corporate income taxTax on business profitsAll registered companies
VATTax on sales, added to invoiceBusinesses above a threshold (e.g. $60k/year)
PAYETax withheld from employee salariesAny business with employees
Withholding taxTax withheld on certain payments to suppliersWhen paying contractors
Presumptive taxSimplified flat tax for small tradersSmall businesses under a revenue threshold

Most new small businesses fall under the presumptive or small business regime — a simplified system with fixed or low-rate taxes and simple quarterly returns. Filing in this regime is straightforward and can often be done online in 10 minutes.

What you must keep, regardless of regime:

  • Sales records — every sale, with date, amount, and customer name where possible
  • Expense receipts — every purchase with proof of payment
  • Bank statements — clean, reconciled monthly
  • Employee records — contracts, PAYE deductions, NSSA / social security contributions
File on time, even with zeros. A zero return filed on time is better than a missing return. Penalties accrue from the missed deadline, not from the tax owing. If you had no income this quarter, file "no income" and avoid the penalty.
Practical exercise 4.3: Register for your country's tax regime this week. Ask a friend with a registered business to walk you through their last filing. File your first return — even if it is a zero return.

Lesson 4.4 — Mobile money integration

In most of Africa today, more customers pay by mobile money (EcoCash, M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, Orange Money) than by card or bank transfer. If you do not accept mobile money, you lose sales — especially from informal-market customers.

You have three options to accept mobile money:

OptionHow it worksFeesBest for
Personal lineCustomer sends to your personal numberFree but mixes personal and businessVery small, start-up phase
Merchant/business accountRegistered merchant code with your business name1–3% per transactionAny established business
Payment gatewayIntegration via Paynow, Flutterwave, Paystack2–4% per transactionBusinesses with online sales / websites

The merchant account is the best step for any business that has more than 20 transactions a week. It costs nothing to set up, gives you a merchant code and QR code, and makes customers trust you more because the payment shows your business name.

Fraud protection is essential. Watch for:

  • Fake confirmation screenshots — always check your own phone or app to confirm money received. Never trust a screenshot.
  • Chargebacks — customers who claim they paid and demand a refund. Keep transaction records.
  • Agent scams — fake mobile-money agents offering to "process" payments for a fee. Never hand your phone to a stranger.
Merchant code rule: If you take more than 20 mobile money transactions a week, get a business merchant code. It builds trust, gives you records, and reduces fraud. Ask your mobile operator this week.
Practical exercise 4.4: Apply for a merchant/business mobile money account with your main operator this week. Test one transaction with a friend. Print your QR code and put it at your point of sale.

Lesson 4.5 — Industry-specific licences

Some businesses need special licences beyond the basic company registration. These are the ones most common across Africa:

Business typeRequired licencesApproximate cost
Food / restaurant / bakeryFood handler's certificate, municipal health permit, food safety inspection$50–$300
Bar / bottle storeLiquor licence from council or national authority$100–$1,000
Pharmacy / health clinicMedical council registration, premises inspection$300–$2,000
Transport / logisticsVehicle licensing, roadworthy certificate, passenger transport permit if applicable$100–$500 per vehicle
Import / exportCustoms registration, export licence for some goods$100–$500
ConstructionContractor grading from the national construction councilVaries
Agriculture / livestockEnvironmental impact assessment for larger farms, animal health permits$50–$500
Chemicals / pesticides / fuelEnvironmental agency permits, dangerous goods licenceHigh — several hundred to thousands

Inspect first, register second. Visit your local council offices and ask directly: "What licences does a business like mine need?" Get it in writing. Then apply. This takes a few hours but saves months of trouble later — including raids, fines, and forced closure.

Unlicensed businesses get closed. Not always, but the moment a competitor or unhappy customer reports you, inspectors arrive. Getting licensed now is cheaper than emergency licensing later with penalties and closures.
Practical exercise 4.5: Visit your local council office. Ask for the exact licence list for your business type. Write it down with costs and renewal dates. If anything is missing, apply this month.

Lesson 4.6 — Insurance — the essential coverage

Insurance feels like money thrown away — until the day you need it. Small businesses in Africa lose more from uninsured events (fire, theft, accident, illness) than from almost any other cause. Three types of insurance every business should consider:

InsuranceWhat it coversTypical cost
Business contents / stockFire, theft, flood damage to equipment and stock0.5–2% of insured value per year
Public liabilityDamage or injury caused to third parties by your business$50–$500 per year for small businesses
Vehicle insuranceThird-party (mandatory in most countries) plus own damage3–10% of vehicle value per year

For food businesses, add product liability — cover against claims if a customer gets sick from your product. It is cheap and protects you from ruin.

For any business with employees, you must have workers compensation / employer's liability — usually mandatory under labour law. If a worker is injured on the job, this insurance pays their medical costs and lost wages.

Insurance is not a cost — it is a floor. It means the worst outcome for your business is "we recover and try again", not "we are finished forever". A $200 policy today can be the difference between surviving a fire and losing everything.
Practical exercise 4.6: Get quotes from at least two insurers. Compare coverage and cost for business contents + public liability. If affordable, take out a policy this month. If not, at least save the monthly premium amount separately as a self-insurance fund.
