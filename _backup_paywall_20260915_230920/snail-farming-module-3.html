3. Housing and Pen Construction

Your snail pen is their entire world. Get the design right and snails thrive. Get it wrong and you fight disease, predators, and stalled growth forever. This module shows you four working pen designs, material selection, and the pest-proofing that saves your stock.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Pen types — hutch, trench, box, cage

Recommendations for start-ups:

  • Rural or smallholding: Trench pen or cage pen. Cheap, durable, hard to break into.
  • Urban or limited space: Hutch pen or box pen. Stackable, manageable.
  • Growing into commercial: Concrete block pen. Permanent, scalable.

Whichever you choose, the design must protect against:

  • Ants, rats, snakes, birds, lizards
  • Direct sun
  • Excessive rain that floods the pen
  • Escape
  • Thieves
Practical exercise 3.1: Walk your site. Decide where your pen will go. Choose the type that fits your space, capital, and predator load. Sketch the design.

Lesson 3.2 — Materials — wood, wire mesh, plastic, cement

MaterialProsConsBest use
Wood (treated)Cheap, easy to work withRots, termites, needs replacementBox pens, temporary structures
Wire meshPredator-proof, ventilationRusts, needs frameCage pens, top covers
Plastic trays/binsClean, portable, cheapSmall scale, degrades in sunBox pens, hatchling pens
Cement blockPermanent, secure, pest-proofExpensive, not portableCommercial pens
Concrete floor + block wallsBest permanent solutionHigh upfront costEstablished farms

The best budget design combines materials: concrete floor, brick or block sides (60 cm high), wire mesh top (anti-bird, anti-rat), mesh screen over the top with 5 mm openings.

Recommended specifications:

  • Height: 60–80 cm walls — enough for shade, not too deep for air
  • Floor: Concrete or hard-packed earth, with drainage
  • Sides: Block or wood, sealed against ants with oil or ash barrier
  • Top: Wire mesh 5 mm, or shade netting plus wire mesh
  • Bedding: 15–20 cm of loose loamy soil mixed with compost
Ant-proofing is critical. Ants are the #1 killer of snail hatchlings in Africa. Use a physical barrier — a moat of water, or a ring of wood ash, or engine oil on a cloth strip around the pen. Check weekly.
Practical exercise 3.2: Price the materials for your chosen pen design at 2 local hardware shops. Compare. Buy from the cheaper source. Build a prototype box pen first to test your technique.

Lesson 3.3 — Site selection

The pen location determines how well the environment stays within the ideal range.

Requirements:

  • Shade for most of the day. Under trees is ideal. Otherwise use shade netting.
  • Well-drained ground. Snails drown in standing water. Choose slightly elevated ground, or add drainage.
  • Wind protection. Not in an open field. Gentle airflow, not wind tunnel.
  • Near water source. You will spray the pen daily.
  • Close to your house. Snails need daily attention. Far away = neglected = dead.
  • Secure. Away from public paths, with a lock or fence.
Practical exercise 3.3: Mark 3 potential sites on your plot. For each, note: shade level, drainage, wind exposure, distance to water, distance to your house, security. Score each 1–5. Pick the highest.

Lesson 3.4 — Sizing a pen for your target number

Standard stocking density: 40–50 snails per m² for growing adults. Higher density = stunted growth, disease, mortality.

Stocking targetPen size needed
100 snails2–3 m²
500 snails10–13 m²
1,000 snails20–25 m²
2,000 snails40–50 m²
5,000 snails100–125 m²

Add extra space for:

  • Breeding area. Separate breeding pen with low density (25 per m²) encourages egg laying.
  • Hatchling nursery. Small trays or boxes for hatchlings. 100–200 hatchlings per m².
  • Feed and water station. Small area set aside.
  • Walking space. 30–50 cm paths for you to work.
The density rule: Under-stock when in doubt. Snails are far more productive at low density. When your pen fills, build a new one rather than crowding.
Practical exercise 3.4: Measure your available space. Calculate the maximum number of snails you can house at 45/m². Start with 50% of that number. Build the pen accordingly.

Lesson 3.5 — Bedding and soil preparation

Snails spend most of their time in the soil. The bedding determines humidity, egg-laying success, and general health.

Ideal bedding:

  • Loamy soil — not clay, not sandy. Crumbly, holds moisture without waterlogging.
  • pH 6.5–7.5 — slightly acidic to neutral.
  • Mixed with compost — 30% compost for nutrition and microbial health.
  • Crushed eggshells or limestone mixed in — provides calcium for shells.
  • Depth 15–20 cm — deep enough for egg laying, not so deep that bottom stays cold.

Preparation:

  1. Dig soil from a clean source — not from a pesticide-treated area.
  2. Sift to remove stones and debris.
  3. Mix 70% soil with 30% well-rotted compost.
  4. Add 1 cup of crushed eggshells or limestone per 10 litres of soil.
  5. Fill the pen with 15–20 cm of the mix.
  6. Dampen lightly — damp but not wet.
  7. Let it settle for 3–5 days before introducing snails.
Never use soil from areas treated with pesticides, herbicides, or chemical fertilisers. These kill snails. If in doubt, source soil from a forest or untouched plot.
Practical exercise 3.5: Prepare your bedding mix. Fill one pen and test humidity after 3 days. If the surface still feels damp, you have the right mix. If dry, add more compost and re-dampen.

Lesson 3.6 — Predator and pest-proofing

PestDamageProtection
AntsKill hatchlings and eggsWater moat, ash ring, oil barrier
RatsEat adults, especially at nightWire mesh sides and top, traps around
SnakesEat adultsWire mesh top, clean surroundings
BirdsPick at snails through open topsWire mesh or shade netting over top
LizardsEat hatchlings and juvenilesFine mesh (5 mm), no gaps
MitesFeed on snail skin, weaken themFresh bedding, quarantine new stock, remove dead snails promptly
TermitesDestroy wooden pensTreat wood, or use block construction

Building a defense system:

  1. Physical barrier. Continuous wire mesh walls with fine openings (5 mm) that go 15 cm below ground level too.
  2. Top cover. Wire mesh or shade netting over the whole pen. Removable for access.
  3. Water moat. A shallow trench around the pen filled with water. Ants cannot cross.
  4. Clean surroundings. No tall grass, no debris, no food scraps around the pen.
  5. Weekly inspection. Check for holes, worn mesh, ant trails. Repair immediately.
The 15 cm rule: Bury the wire mesh 15 cm below ground. Rats, snakes, and many insects burrow under low barriers. This single detail saves more snails than any other protection.
Practical exercise 3.6: Build your predator defense. Bury mesh 15 cm deep, cover the top, add a water moat. Check daily for a week — are any pests getting through? Fix every gap.
