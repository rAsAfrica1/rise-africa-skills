Module 2

Timber Selection, Cutting Lists and Seasoning

Every timber species has a personality. Some are naturally durable, some are termite magnets, some warp as they dry, some work easily. This module matches the species to the hive part, and gives you a cutting list you can take to any sawmill.

  • Choose timber by durability, workability and cost, not by habit
  • Recognise the timber species that are naturally termite-resistant in your area
  • Read a cutting list and know what each line item is for

Lesson 2.1

What a hive timber must do

A beehive is a piece of outdoor furniture that has to survive rain, sun, insects and the constant humidity of a living colony. The timber you choose has to satisfy five demands at once:

  1. Dimensionally stable — it does not warp, shrink or twist enough to open joints. Bee space is measured in millimetres; a board that moves 5 mm has broken the hive.
  2. Reasonably durable outdoors — will not rot within 3 years under a hive roof. Naturally durable or easily treated.
  3. Not attractive to termites — or treatable to become so.
  4. Non-toxic to bees — this rules out most treated timber for interior parts.
  5. Workable with hand tools — the species must plane, saw and nail without splitting constantly.

No single species is best at all five. The correct approach is to match species to part: exterior parts can be more durable but toxic; interior parts must be safe for bees.

The rule: two timber types, not one. Durable but potentially treated timber for the outer shell (roof, stand, outer box). Untreated, bee-safe timber for anything bees touch (frames, top bars, brood box interior).

For most African beekeepers, the practical choice is:

  • Exterior (roof, stand, outer box): any locally available hardwood, treated if necessary, at the cheapest price. Cypress, eucalyptus or a local hardwood are all fine.
  • Interior (frames, top bars, brood box): untreated softwood or a light hardwood. Pine, mango, or cypress in the untreated state.
Never use treated timber for anything bees touch. Preservatives are designed to kill insects. Bees are insects. Honey is food. This rule is absolute for frames, top bars, and the inside of the brood box.

Lesson 2.4

Seasoning — the step that decides whether your hive lasts

Freshly cut timber is "green". It contains 40–80% moisture by weight. As it dries, it shrinks. Different dimensions shrink at different rates — tangential shrinkage is roughly double radial — and the result is warp, twist, bow and split. A hive built from green timber will be twisted and split within six months, and the bee space will be destroyed.

Target moisture

Air-dry timber in most of Africa settles at 10–15% moisture content. This is the correct moisture for building. If you can afford a cheap moisture meter ($40–100), it saves you guessing.

How to air-dry on site

  1. Stack timber off the ground on sacrificial bearers, at least 150 mm clear of soil.
  2. Separate each layer with spacer battens (20 mm thick, 20 mm wide) placed every 400 mm. Air must move between every board.
  3. Site the stack in the shade but with good airflow. Direct sun on one side of a stack causes that side to dry faster, and the boards cup.
  4. Weight the top of the stack with heavy timber or stones. This reduces warp and twist while drying.
  5. Cover the top with a roof (a sheet of roofing or a tarpaulin) but leave the sides open to the wind.
  6. Turn the stack every 4–6 weeks to dry evenly.
  7. Time to air-dry: 20 mm boards in tropical Africa need 6–10 weeks. 50 mm boards need 4–6 months. The rule of thumb: one month per 25 mm of thickness, minimum.

How to know when it is dry

  • Weight test: a dry board weighs 20–30% less than the same board green. Weigh one sample board and track its weight weekly.
  • Moisture meter: target 12% or below.
  • Knock test: strike two dry boards together. They ring. Wet boards thud.
  • Shrinkage test: cut a fresh cross-section and measure it. Re-measure every two weeks. When it stops shrinking, the board is dry.
The seasoning rule: if you can only buy green timber, buy it 3 months before you plan to build, stack it properly, and let it dry. Rushing this step is the single most common cause of hives that twist apart within a year.

Lesson 2.5

The three mistakes that destroy hives

1. Building with unseasoned timber

The hive fits perfectly when built. Six weeks later the boards shrink, the joints open, and the roof gaps. Rain enters. The bees propolise the gaps. A year later the whole thing is scrap. This is the single biggest cause of hive failures in small apiaries.

2. Using treated timber inside the brood box

Preservative chemicals kill bees and contaminate honey. This is a slow kill — the colony may survive for months before succumbing to chronic poisoning. The tell-tale signs are a queen that stops laying, dwindling workers, and eventually absconding. Never treat interior hive timber.

3. Mismatched dimensions between hives

Hives built from different cutting lists do not interchange. A super from one hive does not fit another. Frames from one Langstroth are a millimetre too long for another. In an apiary of twenty hives this is a permanent problem. Decide the standard dimensions once, and never deviate.

The three fixes: (1) season timber for at least 3 months; (2) keep treated timber on the outside; (3) pick one dimension standard and stick to it. That is the whole discipline.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. Why must treated timber never be used for hive frames or the brood box interior?

Correct: preservatives kill bees. Chronic poisoning kills the colony slowly. Never treat interior hive parts.

2. What is the target moisture content for hive timber before building?

Correct: 10–15%. Air-dry condition in most of Africa. Drier causes splitting; wetter causes warp.

3. How long should 20 mm boards air-dry in tropical Africa?

Correct: 6–10 weeks. Rule of thumb: one month per 25 mm of thickness minimum.

4. Why does green timber destroy a hive?

Correct: uneven shrinkage. A hive built from green timber has perfectly matched joints that open within months.

5. What timber choice is correct for exterior hive parts?

Correct: locally available hardwood, treated if necessary. Exterior parts do not touch the bees, so treatment is fine. Interior must be untreated.

Practical assignment — Module 2

Produce a real timber sourcing and seasoning plan for your hive build.

  1. Which hive type are you building and how many? (Langstroth, top-bar, both.)
  2. Your cutting list — one full hive. Use the tables from Lesson 2.3 or your local beekeeping association's dimensions, and state which.
  3. Timber species for exterior and interior parts, with a stated reason for each.
  4. Source and price — at least two timber suppliers with current prices per board or per metre. State if you will use reclaimed timber or mill your own.
  5. Seasoning plan — where you will stack, how long it will dry, and how you will test moisture before building.
  6. Preservative plan — what you will treat and what you will not. Name the preservative product.
  7. One paragraph (150 words) naming the single biggest timber-related mistake you could make on this build and how you will avoid it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 7. The cutting list is the specification; the paragraph is the judgement.
