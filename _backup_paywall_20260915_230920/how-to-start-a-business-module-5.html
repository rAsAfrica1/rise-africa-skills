5. Step 5 — Launch and Get Your First Customer

You have validated, set up, and registered. Now you launch. This module is about the first 30 days of action — how to find your first 10 customers, how to price the first sale, how to turn one sale into testimonials, and how to build the small momentum that becomes a real business.

Module 5 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 5.1 — Your launch checklist

Before you open, run through this checklist. Anything missing will cost you customers or money in week one.

AreaCheckWhy it matters
Product20+ units ready, or service ready to deliverNothing kills a launch faster than "out of stock"
PricingCost calculated, price set, discounts plannedYou cannot negotiate without knowing your floor
PaymentMobile money ready, cash change ready, receipts printedFumbling payment kills the sale
LocationStanding, table, sign, packaging readyVisibility drives walk-ins
CommunicationWhatsApp Business set up, contact list of 100+ potential customersWhere the first 10 customers come from
RecordsSales book, receipt book, cash tin, expense folderYou cannot improve what you do not measure
Backup planWhat if you sell out? What if you sell nothing?Both scenarios need a plan

The two most common launch failures: running out of stock (so you cannot serve the customers you attracted) and having no record system (so you do not know if you actually made money).

Practical exercise 5.1: Print this checklist. Tick every item. Do not launch until all seven areas are ready. Launching underprepared wastes your first week of customers.

Lesson 5.2 — Where to find your first 10 customers

Your first 10 customers almost never come from advertising. They come from your existing network, your physical location, and one or two very deliberate outreach moves.

Here is the fastest sequence:

1 — Start with your WhatsApp contact list. Send a personal message (not a broadcast) to 40 people you know. Not the sales pitch — the introduction: "I have started a [business]. If you or anyone you know needs [product], message me. I am offering [X]% off for the first 10 orders this week." Personal messages get replies. Broadcasts get ignored.

2 — Do a physical launch. Set up where your target customers already are. Even one day of pop-up selling beats three weeks of waiting. Market stall, church event, community meeting, office park lunch hour. Somewhere with 50+ people passing.

3 — Partner with someone who already has your customers. A hairdresser could recommend your cakes to her clients. A hardware shop could recommend your welding to customers buying iron. A mother's group could be your first distribution network. Give them 10% commission for every sale they bring.

4 — Do a targeted social media post. One post on the Facebook group where your target customer hangs out. Not the whole platform — just where they already are. Ask the group admin for permission first (better response).

5 — Follow up on every enquiry, immediately. Within 5 minutes if possible. Speed of response is the single biggest driver of conversion in small business. Slow repliers lose sales to faster competitors.

The 10-customer rule: You do not need 100 customers in the first month. You need 10. Ten paying customers prove the business works. Ten becomes 50 through referrals. Focus on depth over breadth in the first 30 days.
Practical exercise 5.2: Send personal WhatsApp messages to 40 people this week. Set up one physical pop-up. Ask 3 people who serve your target customer to partner with you. Track how each of the first 10 sales came in.

Lesson 5.3 — Pricing the first sale — introductory vs standard

Should your first sale be discounted? Yes — but with limits. An introductory price brings in the first customers. But it teaches the market what you are worth, and if you start too low you will fight for years to raise prices.

The right structure:

Price tierWho pays itDiscountDuration
Friends & familyPeople who know you20–30% offFirst 10–20 sales only
Launch offerPublic, first wave10–15% offFirst 30 days only
Standard priceEveryone after0%Ongoing

Two rules. Rule one: never discount below your cost. Even for friends. If you cannot give 20% off and still make a small profit, that discount is a loss. Rule two: tell people when the discount ends. "This is our launch price for the next 30 days" builds urgency and makes customers feel they got a real deal.

Never drop your price to match a cheaper competitor. Either you have a real cost advantage, or you are losing money on purpose. Every price war destroys profits for everyone in it.
Practical exercise 5.3: Set your three prices: friends/family, launch, standard. Write the date the launch offer ends. Do not move the end date once you set it — you are training the market.

Lesson 5.4 — Free samples vs paid pilots

Should you give away product to build buzz, or charge from day one? Both work — but they work for different reasons.

Free samples are for two things only: (a) testing product-market fit before launch, and (b) generating word-of-mouth at a specific event. Free samples to random people with no plan = money wasted. Free samples in a planned session with feedback collection = money well spent.

Paid pilots are for anything beyond that. Once you have validated demand, every interaction should be paid. Even if it is a small amount, even from friends — payment is what turns a nice exchange into a real customer.

Convert free to paid this way:

  • At the end of a free sample session, ask: "Would you like to buy one now for $X?" Have stock ready. About 20–30% will say yes if they liked the product.
  • For those who say no, ask: "Can I keep your number and message you when the offer ends?" Follow up in 7 days.
  • For corporate or larger orders, offer a small paid pilot: "Try 20 units at 20% off. If you like them, order at full price." This is your strongest business-to-business conversion method.
The conversion rule: Once you have validated demand, every interaction should have a price attached. Free samples are a marketing tool used sparingly. Paid pilot orders are the real starting signal.
Practical exercise 5.4: Plan one free sample session this month (target 20 samples) and one paid pilot with a partner business. Record how many converted to paid customers at each stage.

Lesson 5.5 — The importance of one paying customer

Your first paying customer is not just one sale. It is proof — to you, and to the world — that a stranger will exchange real money for what you have made. This single transaction changes how you think about your business.

Three things happen when you make your first real sale:

1 — You stop being an aspiring entrepreneur. You become an entrepreneur. The line between them is one sale. Every business in Africa, from a market stall to a big exporter, started with one paying customer.

2 — You learn more than any course can teach you. What did they ask about? What surprised them? What did they hesitate on before buying? What would they have paid more for? These insights are worth more than market research.

3 — You have a name and a story. From now on, when someone asks if your business is real, you can say: "Yes — [Name] bought from me last week." That is your first testimonial.

The single most important job in week one is not making 100 sales. It is making one and learning everything you can from it.

Practical exercise 5.5: After your first sale, write down three things the customer said or did. What did they ask? What made them hesitate? What did they like most? Keep these notes — they will shape your next 100 sales.

Lesson 5.6 — Collecting testimonials and photos

Testimonials are the most valuable marketing asset a small business can own. They cost nothing to collect, and they sell better than any advertisement. But most small businesses fail to collect them because they feel awkward asking.

Here is how to do it without feeling pushy:

1 — Ask immediately after the sale. "If you like it, would you mind sending me a short message about your experience? I am just starting out and it would really help." Send the request within 30 minutes of the sale, while the experience is fresh.

2 — Make it easy. Give them the exact words to start with: "Just say what you bought, why you liked it, and whether you would buy again."

3 — Ask for a photo with the product, if the customer is willing. A photo of a real customer holding your product is worth 10 written testimonials.

4 — Request a video if you can. A 30-second video testimonial is the strongest asset in your marketing. If they are happy with the product, they will usually say yes.

5 — Organise and display. Keep a folder (digital or physical) of every testimonial and photo. Post them on your WhatsApp status, your Facebook page, and any place customers see you. Add a "Said by happy customers" section to your product listings.

The testimonial rule: Collect testimonials while the customer is still happy — the moment the transaction finishes. Waiting a day loses half of them. Waiting a week loses 90%.
Practical exercise 5.6: After every one of your first 10 sales, ask for a testimonial. Ask for a photo. Ask for a video if the customer is comfortable. Collect at least 5 testimonials in month one.
