Module 3

Walling, Roof and Thermal Mass

The mushroom house is a thermal battery. Brick walls absorb heat by day and release it at night, smoothing the 24-hour temperature swing to within the ±2 °C the fungus needs. Get the walls wrong and no amount of air conditioning saves the cycle.

Module 3 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Choose the correct wall thickness for your climate and species
  • Build walls that maximise thermal mass without costing a fortune
  • Insulate the ceiling correctly to stop heat loss and condensation
  • Design the roof for shading and rain protection

Lesson 3.1

Why thermal mass is everything

A mushroom house is not insulated like a cold room. It is designed around thermal mass. The wall itself is the temperature control device.

Consider what happens over a 24-hour period in a mushroom house with a light insulated wall:

TimeOutside tempInside (light insulated)Inside (230 mm brick)
6 a.m.15 °C17 °C20 °C
12 noon30 °C26 °C22 °C
3 p.m.32 °C28 °C23 °C
9 p.m.20 °C21 °C21 °C
Midnight16 °C18 °C20 °C
Max swing—11 °C3 °C

The light-wall house swings 11 °C over the day. Oyster mushrooms stall when the swing exceeds 5 °C. The brick house swings 3 °C — within the tolerance of most Pleurotus species.

The rule: a mushroom house with thin or lightweight walls fails, no matter how much equipment is installed. The mass of the wall is the primary environmental control. Everything else is secondary.

What a cavity wall does

A cavity wall is two brick skins with a 50 mm air gap between them. The air gap stops conducted heat. The two brick skins provide twice the thermal mass. The combination gives the mushroom house stable temperature AND some insulation — the best of both.

A cavity wall costs roughly 60–80% more material than a single skin. In very hot or cold climates it pays for itself in reduced heating/cooling cost within 2–3 years.

What a single brick wall does

Acceptable in mild climates. In hot climates it needs a shading strategy — afternoon sun on a single-skin brick wall drives heat inside by mid-afternoon.

Lesson 3.3

Walling construction

Brick and mortar

  • Fired clay brick — best. Consistent, water-resistant, food-safe, high thermal mass.
  • Concrete block — acceptable if solid, or if hollow cores are filled.
  • Stabilised soil block — acceptable in dry climates. Needs heavy exterior render in wet climates.
  • Mortar: 1:4 cement-to-sand for the walling, thicker joint (12 mm) than normal to increase thermal mass slightly.

Interior finish

The interior of the mushroom house must be:

  • Smooth — no crevices, no ledges where spores collect and contaminate.
  • Cleanable — render and food-grade paint, or tile.
  • Food-safe — a paint that is rated for food premises, cured and aired before use.
  • Resistant to moisture — mushrooms produce high humidity. Plain brick absorbs water. Render + food-grade paint is standard.

Do not use untreated bare brick inside the grow room. It absorbs moisture, grows mould, and cannot be cleaned between crops.

Insulated ceiling — mandatory

The ceiling is insulated, not the walls. Heat rises. A well-insulated ceiling is the single most effective barrier to heat loss and condensation.

  • 50–100 mm foam board (polyurethane or XPS), between ceiling boards.
  • Vapour barrier on the warm side of the insulation (see Cold Room course Module 3).
  • Washable ceiling surface — insulated panel with a food-safe face, or plastered and painted.
  • No condensation drip — the ceiling is the surface where condensation forms. Insulation + ventilation prevents it.

Lesson 3.4

The roof

Design principles

  • Overhang 600–800 mm — more than a normal building. This is the primary defence against afternoon sun on the walls and rain on the walls.
  • Light colour — reflects solar heat.
  • Ventilated attic space — a gap between the roof and the ceiling that lets hot air escape.
  • Metal covering — galvanised or aluminium sheet, standard for durability.
  • Slope — 15–20° for rain runoff in tropical downpours.

Roof structure

  • Timber trusses: simple A-frame or lean-to. Timber must be treated or termite-resistant.
  • Purloins: horizontal timbers for the metal sheet fixing.
  • Insulation: the roof does not insulate directly — the ceiling does. Leave a ventilated air gap between roof and ceiling.
  • Ridge vents: a small screened gap at the ridge lets hot air escape the attic, keeping the ceiling cool.

Solar gain control

  • White roof — reflects 70–80% of solar radiation.
  • Shade trees — deciduous trees on the equator side, planted 3–4 m from the building. Trees shade in summer, drop leaves in winter.
  • Insulated ceiling — the final barrier before heat reaches the grow room.

Lesson 3.5

The three walling mistakes

1. Thin walls

Using a 100 mm or lightweight block wall in a hot climate. The temperature swing kills the fruiting cycle. Fix: build thick or add external insulation. This is the mistake that requires demolition to fix.

2. Uninsulated ceiling

Skipping the ceiling insulation to save money. The grow room becomes a solar oven in the afternoon. Fix: retrofit foam board under the ceiling, or accept high air-conditioning bills forever.

3. Bare brick interior

Leaving the interior unfinished. The brick absorbs water, grows mould, contaminates every crop. Fix: render and food-grade paint before the first crop. Cannot be fixed after the first contamination without full strip-and-repaint.

The walling rule: thick walls, insulated ceiling, painted interior. All three. A mushroom house that fails any one will produce less than half its potential, and once contaminated, harder to recover than the extra cost of building it right the first time.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. What is the primary environmental control in a mushroom house?

Correct: wall mass. A brick wall absorbs heat by day and releases it at night, holding the interior temperature stable.

2. What is the minimum wall thickness for a mushroom house in most of Africa?

Correct: 230 mm brick. Single-skin brick. In hot or cold climates, a 350 mm cavity wall is better.

3. Where does the insulation go in a mushroom house?

Correct: ceiling. Heat rises. An insulated ceiling stops heat loss and prevents condensation drip on the crop.

4. Why must the interior of the grow room be rendered and painted?

Correct: moisture and contamination. A smooth, food-grade surface can be cleaned. Bare brick cannot, and it stays damp.

5. How much roof overhang should a mushroom house have?

Correct: 600–800 mm. Primary defence against afternoon sun on the walls and rain ingress.

Practical assignment — Module 3

Produce a walling and roof specification for your mushroom house.

  1. Wall thickness chosen and why (single skin, cavity wall).
  2. Wall material (fired brick, block, stabilised block) and mortar mix.
  3. Interior finish — render material, food-grade paint product, curing period before first crop.
  4. Ceiling construction — insulation type and thickness, vapour barrier placement, washable surface.
  5. Roof design — material, slope, overhang, ventilation gap, ridge vent.
  6. Solar gain control — roof colour, shade trees, other measures.
  7. Site drainage around the walls and roof runoff management.
  8. Two supplier quotations for the walling and ceiling materials.
  9. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest thermal or moisture risk in your build and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 1 and 9. Wall thickness is the primary environmental decision; the paragraph is where you commit to a thermal strategy.
