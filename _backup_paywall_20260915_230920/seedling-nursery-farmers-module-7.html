7. Selling to Farmers

You have grown the seedlings. Now you need to fill your order book every season, year after year, with farmers who trust you. This module covers finding farmers, marketing, contracts, and scaling the nursery into a real business.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 7.1 — Understanding the farming calendar

African farming follows seasons. Sell in the wrong season and no one is buying. Sell in the right season and you can fill your order book for the whole year.

RegionMain planting seasonSecond season
Southern Africa (Zim, Zam, Malawi, SA)Nov–Dec (summer rains)Feb–Mar (autumn)
East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda)Mar–May (long rains)Oct–Dec (short rains)
West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire)Apr–Jun (main rains)Sep–Oct (short rains)
Horn of Africa (Ethiopia, Somalia)Jun–Aug (Kiremt)Feb–Apr (Belg)

When farmers buy seedlings:

  • 2–6 weeks before planting. Most farmers decide to buy at the last minute. Some plan months ahead.
  • You should sow: 4–8 weeks before the planting season to have seedlings ready exactly when they need them.
  • Follow the rains. First rains trigger planting activity. Your production must line up.

Planning your year:

  • Jan–Feb: Prepare, source seed, market to farmers for first season.
  • Mar–May: Peak production and delivery (East Africa main season).
  • Jun–Aug: Second wave of production for autumn/winter crops.
  • Sep–Nov: Short rains season — production for the second planting.
  • Dec: Review year, plan next.
The calendar rule: You cannot sell to farmers out of season. Understand the exact calendar of your region. Plan your nursery production backward from when farmers actually plant.
Practical exercise 7.1: Ask 5 local farmers their planting months. Ask when they start looking for seedlings. Build your regional calendar from these answers.

Lesson 7.2 — Farmer field days and demonstrations

Field days are the #1 way African nurseries find customers. Farmers visit, see your operation, and buy.

How to run a successful field day:

  1. Pick a date 2–4 weeks before the planting season. When farmers are actively planning.
  2. Invite 30–50 farmers. Use local radio, WhatsApp groups, church announcements, extension officers.
  3. Set up your nursery for viewing. Clean benches, healthy seedlings, clear signage.
  4. Demonstrate. Show the whole process — sowing, transplanting, hardening off.
  5. Offer tasting or samples. Where applicable — e.g., tomato variety tasting.
  6. Teach something useful. A short talk on transplanting techniques. Farmers appreciate free knowledge.
  7. Have pricing ready and a clear offer. "Order today for delivery in April at $X."
  8. Give a discount for orders placed on the day. 10% off if ordered at the field day.
  9. Collect contacts. Name, crop, quantity needed, phone number.

What to serve: tea, snacks. Cheap but the gesture is remembered.

The field day rule: One field day can generate 30+ new customers. Compare that to spending $200 on radio ads that reach no one specific. Field days cost less and work far better.
Practical exercise 7.2: Plan a field day for next season. Pick a date, invite 30 farmers, prepare a 20-minute demonstration. Run it, log results, learn.

Lesson 7.3 — WhatsApp groups and farmer networks

WhatsApp is where African farmers do business.

How to use it:

  • Join local farmer groups. Ask extension officers, agricultural supply shops, and farmers you know to add you to their groups.
  • Be helpful, not spammy. Answer questions. Share tips. Do not sell in every message.
  • Post seedling photos regularly. Show your nursery. Let people see progress.
  • Announce seedling availability. "Tomato seedlings ready for sale in 2 weeks. Order now."
  • Use WhatsApp Business. Free business profile, catalogue of products, quick replies.
  • Broadcast lists. Send updates to farmers who opted in. Do not spam those who did not.

Farmer networks:

  • Farmer cooperatives. Meet leaders, present your seedling offer at their meetings.
  • Extension officers. They know every farmer in the area. Build a relationship. Give them a small commission on referrals.
  • Agro-dealers. They sell seed, fertiliser, and tools. Ask if you can leave seedling brochures at their shops, or pay them 5% to refer farmers to you.
  • Church and community groups. Often organise group buying. Present your offer.
  • Agricultural shows and farmer fairs. Set up a stall. Hand out brochures. Collect contacts.
Practical exercise 7.3: Join 3 farmer WhatsApp groups this week. Post one useful tip in each. Wait a week, then announce your seedling offer. Track who responds.

Lesson 7.4 — Contract growing and advance orders

The most stable nurseries are built on advance orders and contracts. You produce exactly what is sold before the season starts.

Order typeWhen placedAdvantage
Spot order (same day)Day of saleFast but unreliable
Advance order (2–4 weeks)Before productionPlanned production, no waste
Contract (whole season)Months aheadGuaranteed revenue, big volumes
Long-term agreementYearlyRepeat business, price stability

How to build a contract system:

  1. Meet with the farmer 2–3 months before planting season. Understand their plans.
  2. Agree on crop, variety, quantity, delivery date, and price.
  3. Sign a simple one-page agreement. Date, parties, quantity, price, delivery, payment terms.
  4. Collect 30–50% deposit. This funds the seed and materials.
  5. Deliver on the agreed date. Collect the remaining balance.
  6. Follow up after planting. Ask how the crop performed.
  7. Plan the next season together. Repeat the process.

What goes in the agreement:

  • Farmer name and contact
  • Nursery name and contact
  • Crop and variety
  • Quantity
  • Price per seedling and total price
  • Deposit amount and payment terms
  • Delivery date and location
  • Quality standards (height, age, hardening)
  • What happens if seedlings fail — replacement or refund
The deposit rule: Never start a large batch of seedlings without a deposit. A farmer who will not pay a 30% deposit is not a committed customer. If they cancel, you lose your production cost. The deposit protects you.
Practical exercise 7.4: Write a simple one-page contract template. Use it for your first advance order. Adjust after each contract based on what worked and what did not.

Lesson 7.5 — Selling to farmer groups and cooperatives

Farmer groups and cooperatives buy in bulk. One contract with a cooperative can cover your entire season.

How to approach them:

  1. Identify active groups in your region. Ask extension officers, agricultural offices, and other farmers.
  2. Meet with the group's leadership. Chairperson, secretary, treasurer. Not just one member.
  3. Present your seedling offer. Bring samples. Explain quality, pricing, delivery, and terms.
  4. Offer group pricing. 10–20% below retail. Payment terms flexible.
  5. Sign a group agreement. One contract, one deposit, one delivery. Simpler than 30 individual sales.
  6. Deliver to the group. They distribute to members.

Cooperative benefits:

  • Large volumes in one transaction
  • Lower marketing cost
  • Reliable repeat business
  • Group reputation rubs off on you

Cooperative challenges:

  • Slower decision-making (committee approval)
  • Sometimes payment is delayed
  • Quality expectations are high — cooperatives often have strict standards
Practical exercise 7.5: Identify 2 cooperatives or farmer groups in your region. Request a meeting. Present your nursery. One contract is worth 20 individual sales.

Lesson 7.6 — Scaling the seedling nursery

Once your nursery is stable and profitable, three growth paths:

PathInvestmentTime to payoffRisk
Add crops (herbs, ornamentals, tree varieties)$200–$8001 seasonLow
Add grafting (mango, avocado, citrus)$300–$1,5006–18 monthsMedium
Add a second site$3,000–$10,0001–2 yearsMedium-high
Add training / consultancy$0–$200ImmediateVery low
Add tissue culture (long term)$20,000+3–5 yearsHigh

The safest growth path:

  1. Year 1: Master 2 vegetable crops. Learn production, quality, delivery.
  2. Year 2: Add 2–3 more crops. Build a base of 10+ commercial farmers.
  3. Year 3: Add grafting for fruit trees. Higher-value, longer-cycle products.
  4. Year 4+: Consider training other nursery operators, or a second site.
The training opportunity: Once you have 3+ years of experience, training new nursery operators becomes very profitable. You sell knowledge, not seedlings. No land, no materials — pure margin.
Practical exercise 7.6 — Capstone: Write your one-page seedling nursery business plan. Current crops, target customers, next season's production plan, expected revenue, and one growth goal per quarter. Post it in your nursery. Review every season.
