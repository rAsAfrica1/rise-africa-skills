Method 1 — Solar and Air Drying Sheds

Drying is the oldest food preservation method in the world and still the most widely used. It is also the one most often done badly — on a mat in the open, at the mercy of rain, animals and dust. This module builds a proper drying shed.

Lesson 2.1

The physics of drying — briefly

Water leaves food when the air around it can hold more water vapour than the food surface. Two things make that happen fast:

  1. Warm air holds more water. At 20 °C, air can hold about 17 g of water per cubic metre. At 40 °C, it can hold about 51 g. Warming air triples its water-carrying capacity.
  2. Moving air carries moisture away. Still air becomes saturated and stops drying. Moving air constantly replaces saturated air with fresh dry air.

The combination — warm plus moving — is the entire basis of every drying method. A solar drying shed does two jobs: it heats air, and it moves air.

How fast food dries depends on

  • Air temperature — higher is faster, up to a limit that varies by product
  • Relative humidity — lower is faster
  • Air speed — 1–2 m/s is ideal; too fast blows product around, too slow lets it sweat
  • Surface area — sliced product dries faster than whole
  • Product moisture — wetter product takes longer
The drying rule: the shed does not do the drying. The air does. The shed makes the air warm, dry and moving. Everything else is engineering around that.

For most smallholders, the cabinet solar dryer is the correct choice. It is simple, cheap, effective, and can be built locally from timber, glazing (glass or polycarbonate) and mesh. Tunnel dryers are better for a processing enterprise at 100+ kg per day.

Lesson 2.3

Building a cabinet solar dryer

A basic cabinet dryer is a box with a glazed lid, a black interior, and ventilation holes at the bottom and top. Air enters cool at the bottom, is warmed by the glazing and the black interior, passes over the product, and exits moist at the top. Simple and effective.

Dimensions for a small commercial unit

ElementDimension
Cabinet interior width600 mm
Cabinet interior length1,200 mm
Cabinet interior depth600 mm
Number of shelves4–6, spaced 100–150 mm apart
Inlet vents (bottom, both sides)2 × 200 × 50 mm, screened
Outlet vents (top, both sides or rear)2 × 200 × 50 mm, screened
Glazed lid slope15° toward the equator side for maximum sun

Materials

  • Timber frame: 50 × 50 mm timber for the frame, food-safe treatment only on the exterior
  • Cabinet walls: 12 mm plywood on exterior; interior lined with matte black metal or painted matte black for heat absorption
  • Glazing: 4 mm glass or 4–6 mm polycarbonate sheet. Polycarbonate is lighter and does not shatter, but scratches and loses clarity over time. Glass is cheaper and clearer.
  • Shelves: food-safe mesh (stainless or aluminium) on a timber frame
  • Vent screens: insect mesh, 1 mm aperture

Assembly steps

  1. Build the cabinet base and walls. Line the interior with black material.
  2. Fit the shelves, ensuring they are removable for cleaning.
  3. Install the inlet vents at the lowest point of the two side walls.
  4. Install the outlet vents at the highest point of the opposite side wall (or in the roof).
  5. Fit the glazed lid on a sloped frame.
  6. Add a small chimney (200 mm tall, screened) on the outlet side to boost airflow.
  7. Cure the timber and any paint for at least 7 days before use.
Test before first use: put a thermometer inside the cabinet on a sunny day and record the temperature every hour. The interior should reach 45–60 °C on a bright day. If it stays below 40 °C, the vents are too large or the glazing is not tight enough.

Lesson 2.4

Sizing for a real harvest

Drying capacity depends on the product, the shelf area, and the ambient conditions. As a rule of thumb:

  • 1 m² of shelf area holds 4–8 kg of fresh product (sliced 3–5 mm thick)
  • Fresh-to-dried ratio: typically 5–10 kg fresh → 1 kg dried, depending on product
  • Drying time in a solar cabinet: 1–3 days for most vegetables and fruit, up to 5 days in humid weather

Example: tomato drying

You want to dry 100 kg of fresh tomato per week. Tomato slices 5 mm thick, 8 kg fresh per m² of shelf. Drying time 2 days. Fresh-to-dried ratio 8:1.

  • Shelf area needed at any moment: 100 kg ÷ 8 kg/m² = 12.5 m²
  • If each cabinet has 4 shelves of 600 × 1,200 mm = 2.88 m² per cabinet
  • Cabinets needed: 12.5 ÷ 2.88 ≈ 4.3, round to 5 cabinets
  • Dry output per week: 100 kg ÷ 8 = 12.5 kg of dried tomato

The same calculation applies to any product, with two substitutions: shelf loading (kg/m²) and fresh-to-dried ratio.

Lesson 2.5

Quality control during drying

Colour and appearance

Fast drying preserves colour. Slow drying causes browning, especially in fruit. To keep colour: dry within 24–48 hours, keep the temperature below the browning threshold (65 °C for most fruit), and use an ascorbic acid dip (1 g/L) before drying to preserve vitamin C and colour.

Sulphiting — and why this course does not recommend it

Many commercial dryers use sulphur dioxide to preserve colour. Sulphites cause asthma in some people and are regulated. For a small African processor, it is easier and safer to skip sulphites and accept slightly darker colour.

Case hardening

If the cabinet is too hot at the start, the surface of the product dries too fast and seals, trapping moisture inside. This is called case hardening. Prevention: start with a moderate temperature (40 °C) and raise it gradually.

Target final moisture by product

ProductTarget moistureTest
Vegetables8–10%Leathery texture, no free water when squeezed
Fruit15–20%Pliable, not sticky
Meat (biltong / jerky)Under 20%Firm but bendable
Fish15–20%Firm flesh, no moisture at bone
HerbsUnder 10%Crumble in hand

Packaging

Dried product must be packed airtight, out of light, in a cool place. Moisture-proof packaging (laminated foil, food-grade plastic jars) protects against re-absorption of moisture from the air. Never pack warm product — let it cool to room temperature first.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. What two things does a solar drying shed do?

Correct: warm and move. Warm air holds more moisture; moving air carries moisture away. Both are needed.

2. What is the correct type of dryer for a smallholder starting out?

Correct: cabinet solar dryer. Cheap, effective, protects from rain and insects, 2–4× faster than open sun.

3. What is case hardening?

Correct: surface sealing. Start at 40 °C, raise gradually. Never start at high heat.

4. What is the typical fresh-to-dried ratio for vegetables?

Correct: 5–10:1. Most of the fresh weight is water that must be removed.

5. Why does this course not recommend sulphiting?

Correct: health and regulation. Simpler to accept slightly darker colour than to manage sulphite regulation and consumer risk.

  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest quality risk in your drying operation (browning, insects, re-wetting, case hardening) and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 4 and 8. The sizing arithmetic and the quality risk paragraph carry the real weight.
