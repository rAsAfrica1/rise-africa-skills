Module 12 — Preservation Business: Packaging, Storage and Selling

Turning preserved food into a business — costing, packaging, storage, labelling and customers.

Home › Preservation Buildings — 10 Methods › Module 12

Learning outcomes

  • Calculate the cost per unit and the margin for every product.
  • Choose packaging that protects the product and sells it.
  • Set storage conditions and shelf life for each product type.
  • Write labels that comply with regulation.
  • Build traceability and recall systems into a small food business.
  • Choose and manage sales channels — direct, retail, wholesale, online.
  • Keep records that show what works.

1. Costing — the discipline that decides survival

Most small food businesses fail because they price from hope, not from arithmetic. Cost every product fully before you sell it.

Cost lineIncludeCommonly forgotten
Raw materialMain ingredient, at yield weight after trimming, not purchase weightLosses during prep — 20–40 % on fruit and vegetables
Secondary ingredientsSugar, salt, acid, pectin, spices, curing saltSmall amounts that add up
PackagingJar, lid, label, cartonCarton and box; label printing setup
Fuel and energyGas, electricity, wood for the smokehouseHeat-up time, not just production time
WaterWashing, cleaning, process waterWastewater disposal if charged
LabourYour time at a real rate — not zeroPrep, cooking, filling, labelling, cleaning, delivery
OverheadsBuilding share, insurance, licences, testingTesting, once per recipe, per market
DeliveryVehicle, fuel, ice, cold boxesYour time to deliver
Waste and rejection5–10 % of total cost in a real businessSpoiled batches, jar breakages, returns

Worked costing — 500 g jar of mango jam

Mango pulp: 0.35 kg × 2.50/kg = 0.88
Sugar: 0.35 kg × 1.20/kg = 0.42
Pectin, lime juice: 0.12
Jar + lid: 0.55
Label: 0.18
Carton share: 0.05
Gas and electricity: 0.20
Labour (10 min at 6/hr): 1.00
Overheads (building, insurance, licences): 0.30
Delivery share: 0.25
Rejection allowance (5 %): 0.20
Total cost per jar = 4.15

Target margin 45 % of selling price:
Price = 4.15 / (1 − 0.45) = 7.55
Round to 7.50 retail.

At 7.50, margin = (7.50 − 4.15) / 7.50 = 45 %.

For wholesale, sell at 60 % of retail = 4.50. That is barely above cost — so wholesale only makes sense at higher volume with lower labour minutes per jar.

Packaging is a sales tool as well as protection

For a small producer, shelf presence is often what wins the sale. A clear glass jar with a clean, legible label outsells a cheaper plastic tub with a printed sticker every time. Budget 15–20 % of your cost for packaging and treat it as an investment, not an expense.

Storage rooms

  • Dry store — for jars, packaging, dry ingredients. Cool, dry, dark, pest-proof. Shelving 200 mm off the floor.
  • Chilled store — for finished perishable product. 0–4 °C. Separate from raw materials.
  • Freezer store — −18 °C for long-term. Labelled and rotated.
  • Cellar / cool store — for roots, onions, some ferments. Ventilated. See Module 9.
  • All stores: pest-proof, labelled, FIFO (first in, first out).

4. Labelling and regulation

Preserved food sold to the public is regulated in every market. Label requirements vary by country, but the core is similar everywhere.

FieldRequired in most marketsNotes
Product nameYesMust not be misleading — "jam" has a legal definition of fruit and sugar content in many countries
Ingredients listYesDescending order by weight at the time of mixing
Net weightYesMetric units; correct font size for the pack size
Business name and addressYesThe address must be a real, contactable one
Batch / lot codeYesEnables recall; must be in your records too
Best-before or use-byYesUse-by for safety (chilled); best-before for quality (ambient)

Country of originIn many marketsCheck local rules
AllergensYesHighlight in bold if the ingredient appears in the list
NutritionRequired in some marketsUsually waived for small producers — confirm locally

Do not use a "best-before" you have not verified

A best-before date is a claim. If your product spoils before that date, you have sold a faulty product. Test each product over its claimed shelf life — hold a sample from each batch at the storage temperature and open it at the end of the claimed period. If it is not right, shorten the date.

5. Traceability and recall

Every unit sold must be traceable back to its batch, and every batch must be traceable forward to its customers. This is what makes a recall possible.

  1. Batch code on every unit: date + product + sequence. Example: 250512-JAM-01.
  2. Batch sheet for each production run: raw material source, quantities, process parameters (pH, °Brix, temperature), yield, jars filled, jar codes issued.
  3. Sales record: batch code, customer, quantity, date.
  4. Retained sample: one unit of each batch held for the full shelf life at storage temperature.
  5. Recall procedure: identify the batch, list customers, phone them within 24 h, recover product, investigate, write up.
  6. Practice the recall once a year. How long does it take you to list every customer who received a batch from last month?

Do not chase volume at the cost of margin

Wholesale looks attractive because it moves product. But if your cost per jar is 4.15 and you sell to a distributor at 4.50, you are making 0.35 per jar and will lose money the first time anything goes wrong. Only enter wholesale when your cost per unit has fallen — through volume purchasing, better labour productivity, or a simpler product range.

9. Closing — course review

You have now covered ten preservation methods — drying, salting, sugar, fermenting, cooling, freezing, smoking, root cellars, oil-salt-brine and combinations — and the buildings and controls that make each one work.

The next steps in your operation are usually one of:

  • Focus on two products. Most successful small food businesses sell two or three products very well, not ten adequately.
  • Build the retail brand. Labels, story, consistent packaging, a stall or shop.
  • Scale the building, not the range. A larger drying room or a bigger smokehouse is more profitable than adding products.
  • Train your team. Every process in this course is a job someone else can do if the records are clear.

The habit that matters most

Weigh, measure and write it down. Every batch. Every product. Every time. Your recipe, your cost, your batch code and your yield are the only things that tell you what is really happening in your business.

Assignment 12 — Final assignment for the course

  1. Choose your two main products. Fully cost one unit of each, with every line item.
  2. Set a retail price and a wholesale price for each. State the margin at each.
  3. Choose your packaging for each product. State the material, size, and why it suits the product.
  4. State the storage temperature, humidity and shelf life for each product.
  5. Write a compliant label for each product.
  6. Design a batch sheet with at least 12 fields, and fill in one example.
  7. Write your recall procedure in 6 steps or fewer, with named responsibility.
  8. Choose your sales channel(s) and state why. State the price per unit in each channel.
  9. Write a single paragraph: the one habit that, if you keep it for a year, will most improve your preservation business. Explain why.
