2. Crops with the Best Returns

One hectare can grow 50 different vegetables. But only about 10 are worth your time in any given market. This module shows you how to identify the highest-return crops for your area, and why some "popular" crops quietly lose money.

Module 2 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — Tomato — the king of vegetable crops

Tomato is the highest-value vegetable crop for African smallholders. A well-managed hectare can produce 40–70 tonnes per year across 2–3 crops. At $0.80–$2.00/kg retail, that is $32,000–$140,000 gross revenue per hectare per year.

MetricValue
Days to first harvest75–90 from transplant
Yield per hectare (good management)40–70 tonnes/year
Water need at peak50,000–60,000 L/ha/day
Seedling cost per hectare$250–$600 (25,000–40,000 seedlings)
Fertiliser cost per hectare$400–$900
Pesticide/fungicide cost$200–$600
Labour cost per hectare$600–$1,200
Total input cost$1,450–$3,300
Typical gross revenue$32,000–$140,000
Net margin60–75%

Best tomato varieties for African markets:

  • Roma VF — determinate, thick skin, good for transport, disease-resistant
  • Moneymaker — reliable, widely adapted, good flavour
  • Anna F1 — hybrid, high yield, uniform fruit
  • Eva F1 — hybrid, excellent for fresh market
  • Rio Grande — processing variety, paste and canning

Why tomato fails for beginners:

  • Water stress at flowering causes flower drop and zero fruit. Peak water need is exactly when the plant looks strongest.
  • Blight and bacterial wilt kill plants within days. Weekly preventive sprays are mandatory in humid areas.
  • Poor staking leads to fruit touching the ground, rot, and 30%+ losses.
  • No market contract — 40 tonnes dumped on a saturated local market crashes the price to $0.20/kg.
The tomato rule: Never plant more than 0.3 hectares of tomato without a confirmed buyer (market trader, restaurant, processor) who has agreed a price range. Grow the rest as backup crops.
Practical exercise 2.1: Talk to 3 tomato traders at your market. Ask: what variety sells best? What price do you pay in peak season? When do you have too much? Write the answers.

Lesson 2.2 — Onion — the store-able staple

Onions are the best vegetable for a beginner with land but limited market access, because they store for 3–6 months. You can harvest and sell as the price rises.

MetricValue
Days to harvest120–150 from transplant
Yield per hectare25–40 tonnes
Storage life3–6 months (proper drying and curing)
Water need30,000–40,000 L/ha/day
Cost of production per hectare$1,200–$2,500
Revenue at $0.70/kg$17,500–$28,000
Net margin50–65%

Best varieties:

  • Red Creole — best storage life, popular in East Africa
  • Texas Grano 502 — sweet, mild, popular in West Africa
  • Bombay Red — popular across Africa, good shelf life

Critical onion-specific rules:

  1. Long-day vs short-day. Grow the correct type for your latitude. Equatorial regions (Kenya, Uganda) use short-day. Southern Africa uses long-day.
  2. Cure properly. After harvest, dry in the field 7–14 days until the tops are completely brown. Then cure in a shaded, ventilated store for another 2–3 weeks.
  3. Never irrigate within 2 weeks of harvest. Wet onions rot in storage.
Practical exercise 2.2: Check the variety day-length requirement against your latitude. Ask a local extension officer which onion variety works best in your area.

Lesson 2.3 — Cabbage and leafy brassicas

Cabbage, kale, rape, and broccoli are reliable, cheap to grow, and have strong demand year-round in most African markets.

CropDaysYield/haPrice/kgDifficulty
Cabbage75–10040–70 tonnes$0.30–$0.80Easy
Kale (sukuma wiki)60–9025–40 tonnes$0.40–$1.00Very easy
Rape60–7530–50 tonnes$0.40–$0.90Easy
Broccoli80–10015–25 tonnes$1.50–$3.00Medium

Why these work for beginners:

  • Low water need compared to tomato — 25,000–35,000 L/ha/day
  • Fewer pests than tomato — mainly aphids, caterpillars
  • Reliable yield — very forgiving of imperfect management
  • Weekly harvest for kale and rape — continuous cash flow
  • Local demand is guaranteed — every market sells them

Cabbage spacing:

  • Large heads: 60 × 60 cm (28,000 plants/ha)
  • Medium heads: 45 × 45 cm (49,000 plants/ha)
  • Kale/rape: 30 × 40 cm (83,000 plants/ha)

Kale is the perfect "quick cash" crop — plant it, harvest weekly for 6+ months, and it keeps producing while the slower crops mature.

Practical exercise 2.3: Plant 500 m² of kale this week. It gives you a weekly harvest and immediate cash flow while you plan the rest of the hectare.

Lesson 2.4 — Carrot, beetroot, and root crops

Root crops have one advantage over every other vegetable: they store well, need less water, and are cheap to grow.

CropDaysYield/haWater needStorage
Carrot90–12020–35 tonnes25,000 L/day3–4 months
Beetroot75–10020–30 tonnes25,000 L/day2–3 months
Radish25–3515–25 tonnes20,000 L/day2–4 weeks
Turnip50–7025–40 tonnes22,000 L/day2–3 months

Soil requirements for root crops:

  • Deep, loose, stone-free soil — roots fork or split otherwise
  • pH 6.0–7.0 — acidic soil makes roots stubby
  • Fresh manure before planting = split roots. Use composted manure from the previous season instead.

The two rules that make carrot growing profitable:

  1. Thin aggressively. Carrots must be thinned to 3–5 cm apart at 3 weeks old. Many beginners skip this — the result is tiny, thin carrots with zero market value.
  2. Consistent moisture. Alternating wet-dry causes splitting. Keep soil evenly damp throughout.
The carrot rule: Carrot seed must be sown directly into the final bed — transplanting produces forked, unsellable roots. Never raise carrots in trays.
Practical exercise 2.4: Plant 200 m² of carrot this week. Return to thin them at 3 weeks exactly. Compare yield to a control plot you leave unthinned.

Lesson 2.5 — Green pepper and hot pepper

Peppers are the highest-value-per-kg vegetable after specialty herbs. A hectare can generate $15,000–$35,000 per year.

Pepper typeDaysYield/haPrice/kg
Green bell pepper90–12015–25 tonnes$0.80–$2.00
Red/yellow bell pepper110–14012–20 tonnes$1.50–$3.50
Hot chili (bird's eye)90–1208–15 tonnes dry$2.00–$5.00 (dry)
Scotch bonnet100–13010–18 tonnes$1.20–$3.00

Pepper-specific rules:

  • Never water the leaves. Peppers are highly susceptible to bacterial and fungal leaf diseases. Water at the base only.
  • Calcium is critical. Deficiency causes blossom-end rot — black sunken spots on the fruit. Add 200 kg/ha agricultural lime before planting.
  • Stake tall varieties. Fruit-laden plants fall over without support.
  • Harvest green or red. Green sells fast at lower price; red/yellow sells for 2× to high-end buyers.
Practical exercise 2.5: Test your soil pH. If below 6.0, add agricultural lime at 200 kg/ha this season. Plant 500 m² of green pepper.

Lesson 2.6 — Herbs — the highest margin crop per square metre

Herbs are the single highest-margin crop for a small plot. One square metre of basil can earn $15–$40 per year. One square metre of tomato earns $3–$8.

HerbPrice per kgYield per m²Best buyer
Basil$6–$151.5–3 kg/yearRestaurants, hotels
Coriander (dhania)$2–$52–4 kg/yearMarkets, restaurants
Mint$4–$82–4 kg/yearRestaurants, tea buyers
Rosemary$8–$201–2 kg/yearRestaurants, dried herb buyers
Thyme$8–$181–2 kg/yearRestaurants, dried herb buyers
Oregano$6–$151.5–3 kg/yearRestaurants, dried herb buyers

Why herbs work on a small plot:

  • High value per square metre — one 100 m² herb plot can earn more than a 1,000 m² tomato plot
  • Low water need — most herbs are drought-tolerant once established
  • Long harvest window — 2–5 years per planting for woody herbs
  • Restaurants pay premium prices and buy consistently

The herb market reality:

  • Fresh herbs need quick delivery to restaurants. Not for distant markets.
  • Dried herbs have a longer shelf life and can ship anywhere. Prices are lower but volume is bigger.
  • Essential oil crops (lemongrass, eucalyptus) need a distillery — a separate business model.
The herb rule: Start with 100 m² of basil and coriander. Sell to 3–5 restaurants within 5 km. If you cannot sell $50/week, do not expand the herb plot.
Practical exercise 2.6: Visit 5 restaurants within 10 km. Ask the chef: which herbs do you buy, in what quantity, at what price, and from whom? One confirmed buyer is worth 500 m² of herbs.
