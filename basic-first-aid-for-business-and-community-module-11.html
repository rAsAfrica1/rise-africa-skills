1. First Aid for Pregnant Women

Pregnant women can face emergencies like bleeding, seizures, or going into labour early. In Africa, many women give birth without a skilled attendant, so knowing basic first aid can save two lives. If a pregnant woman is bleeding from the vagina, lay her down on her left side to improve blood flow to the baby. Do not insert anything into the vagina. Call for help immediately. If she is in a rural area, arrange transport to the nearest clinic or hospital. While waiting, keep her warm and calm. Give her small sips of water if she is conscious. If she has a seizure, protect her from injury by clearing the area and cushioning her head. Do not restrain her. After the seizure, turn her on her left side to keep her airway clear. If she goes into labour and you cannot reach a health facility, prepare for delivery. Wash your hands with soap and clean water. Use clean cloths or plastic sheets. As the baby's head appears, support it gently. Do not pull. After birth, dry the baby, keep it warm, and place it on the mother's chest. Cut the cord with a clean, boiled blade, tying it with clean string. If the mother is bleeding heavily after birth, massage her lower abdomen firmly until the uterus feels hard. This helps stop bleeding. Put the baby to the breast to suckle, which also helps contract the uterus. If bleeding continues, get her to a hospital urgently. For any pregnant woman with severe headache, blurred vision, or swelling of face and hands, suspect pre-eclampsia. Keep her calm and get medical help fast. Remember: never give any medication without medical advice. Your role is to stabilise and transport.

2. First Aid for Older Adults

Older adults often have thinner skin, weaker bones, and chronic conditions like high blood pressure or diabetes. Falls are the most common emergency. If an older person falls, do not rush to lift them. First, check if they are conscious and breathing. Ask what hurts. If they have a head injury, confusion, or cannot move a limb, suspect a fracture or stroke. Keep them still and call for help. If they are bleeding, apply firm pressure with a clean cloth. For a suspected fracture, immobilise the limb with a splint made from a stick or cardboard, padded with cloth. Do not try to straighten the bone. If they are unconscious but breathing, place them in the recovery position on their side to prevent choking. Older adults are also at risk of heat stroke and dehydration. In hot African climates, ensure they drink water regularly. Signs of heat stroke include hot dry skin, confusion, and high body temperature. Move them to shade, remove excess clothing, and cool them with wet cloths and fanning. If they have diabetes, they may suffer from low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia). Signs include shaking, sweating, and confusion. If they can swallow, give them something sweet like sugar water or fruit juice. If they are unconscious, do not give anything by mouth. Place them on their side and call for help. For stroke, remember FAST: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency. Note the time symptoms started. Do not give aspirin unless advised by a doctor. Keep the person calm and monitor breathing. Older adults may also have difficulty breathing due to heart failure or asthma. Let them sit upright and use their prescribed inhaler if available. If breathing stops, begin CPR if trained. Always treat older adults with dignity and patience.

3. First Aid for People with Disabilities

People with disabilities may have specific needs during emergencies. Always ask the person what help they need, if they can communicate. For someone who is deaf or hard of hearing, get their attention by waving or tapping their shoulder gently. Use clear gestures or write notes. If they are injured, show them what you are doing before you touch them. For someone who is blind or has low vision, announce yourself and explain what is happening. Guide them by letting them hold your arm. For someone with a physical disability, do not move them unless they are in danger. If you must move them, ask how they prefer to be lifted. If they use a wheelchair, do not try to lift them out unless necessary. If they have a seizure, protect their head and do not restrain them. After the seizure, turn them on their side if possible. For someone with an intellectual disability, use simple words and be patient. They may not understand what is happening, so reassure them. If they are injured, treat the injury as you would for anyone else. For people with autism, they may be sensitive to touch, noise, or light. Reduce stimulation and speak calmly. If they have a meltdown, give them space and keep them safe. For someone with a communication board or device, use it to help them express pain or needs. In case of evacuation, assist them according to their needs. If they have a service animal, do not separate them unless absolutely necessary. Remember that people with disabilities are experts on their own needs. Respect their autonomy. If they are unconscious, check for medical alert bracelets or cards. These may indicate conditions like epilepsy or diabetes. Always treat them with the same urgency and respect as anyone else.

4. First Aid for Children in Schools and Daycares

Children are curious and active, so injuries are common. In schools and daycares, you must be prepared for choking, bleeding, burns, and head injuries. For choking, if the child is coughing, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot breathe or make sound, give back blows: place them face down on your forearm, supporting the head, and give 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades. If that fails, turn them face up and give 5 chest thrusts with two fingers on the lower half of the breastbone. Repeat until the object comes out or they become unconscious. If unconscious, start CPR. For bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or sterile gauze. If blood soaks through, add more cloth without removing the first. Elevate the wound if possible. For burns, cool the area with clean running water for at least 20 minutes. Do not use ice, butter, or toothpaste. Cover with a clean, non-stick dressing. For head injuries, watch for vomiting, drowsiness, or unequal pupils. If any of these occur, seek medical help immediately. For seizures, clear the area and cushion the head. Do not put anything in the mouth. Time the seizure. If it lasts more than 5 minutes, call for emergency help. For asthma attacks, help the child sit upright and use their inhaler if available. If they don't have one, call for help. For allergic reactions, look for swelling of face or throat, difficulty breathing, or hives. Use an epinephrine auto-injector if prescribed. In daycares, ensure all staff know each child's allergies and medical conditions. Keep a first aid kit stocked with bandages, antiseptic, gloves, and a CPR mask. Always document injuries and inform parents. Remember to stay calm and reassure the child.

5. Mental Health First Aid: Panic Attacks and Emotional Distress

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear, with symptoms like rapid heartbeat, shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, and a feeling of losing control. It can be mistaken for a heart attack. If someone is having a panic attack, stay calm and speak in a soothing voice. Ask them to sit down and take slow, deep breaths. Count with them: breathe in for 4 seconds, hold for 4, breathe out for 6. Reassure them that it will pass. Do not tell them to calm down or that it's all in their head. Move them to a quiet, safe space if possible. If they have a paper bag, do not use itit can be dangerous. Instead, encourage them to breathe normally. After the attack, ask if they have had this before and if they need any help. For emotional distress, such as after a traumatic event, listen without judgment. Let them talk if they want. Do not force them to share. Offer practical help like water, a blanket, or a phone to call a loved one. In African communities, mental health stigma is common, so be discreet and respectful. If someone is expressing suicidal thoughts, take it seriously. Ask directly: 'Are you thinking of killing yourself?' This does not plant the idea. If they say yes, do not leave them alone. Remove any means of harm, like sharp objects or medications. Call a crisis line or take them to a health facility. In many African countries, mental health services are limited, but you can contact a trusted community leader, religious leader, or health worker. For grief, allow them to cry and express anger. Do not rush the process. Your role is to provide comfort and connect them to professional help. Remember, you are not a therapist, but you can be a lifeline.

6. First Aid for Opioid Overdose and Naloxone Use

Opioid overdose happens when someone takes too much of drugs like heroin, tramadol, or prescription painkillers. Signs include slow or stopped breathing, blue lips or fingertips, pinpoint pupils, and unresponsiveness. If you suspect an overdose, call for emergency help immediately. Check if the person is breathing. If not, start CPR. If they are breathing but unresponsive, place them in the recovery position on their side. Naloxone is a life-saving medicine that reverses opioid overdose. In Africa, naloxone may be available at some pharmacies or harm reduction programs. If you have naloxone, give it immediately. For injectable naloxone, draw 0.4 mg into a syringe and inject into the thigh muscle or upper arm. For nasal spray, insert the nozzle into one nostril and press the plunger. If the person does not wake up after 2-3 minutes, give another dose in the other nostril or muscle. Continue rescue breathing if needed. Naloxone works for 30-90 minutes, which may be shorter than the opioid, so the person could overdose again. Stay with them until help arrives. Do not put them in a cold bath or make them drink water. Do not slap them or try to walk them around. After naloxone, they may experience withdrawal symptoms like confusion, agitation, or vomiting. Reassure them. In many African communities, opioid use is rising, especially among youth. If you know someone who uses drugs, encourage them to get help. Keep naloxone in your first aid kit if you work in high-risk areas. Training is available through local health departments or NGOs. Remember, overdose is a medical emergency, not a moral failing. Your quick action can save a life.

<h2>Practical Reference: Special Situations and Vulnerable Populations</h2>

<p>In African workplaces and communities from a market stall in Kumasi to a construction site in Nairobi, from a daycare in Lagos to a family compound in rural Malawi first aid must adapt to the person in front of you. Pregnancy, older age, disability, childhood, mental distress, and opioid overdose each change what you assess, what you do first, and what you must never do. For a business owner or community responder, getting these details right protects lives, reduces liability, and keeps your enterprise and your people functioning. This practical reference gives you the decision points, numbers, and checklists you can act on today.</p>

<h3>Why this module matters for African businesses</h3>
<p>Micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) employ the majority of workers across sub-Saharan Africa, and many operate without an on-site nurse. When a pregnant employee collapses at a tailoring workshop, when an older trader faints in a market, when a child chokes at a daycare attached to a shop, or when a customer shows signs of opioid overdose, the first responder is usually a colleague or owner not a paramedic. Emergency medical services in many African cities have long response times, and in rural areas may be volunteer-based or unavailable. That means the first 510 minutes are yours. This lesson equips you for them.</p>

<h3>1. First aid for pregnant women</h3>
<p>Pregnancy is not an illness, but it changes physiology: greater blood volume, higher oxygen demand, and a uterus that can compress the vena cava when the woman lies flat on her back. In any emergency after 20 weeks of pregnancy, position her on her left side or tilt her 1530 to the left. Never put pressure on the abdomen. If she is bleeding vaginally, having severe headache with blurred vision, convulsing, or complaining of severe abdominal pain, treat it as an obstetric emergency and arrange transport immediately. Eclampsia, antepartum haemorrhage, and sepsis are leading causes of maternal death in Africa, and delays in reaching care are a major contributor.</p>

<h3>2. First aid for older adults</h3>
<p>Older adults in African communities often live with hypertension, diabetes, arthritis, or HIV on antiretroviral therapy. Falls, stroke, hypoglycaemia, and heat exhaustion are common. Handle them gently: the skin is fragile and bones are brittle. Ask about medications and chronic conditions if they are conscious. Do not give food or drink to anyone with altered consciousness stroke and hypoglycaemia can look similar, and aspiration is a real risk. Keep them warm; older adults lose body heat quickly.</p>

<h3>3. First aid for people with disabilities</h3>
<p>Disability is diverse physical, sensory, intellectual, and psychosocial. The first rule is to ask the person how they want to be helped, and to speak directly to them, not only to their companion. Do not move a wheelchair, crutch, or guide dog unless you must for safety. For a deaf person, get their attention with a light touch on the shoulder or by waving in their line of sight. For a blind person, identify yourself and describe what you are about to do. For someone with an intellectual disability, use short, concrete sentences and allow extra time to respond. Respecting autonomy is both ethical and practical it improves cooperation and outcomes.</p>

<h3>4. First aid for children in schools and daycares</h3>
<p>Children are not small adults. Their airways are narrower, their heads proportionally larger, and their bodies lose heat faster. Choking is a leading emergency in daycare settings, often from groundnuts, sweets, or small objects. For a choking infant under 1 year, give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades followed by 5 chest thrusts; for a child over 1 year, give 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts. For CPR, use two fingers for infants, one hand for small children, two hands for older children. Always notify the guardian immediately and document everything this protects both the child and the business.</p>

<h3>5. Mental health first aid: panic attacks and emotional distress</h3>
<p>Panic attacks feel like heart attacks: racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, trembling, fear of dying. The first aider's job is to stay calm, move the person to a quiet space, and help them slow their breathing breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 6. Do not restrain them or tell them to "calm down." For someone in emotional distress after a bereavement, accident, or job loss, listen without judgement, offer water, and ask what would help. If they mention suicide or self-harm, stay with them, remove means if safe to do so, and connect them to a trained counsellor or health facility. In many African countries, mental health services are scarce, so a warm handover to a trusted community health worker or religious leader can be life-saving.</p>

<strong style="color:#c1121f"> SAFETY WARNING Opioid overdose</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0">If you suspect opioid overdose (pinpoint pupils, slow or no breathing, blue lips, unresponsive), call emergency services immediately and give naloxone if available. Naloxone is safe and can be repeated every 23 minutes if breathing does not improve. Do not leave the person alone they may go back into overdose when naloxone wears off. Do not put anything in their mouth or make them vomit. In Africa, opioid misuse is rising with tramadol and heroin use, especially in transit cities like Mombasa, Lagos, and Durban. Carry naloxone where permitted, and know your local poison control or emergency number.</p>

<h3>6. First aid for opioid overdose and naloxone use</h3>
<p>Opioid overdose kills by stopping breathing. Naloxone reverses it within minutes. If the person is not breathing, give rescue breaths one every 56 seconds until naloxone takes effect or help arrives. Naloxone can be given intranasally or by intramuscular injection. After giving it, place the person in the recovery position on their side, keep them warm, and monitor breathing continuously. In African contexts where naloxone may not be stocked, the priority is rescue breathing and rapid transport. Businesses in transport, hospitality, and security sectors should consider whether naloxone is appropriate for their risk profile and legal environment.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scene safety first</strong> check for traffic, fire, violence, or electrical hazards before approaching.</li>
<li><strong>Consent and communication</strong> introduce yourself, ask permission, speak directly to the person.</li>
<li><strong>Primary assessment</strong> airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure (ABCDE).</li>
<li><strong>Pregnant woman</strong> left lateral tilt, no abdominal pressure, escalate early.</li>
<li><strong>Child choking</strong> 5 back blows, then 5 chest or abdominal thrusts depending on age.</li>
<li><strong>Mental distress</strong> calm, quiet space, breathing support, no restraint, refer if suicidal.</li>
<li><strong>Suspected opioid overdose</strong> call for help, give naloxone, rescue breaths, recovery position.</li>
<li><strong>Document and handover</strong> record time, signs, actions, and who took over care.</li>
</ul>
