1. Introduction and Why It Matters

In artisan gold mining, you cannot recover gold that is locked inside rock. Ore preparationcrushing and millingis the first step to liberate gold particles so they can be concentrated or amalgamated. If you skip or rush this stage, you leave gold in the tailings and waste your hard work. In many African artisanal operations, gold occurs as fine particles disseminated in quartz veins or as free flakes in alluvial gravel. Crushing reduces large rocks to small pieces; milling grinds them to powder, increasing surface area for chemical or physical extraction.

Consider this: a 10 kg rock with 5 grams of gold per tonne (5 g/t) contains only 0.05 grams of goldabout the size of a grain of rice. If you crush poorly, that tiny grain may remain trapped in a big chunk and be discarded. Proper crushing and milling can recover over 90% of that gold, while poor preparation might recover less than 50%. That difference is your daily income.

In Africa, most artisanal miners use manual methods: hammers, pestles and mortars, or small jaw crushers. Mechanized mills like ball mills are common in larger operations. The goal is to reduce ore to a consistent sizetypically 100% passing a 2 mm sieve for gravity separation, or finer (75 microns) for cyanidation or amalgamation. The finer the grind, the more gold you expose, but over-grinding wastes energy and creates slimes that hinder recovery.

This module teaches you how to crush and mill ore safely and efficiently, using locally available tools. You will learn to select the right equipment, follow step-by-step methods, avoid common mistakes, and practice a complete ore preparation routine. Remember: good preparation is the foundation of profitable gold recovery.

2. Tools and Materials

To crush and mill ore effectively, you need the right tools. Start with safety gear: heavy leather gloves, safety glasses or goggles, a dust mask (N95 or better), and steel-toed boots. Dust from crushing contains silica, which causes silicosisa deadly lung disease. Never work without a mask.

For manual crushing, you need a sledgehammer (46 kg) and a hardened steel plate or anvil. A flat granite or steel surface works well. For finer crushing, use a pestle and mortar made of hard wood or steel. In many African markets, you can buy a cast-iron mortar and pestle set for about $30$50. For milling, a ball mill is ideal: a cylindrical steel drum that rotates with steel balls inside. Small ball mills (e.g., 1 m diameter, 1.5 m long) are available in mining supply shops in Ghana, Tanzania, and South Africa for $2,000$5,000. Alternatively, a hammer mill or a stamp mill can be used. If mechanized options are too expensive, a manual grinding stone (like a metate) can grind small batches, but it is slow.

You also need screens or sieves. A set of sieves with mesh sizes 10 mm, 5 mm, 2 mm, and 1 mm helps control product size. A 200-mesh (75 micron) sieve is needed for fine milling. For transporting ore, use buckets, wheelbarrows, and shovels. A water source is essential for wet milling to reduce dust and improve grinding. Finally, a scale (at least 1 kg capacity) helps measure feed and product for consistency.

In rural areas, improvise: a car axle and a strong wooden frame can make a simple stamp mill. But always prioritize safety and efficiency. Invest in good toolsthey pay back quickly in recovered gold.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Prepare the site. Choose a flat, stable area away from living quarters. Set up a crushing pada large steel plate or concrete slab. Ensure good ventilation. Wear your safety gear.

Step 2: Sort and clean ore. Remove obvious waste rock, roots, and clay. Clay can be washed off with water. Sort ore by hardnessharder rocks need more crushing.

Step 3: Primary crushing. Use a sledgehammer to break large rocks (up to 30 cm) into pieces smaller than 10 cm. Work on the steel plate. Aim for a pile of consistent size. This takes about 1 hour per 50 kg of ore for one person.

Step 4: Secondary crushing. Use a pestle and mortar or a smaller hammer to reduce pieces to less than 2 cm. You can also use a jaw crusher if available. Feed the crusher slowly to avoid jamming.

Step 5: Milling. If using a ball mill, fill it to 4050% of its volume with ore and steel balls (typically 510 cm diameter). Add water to make a slurry (about 50% solids). Run the mill for 12 hours per 50 kg batch. Check grind size by taking a sample and sievingaim for 80% passing 75 microns for amalgamation. If using a manual grinding stone, grind in small batches (12 kg) for 2030 minutes until fine.

Step 6: Classification. After milling, pour the slurry through a series of screens to separate coarse particles. Return oversize to the mill for further grinding. The fine product goes to the next stage (e.g., sluice box, panning, or amalgamation).

Step 7: Clean up. Wash all equipment and the work area. Gold particles can stick to surfaces. Collect wash water and settle ityou might recover fine gold. Record the weight of ore processed and the time taken to improve your efficiency.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not crushing fine enough. Many miners stop at 23 cm pieces, leaving gold locked in coarse rock. Fix: Crush to at least 2 mm before milling. Use a sieve to checkif 20% is oversize, crush more.

Mistake 2: Over-grinding. Grinding to dust (less than 50 microns) creates slimes that float and are hard to settle, causing gold loss. Fix: Monitor grind size. For gravity separation, 100200 microns is ideal. For amalgamation, 75100 microns. Stop when you reach target size.

Mistake 3: Using dirty equipment. Leftover material from previous batches can contaminate your ore. Fix: Clean hammers, mortars, and mills between batches. Use a brush and water. Do not mix different ore types without cleaning.

Mistake 4: Adding too much or too little water in ball mill. Too little water makes a paste that doesn't grind; too much water reduces impact forces. Fix: Aim for 50% solids by weight. A simple test: the slurry should flow like thick soup.

Mistake 5: Ignoring dust. Dry crushing creates silica dust that harms lungs. Fix: Always wear a mask. Wet the ore slightly before crushing if possible. Work upwind.

Mistake 6: Not checking mill ball charge. Worn or too few balls reduce grinding efficiency. Fix: Replace balls when they wear down to half their original size. Maintain ball charge at 4050% of mill volume.

Mistake 7: Skipping classification. Sending coarse and fine particles together to concentration loses gold. Fix: Always screen after milling. Re-grind oversize.

Mistake 8: Poor record keeping. Without records, you cannot improve. Fix: Keep a log of ore weight, milling time, and gold recovered. Calculate recovery rate (gold recovered / gold in ore) to identify problems.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in ore preparation is non-negotiable. The biggest hazard is silica dust. When you crush dry rock, fine dust fills the air. Inhaling it causes silicosis, an incurable lung disease. Always wear a NIOSH-approved N95 or P100 respirator. If you cannot afford one, at least use a wet cloth over your nose and mouthbut this is not fully protective. Wet crushing reduces dust: sprinkle water on ore before hammering.

Hearing protection is also important. Hammering and ball mills are loud. Use earplugs or earmuffs. Eye protection prevents flying chips from injuring your eyes. Steel-toed boots protect your feet from falling rocks.

Ergonomics matter. Avoid lifting heavy rocks with your back. Use your legs. Take breaks every 30 minutes to stretch. Repetitive hammering can cause tendonitis. Alternate tasks.

Equipment safety: Before using a ball mill, check that all guards are in place. Never put your hands inside a running mill. Ensure electrical connections are safe and grounded. If using a diesel engine, keep it well-ventilated to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning.

Best practices: Work in teams. Have a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and eye wash. Know the location of the nearest clinic. Do not work alone. Keep children and animals away from the work area. Store tools safely.

Environmental best practices: Contain tailings to prevent erosion and water pollution. Do not dump mercury or chemicals into rivers. Use settling ponds for wash water. Reclaim land after mining.

Finally, maintain your equipment. A well-maintained hammer and mill last longer and work better. Lubricate mill bearings regularly. Replace worn parts. This reduces accidents and increases productivity.

6. Practical Exercise

Objective: Crush and mill a 20 kg sample of gold ore to 80% passing 75 microns, then evaluate your recovery potential.

Materials: 20 kg gold ore, sledgehammer, steel plate, pestle and mortar, ball mill (or grinding stone), set of sieves (10 mm, 5 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, 200 mesh), water, buckets, scale, safety gear (gloves, goggles, mask, boots), notebook.

Procedure:
1. Weigh 20 kg of ore. Record weight.
2. Put on safety gear. Work in a well-ventilated area.
3. Primary crushing: Use sledgehammer to break ore to <10 cm. Time this step.
4. Secondary crushing: Use pestle and mortar to reduce to <2 cm. Time this step.
5. Milling: If using ball mill, add ore and water (50% solids) and run for 1 hour. If using grinding stone, grind in 2 kg batches for 20 minutes each. Time total milling.
6. Sieve analysis: Take a 500 g sample of milled product. Sieve through 2 mm, 1 mm, and 200 mesh. Weigh each fraction. Calculate percentage passing 200 mesh. Target: >80%.
7. If <80% passing, mill for another 30 minutes and re-test.
8. Once target reached, process the milled ore using your normal method (e.g., panning, sluice, amalgamation). Weigh gold recovered.
9. Calculate recovery: (gold recovered / gold in ore) x 100. Assume ore grade 5 g/t, so 20 kg contains 0.1 g gold. If you recover 0.08 g, recovery is 80%.

Analysis: Record all times, weights, and observations. Identify bottlenecks. Did crushing take too long? Was milling inefficient? Adjust your method next time.

Safety: Always wear mask during dry crushing. Wet ore before crushing to reduce dust. Do not overfill mill. Keep hands away from moving parts.

Extension: Repeat with different milling times (30, 60, 90 minutes) to find optimal time for your ore type. This exercise teaches you to optimize your own process.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Ore Preparation for Artisan Gold Mining

Ore preparation is the first and most consequential stage of any artisanal gold operation. Before a single gram of gold can be recovered, the host rock must be reduced from solid reef or alluvial gravel into a fine, liberated pulp. In the African artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) context from the gold belts of Tanzania, Ghana, Zimbabwe, Burkina Faso, Mali, Kenya and Uganda the efficiency of crushing and milling directly determines recovery rates, mercury consumption, operating cost and, ultimately, the margin per tonne. This practical reference consolidates the numbers, tools and checks you need to run ore preparation as a business, not just a chore.

Across Africa, ASM produces a significant share of national gold output. Ghana's ASM sector, for example, contributes a substantial portion of total gold production and employs over a million people directly and indirectly. Yet most operators lose 3060% of recoverable gold at the crushing and milling stage because of poor size reduction, worn liners, or over-coarse grinding. Getting ore preparation right is the single highest-return intervention available to a small operator.

Why Size Reduction Governs Recovery

Gold in African ASM orebodies is usually fine-grained and locked inside quartz or sulphide matrix. Liberation freeing the gold grains from the gangue only happens when the ore is ground fine enough. If you stop at 5 mm, gravity tables and sluices will lose most of the fine gold. If you grind too fine, you create slimes that blind your recovery equipment and increase water and power costs. The practical target for most artisanal gravity circuits is a product size (P80) of 75150 m, roughly the fineness of fine flour.

Step-by-Step Practical Method

  1. Characterise the ore. Take a 2050 kg representative sample. Note hardness (quartz vs. soft oxide), moisture and visible sulphides. Hard quartz needs more grinding energy.
  2. Primary crush. Feed ROM ore to the jaw crusher. Keep the feed below the manufacturer's maximum to avoid jamming. Reduce to 50100 mm.
  3. Secondary crush. Run through a cone or hammer mill to 1020 mm. Remove tramp metal and wood to protect the mill.
  4. Grind. Charge the ball mill with ore, water and steel balls at the correct ball-to-ore ratio (typically 5:1 to 10:1). Run for the time determined by your grind test usually 3090 minutes per batch.
  5. Check the grind. Take a pulp sample, pan it, and feel for grit. If it feels sandy, grind longer. If it is slick slime, you have over-ground.
  6. Feed recovery. Send the pulp to your gravity circuit (shaking table, sluice, or centrifuge) or to leaching. Do not let pulp sit and settle feed it promptly.
SAFETY WARNING RESPIRATORY & MERCURY HAZARD

Crushing and milling generate fine silica dust that causes silicosis an irreversible lung disease. Always wear a fitted dust mask (N95 or better), use water sprays or wet milling to suppress dust, and never dry-grind in an enclosed space without ventilation. If you use mercury downstream, know that grinding liberates more mercury-reactive gold and increases exposure risk. Mercury is neurotoxic handle it only with gloves, in a ventilated area, and never burn amalgam indoors. Under the Minamata Convention, many African states now restrict mercury use in ASM.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Under-grinding (coarse product). Fix: extend mill time or add a second grinding pass; test with a pan.
  • Over-grinding (slimes). Fix: shorten mill time, reduce ball charge, or add a classification step.
  • Wrong ball-to-ore ratio. Fix: weigh your charge; aim for 5:1 to 10:1 by mass.
  • Worn jaw plates or mill liners. Fix: inspect weekly; replace when clearance exceeds spec worn liners cost recovery and energy.
  • Contamination. Fix: keep oil, grease and wood out of the mill; they coat gold and block amalgamation or leaching.
  • No representative sampling. Fix: sample every batch so you can calculate real grade and recovery.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Representative ore sample taken and hardness recorded.
  2. Jaw crusher feed size within manufacturer limit; no tramp metal.
  3. Secondary crusher producing 1020 mm product.
  4. Ball mill charged at correct ball-to-ore ratio (5:110:1).
  5. Grind time verified by pan test target P80 75150 m.
  6. Dust suppression (water spray or wet milling) active; masks worn.
  7. Worn liners and media inspected and replaced on schedule.
  8. Batch weight, grind time and recovery recorded for costing.
