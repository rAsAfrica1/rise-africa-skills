1. When to Plant: Timing with Rains and Temperature

Planting at the right time is the single most important decision for your wheat and barley harvest. In most African smallholder regions, you want to sow when the rains have reliably started and the soil is moist to at least 15 cm deep. For wheat, the ideal soil temperature for germination is between 12C and 25C. Barley is tougher and can germinate at 5C, but it still needs moisture. If you plant too early, seeds may rot in dry soil or be eaten by birds and insects. If you plant too late, the crop may hit drought or heat stress during flowering, cutting your yield by half or more.

In East Africa, for example, the long rains usually begin in late March to early April. Wheat should be planted within the first two weeks of steady rain. In the highlands of Ethiopia, barley is often planted in June or July with the main rains. In South Africa's winter rainfall region, plant wheat from mid-April to mid-June. For barley, you can plant two to three weeks earlier than wheat because it tolerates cold better. Always check with your local extension officer for the exact window in your area.

A simple test: dig a hole 15 cm deep. If the soil at the bottom is moist enough to form a ball when squeezed, and rain is forecast for the next few days, you can plant. If the soil is dry, wait. Do not plant into dry soil hoping for rainit is a gamble that often fails. Also avoid planting right before a heavy storm, as seeds can wash away. The best time is when the soil is moist but not waterlogged.

Keep a record of your planting dates each season. After a few years, you will know the best week for your farm. Remember, a delay of just one week can reduce wheat yields by 10-20% in some areas. So prepare your land early and have your seed ready before the rains come.

2. Broadcasting vs. Row Planting: Pros and Cons

Broadcasting means scattering seed by hand over the soil surface. It is fast and needs no special tools. A farmer can broadcast one hectare in about two hours. But it has big problems: seed falls unevenly, so some plants are crowded and others are far apart. You also need 20-30% more seed than row planting. Birds and rats eat more seed because it sits on top. Weeds grow easily because you cannot hoe between plants. And you cannot easily apply fertiliser or control weeds. Broadcasting is only recommended for very small plots or when you are short of labour and time.

Row planting means putting seeds in lines, usually 15-25 cm apart for wheat and barley. You can make rows with a hoe, a jab planter, or an ox-drawn drill. Row planting uses less seedabout 100-120 kg per hectare for wheat instead of 150-180 kg for broadcasting. Plants get even sunlight, water, and nutrients. You can walk between rows to weed, top-dress fertiliser, and spray pests. Yields are typically 20-40% higher than broadcasting. The downside is that row planting takes more time and labour. With a hoe, one person can plant only 0.2-0.3 hectares per day. But the extra yield pays for the extra work.

For smallholders, a compromise is to broadcast then cover with a light harrow or rake. This improves seed-soil contact but still leaves uneven spacing. If you have oxen, an ox-drawn seed drill is bestit opens a furrow, drops seed, and covers it in one pass. It can plant 1-2 hectares per day. If you have no oxen, a jab planter is a good manual tool. It makes a hole, drops a seed, and covers it. You can plant 0.5 hectares per day with a jab planter.

In short: broadcast only if you must, but aim for rows. Even simple rows made with a hoe will give you a better harvest. Start with a small area and compare. You will see the difference.

3. Using a Jab Planter or Ox-Drawn Seed Drill

A jab planter is a hand-held tool that makes planting easy and precise. It has a metal spike that opens a hole in the soil, a seed hopper, and a mechanism that drops one or two seeds into the hole and covers it. You push it into the soil, squeeze the handle, and pull it out. The whole action takes about two seconds. With a jab planter, you can plant wheat or barley at the correct depth (3-5 cm) and spacing (15-20 cm between plants). You use less seedabout 100 kg per hectareand you do not have to bend down. A good jab planter costs $30-$60 and lasts several seasons. It is ideal for small plots up to 1 hectare.

To use a jab planter, first prepare a fine seedbed. Mark your rows with a string or a simple wooden marker. Set the planter to drop the right number of seeds. For wheat, one seed per hole is enough if spacing is 15 cm. For barley, one or two seeds. Walk backwards so you do not step on the planted rows. Keep the planter vertical and push it in to the correct depthmost jab planters have a depth stop. After planting, you may need to lightly press the soil with your foot to ensure good seed contact.

An ox-drawn seed drill is a bigger investment but saves labour and time. It is a metal box with a seed hopper, tubes that drop seed into furrows, and a covering chain. Two oxen pull it. One person drives the oxen, another walks behind to check seed flow. A good drill can plant 1-2 hectares per day. It places seed at a consistent depth and spacing. You can also attach a fertiliser hopper to apply starter fertiliser at the same time. A new drill costs $300-$800, but you can share with neighbours or hire one from a cooperative. Many extension services offer drills for rent.

If you cannot afford a drill, consider a simple row marker and a hoe. But a jab planter is the best low-cost tool for smallholders. It pays for itself in one or two seasons through saved seed and higher yields.

4. Correct Seed Rate and Spacing for Wheat and Barley

Seed rate is the amount of seed you plant per hectare. If you plant too little, you get fewer plants and lower yield. If you plant too much, plants compete for water and nutrients, and you waste money on seed. For wheat, the recommended seed rate for smallholders is 100-120 kg per hectare. For barley, it is 80-100 kg per hectare. These rates assume good seed with at least 85% germination. If your seed is older or poor quality, increase the rate by 10-20%. For broadcasting, you need 150-180 kg per hectare for wheat and 120-150 kg for barley because not all seed will establish.

Spacing is the distance between plants and between rows. For wheat, aim for 15-20 cm between rows and 5-10 cm between plants within the row. That gives about 200-300 plants per square metre. For barley, rows can be 15-25 cm apart, with 5-8 cm between plants. Barley tillers more than wheat, so it can be a bit thinner. If you are using a jab planter, drop one seed every 15 cm in the row. If you are using a drill, set the seed metering to the correct rate. A simple way to check: plant a small area, then count the seeds in one square metre. You should find 200-300 seeds for wheat, 150-250 for barley.

Many farmers in Africa plant too thickly because they fear poor germination. But thick planting leads to weak stems, more disease, and smaller heads. It also uses more water. On the other hand, too thin planting lets weeds take over. The best approach is to test your seed germination before planting. Take 100 seeds, put them in a moist cloth for 5 days, and count how many sprout. If 85 or more sprout, use the normal rate. If fewer, increase the rate.

Also consider row spacing for weeding. Wider rows (25-30 cm) allow you to use a hoe or ox-drawn cultivator between rows. Narrow rows (15 cm) shade the soil and suppress weeds, but you cannot easily weed. For smallholders with limited labour, 20-25 cm rows are a good compromise. Always calibrate your planter or drill before planting the whole field. A small mistake in seed rate can cost you a lot of yield.

5. Planting Depth: Why 3-5 cm Matters

Planting depth is the distance from the soil surface to the seed. For wheat and barley, the ideal depth is 3-5 cm. If you plant too shallow (less than 2 cm), the seed may dry out before it sprouts, or birds will eat it. The roots may also be exposed. If you plant too deep (more than 6 cm), the seedling uses all its energy to push through the soil and may die before it reaches sunlight. Deep planting also makes the plant more vulnerable to soil-borne diseases. A seed planted at 3-5 cm has enough moisture and warmth to germinate quickly and emerge strongly.

How do you get the right depth? First, prepare a fine seedbed. Large clods and rocks make depth uneven. If you are using a jab planter, set the depth stop to 4 cm. Most jab planters have a metal plate that you can adjust. Push the planter into the soil until the plate touches the surface. That puts the seed at the right depth. If you are using a hoe, make a furrow 4-5 cm deep. You can check by putting a stick in the furrow and marking the soil level. Drop the seed, then cover with 2-3 cm of soil. Do not cover too deep.

For an ox-drawn drill, adjust the depth wheels or the tension on the furrow openers. The drill should place seed at 3-5 cm. After planting, check a few spots by digging carefully with your hands. If you find seeds at 2 cm or 7 cm, adjust the drill. Also, soil type matters. In sandy soil, plant a bit deeper (5 cm) because it dries out faster. In clay soil, plant at 3-4 cm because it holds moisture longer and can crust over. If the soil is very dry on top, you may need to plant deeper to reach moisture, but never deeper than 6 cm.

After planting, press the soil lightly with a roller or your foot. This improves seed-soil contact and helps the seed absorb water. But do not compact the soil too much. A good rule: if you can see the seed after a light rain, it was too shallow. If the seedlings take more than 10 days to emerge, they may be too deep. Check and adjust next time. Correct depth is a simple thing that makes a big difference.

6. Intercropping with Legumes for Soil Health

Intercropping means growing two or more crops together in the same field. For wheat and barley, the best partners are legumes like faba beans, field peas, lentils, or chickpeas. Legumes pull nitrogen from the air and put it into the soil. This reduces your need for fertiliser and improves soil health. In Africa, many smallholders already intercrop maize with beans. You can do the same with wheat or barley. The legume also provides ground cover, which reduces weeds and erosion. And you get a second crop to eat or sell.

How to intercrop? One simple method is to plant one row of wheat or barley, then one row of legumes. For example, plant wheat at 20 cm row spacing, and after every 4 rows of wheat, plant 1 row of faba beans. Or you can mix seed and broadcast both together, but row intercropping is better for management. The legume should be planted at the same time as the cereal or slightly later. For faba beans, use 50-60 kg seed per hectare. For field peas, 40-50 kg per hectare. Do not plant the legume too thickly, or it will compete with the cereal.

Intercropping has many benefits. The legume fixes 20-60 kg of nitrogen per hectare, which can increase wheat yield by 10-20% in the next season. The legume also breaks disease cycles. For example, wheat rust does not affect faba beans. The cereal stems support the legume, and the legume covers the soil. But there are challenges. You need to manage two crops. If you use a herbicide, it may kill the legume. So you must weed by hand or use selective herbicides. Also, the legume may attract pests that also attack wheat, like aphids. Monitor your field regularly.

A good rotation is to intercrop this season, then next season plant the legume as a sole crop, then wheat. This builds soil fertility. In Ethiopia, farmers often intercrop barley with faba beans and get good results. In Kenya, wheat with field peas is common. Start with a small plot. If it works, expand. Intercropping is a low-cost way to improve your soil and your harvest.

<h3>1. When to Plant: Timing with Rains and Temperature</h3>
<p>Planting date is the single most powerful yield lever in rainfed smallholder systems. Wheat and barley germinate reliably when soil temperature is between 12 C and 25 C and when 2540 mm of rain has fallen to wet the seedbed. In Ethiopia's Arsi and Bale zones, the optimum window is late June to mid-July; in Kenya's Narok and Uasin Gishu counties, it is late March to mid-April for the long-rains crop. Barley tolerates slightly cooler soils than wheat and can be planted 710 days earlier. Late planting pushes grain fill into heat stress and terminal drought, cutting yields by 3050%.</p>

<h3>2. Broadcasting vs. Row Planting</h3>
<p>Broadcasting is fast and needs little equipment, but it wastes seed (3050% more seed per hectare), produces uneven depth, and makes weeding and fertiliser placement difficult. Row planting whether by hand hoe, jab planter, or ox-drawn drill gives uniform depth, easier weeding, and typically 1525% higher yields for the same seed. For a market-oriented smallholder, the extra labour of row planting is repaid by better grain quality and easier harvest.</p>

<h3>3. Using a Jab Planter or Ox-Drawn Seed Drill</h3>
<p>The jab planter is the cheapest entry into precision planting: one person can plant 0.30.5 ha per day with consistent depth and spacing. Ox-drawn seed drills (such as the Ethiopian <em>Bereket</em> or the CIMMYT-designed <em>zero-till</em> drill) cover 12 ha per day, place seed and fertiliser together, and reduce labour by 4060%. For smallholders renting oxen, a drill also improves timeliness critical when the rains start late.</p>

<h3>4. Correct Seed Rate and Spacing</h3>
<p>Seed rate depends on variety, seed size, and germination percentage. Adjust upward if germination is below 90%. Row spacing of 2025 cm is standard for smallholder wheat and barley; closer rows suppress weeds but increase seed cost.</p>

<h3>5. Planting Depth: Why 35 cm Matters</h3>
<p>Too shallow (&lt;2 cm) and seed dries out or is eaten by birds; too deep (&gt;6 cm) and the seedling exhausts its reserves before reaching light. The 35 cm band balances moisture access with emergence vigour. On heavy clay soils, aim for the shallower end (3 cm); on sandy soils, 45 cm. In dry seedbeds, plant deeper; in wet seedbeds, shallower.</p>

<h3>6. Intercropping with Legumes for Soil Health</h3>
<p>Intercropping wheat or barley with faba bean, field pea, or chickpea fixes 2060 kg N/ha, breaks disease cycles, and improves soil structure. On smallholder farms, a 1:1 or 2:1 cereal-legume row ratio is practical. The legume also provides a second income stream (grain or fodder) and reduces risk if the cereal fails.</p>
