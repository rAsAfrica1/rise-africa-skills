Module 5

🐷 Breeding Stock Nutrition

Specialised nutrition for boars and breeding sows. Covers what a working boar actually needs for semen quality, how to develop a gilt that lasts, the two very different halves of gestation, feeding the lactating sow when heat is fighting you, rebuilding a thin sow between weaning and service, and the mineral and vitamin decisions that decide whether a sow reaches parity six or is culled at parity three.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Calculate a mature boar's daily feed, energy and lysine allowance from published specifications
  • Set body condition and mineral targets for a replacement gilt before her first service
  • Split gestation into its published feeding stages and state the energy target for each
  • State the daily energy, lysine and intake targets for a high-producing lactating sow
  • Explain why the weaning-to-service period is fed differently from gestation
  • Calculate how gilt replacement cost per litter changes with sow lifetime parity

1. Boar Nutrition and Fertility 2. Gilt Development and Conditioning 3. Pregnant Sow Nutrition 4. Lactating Sow Nutrition 5. Post-Weaning Sow Recovery 6. Longevity and Herd Health
Lesson 5.1~11 min

Boar Nutrition and Fertility

In this lesson
  • Calculate a mature boar's daily feed, energy and lysine allowance from published specifications
  • Explain how selenium and vitamin E act together to protect sperm quality
  • Price the boar's annual feed bill against the value of the piglets he is responsible for

A boar is the cheapest animal on the farm to feed and the most expensive one to get wrong. On a twenty-sow unit one boar stands behind every piglet born, so a fertility problem in one animal is a production problem across the whole herd. Yet the boar is usually the animal fed on leftovers, on whatever is in the store, or on the gestation ration because nobody wanted to mix a separate batch.

Start with the published targets. A mature boar of roughly 350 to 400 kg eats about 2.5 kg of feed a day. At that intake he needs about 3,086 kcal of metabolisable energy and 2,308 Mcal of net energy daily. His diet should carry 0.62 percent standardised ileal digestible lysine and around 13 to 14 percent total protein.

Check the lysine arithmetic yourself. 0.62 percent of 2.5 kg is 0.0062 x 2,500 g = 15.5 g of digestible lysine a day, which matches the published figure of 15 to 16 g. If your boar only eats 2.0 kg because the ration is unpalatable or the pen is hot, he gets 0.0062 x 2,000 = 12.4 g, roughly 20 percent short. The published guidance is blunt about this: underfeeding boars can have negative consequences in sperm production.

The boar's amino acid pattern is notably different from a sow's. Expressed as a percentage of lysine, he needs methionine plus cystine at about 70 percent and threonine at about 74 percent, against roughly 57 to 58 percent and 62 to 63 percent for sows. In practice that means a boar ration built on maize and a single oilseed cake will run short on sulphur amino acids before it runs short on lysine.

Minerals and vitamins are where boar diets earn their keep. Published mature boar specifications are calcium 0.80 percent, available phosphorus 0.40 percent, zinc 125 ppm, copper 15 ppm, selenium 0.30 to 0.50 mg per kg, vitamin E 44 IU per kg and vitamin A 2,000 to 4,000 IU per kg. Zinc supports testicular steroidogenesis and sperm membrane integrity; a deficiency shows up as reduced motility and, in bad cases, testicular atrophy.

Selenium and vitamin E work as one system. Selenium is the core of glutathione peroxidase, the enzyme that protects sperm from lipid peroxidation, and vitamin E protects the same membranes from the other side. In published trials, supplementing 0.5 ppm selenium against an unsupplemented control moved sperm motility from 60.4 to 87.9 percent, normal sperm morphology from 24.2 to 61.9 percent, and fertilisation rate from 73 to 99 percent. Those are the largest single-nutrient responses anywhere in swine reproduction. Organic selenium, as selenomethionine from selenised yeast, is preferred over sodium selenite because it builds tissue reserves that can be mobilised under stress; note that the EU caps selenised yeast at 0.2 mg selenium per kg of complete feed.

Timing matters. Sexual maturity begins at 5 to 7 months, semen can usually be collected from 7 to 8 months, and peak sperm production runs from 12 to 24 months. Nutrition applied at 10 months is building the boar you will use at 14 months, not the one you are using this week.

One more risk: zearalenone, the oestrogenic mould toxin, reduces libido and sperm quality in boars above about 40 ppm. Mouldy maize in the boar pen is not a small problem.

Worked example. A producer near Kaduna, Nigeria, keeps one boar for twenty sows. The boar eats 2.5 kg a day, so 2.5 x 365 = 912.5 kg a year. At a boar ration costing 0.45 USD per kg, that is 912.5 x 0.45 = 410.63 USD a year. Those twenty sows wean roughly 20 x 20 = 400 piglets a year, so the boar's entire feed bill is 410.63 divided by 400 = 1.03 USD per piglet weaned. Cutting his ration to save 100 USD a year, and losing even a five percent farrowing rate as a result, would cost twenty piglets worth 40 USD each, or 800 USD. The maths is never close.

Lesson 5.2~11 min

Gilt Development and Conditioning

In this lesson
  • Set body condition and mineral targets for a replacement gilt before her first service
  • Compare the cost of raising a gilt against buying one at local prices
  • Identify the feed risks that most often shorten a first-parity sow's career

A gilt is not a finisher pig that happens to be female. A finisher is fed to reach market weight cheaply and then leave the farm. A gilt is fed to build a skeleton, a mammary system and a body reserve that will still be working through six litters. Feed her like a finisher and she will farrow once, milk badly, come back thin, and be culled.

The first target is condition, not weight. Published guidance sets gilts entering their first gestation at a body condition score of 3.5 to 4.0 on a nine-point scale, which is deliberately higher than the 2.5 to 3.5 considered acceptable for older sows. A gilt has to carry her own growth as well as her litter, so she needs a real reserve at service. Learn to score by hand: run your palm across the ribs, the spine and the hip bones. If the bones are sharp and easy to count, she is under target. If you cannot find them at all, she is over-conditioned, which brings its own problems at farrowing.

The second target is bone. Published breeding specifications call for higher minerals in gilts and first-parity sows precisely because skeletal development is still going on: total calcium 0.85 to 0.95 percent and total phosphorus 0.41 to 0.62 percent, with standardised total tract digestible phosphorus of 0.30 to 0.35 percent in gestation and a calcium to digestible phosphorus ratio of 2.0 to 2.3 to 1. Multiparous sows, whose skeletons are finished, may be fed lower calcium, in the region of 0.70 to 0.75 percent, provided condition is maintained. Skimping on gilt minerals produces lame sows, and lameness is one of the commonest reasons a young sow is culled.

Our reference standards do not publish a smallholder gilt growth curve, so use the late finisher specifications as your amino acid base and manage energy by eye. The 100 to 130 kg finisher band sits at 0.70 to 0.85 percent SID lysine on 3,100 to 3,250 kcal ME per kg. Feed that amino acid level, but restrict the daily amount so she gains steadily rather than fattening. Then add the breeding mineral pack.

One emerging option worth knowing about is the vitamin D metabolite 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, supplemented at roughly 2 to 5 micrograms per kg. Published guidance notes it carries a cost premium over plain vitamin D3 and that its use is most justified in premium and gilt development programmes, where bone mineralisation and immune status genuinely pay back.

Two feed risks deserve naming. Zearalenone is an oestrogenic mycotoxin, and young gilts are more sensitive than older sows. Repeated exposure around 1 ppm can cause uterine prolapse, and 5 to 10 ppm reduces fertility and causes swollen vulvas and early embryo loss. Second, aflatoxin limits for breeding animals sit at 20 ppb, ten times stricter than the 200 ppb tolerated for finishers over about 45 kg. The moment you decide a pig is a gilt rather than a finisher, her grain quality standard changes.

Worked example. A producer in Lilongwe, Malawi, is deciding whether to buy replacement gilts at 180 USD each or select them from her own finisher pens. To raise one to 130 kg she starts from a weaner worth 40 USD and needs about 130 minus 25 = 105 kg of gain. At a feed conversion ratio of 3.2 that is 105 x 3.2 = 336 kg of feed. At 0.42 USD per kg the feed is 336 x 0.42 = 141.12 USD. Add the breeding mineral upgrade at roughly 0.02 USD per kg over 336 kg, which is 6.72 USD. Her total is 40 + 141.12 + 6.72 = 187.84 USD, slightly more than the 180 USD purchase price.

So on feed cost alone, buying wins narrowly. But home-raised gilts arrive already exposed to her farm's disease picture and she can select on her own records for teat number, feet and temperament. That non-cash advantage is what usually decides it, and it is worth writing down rather than guessing at.

Lesson 5.3~12 min

Pregnant Sow Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Split gestation into its published feeding stages and state the energy target for each
  • Convert a daily energy requirement into a daily feed allowance in kilograms
  • Explain why late gestation feeding determines colostrum quality and piglet survival

Gestation is not one feeding period. It is two, and the change between them is large enough that treating gestation as a single flat ration is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes in small pig units.

The published stages are these. Days 0 to 28, establishing pregnancy: 5.9 Mcal ME per day, about 4.3 Mcal net energy, with SID lysine at 0.50 to 0.56 percent of the diet or roughly 8 to 10 g a day. Days 28 to 90, fetal development: the same 5.9 Mcal ME and the same lysine specification. Days 90 to 112, the mammary development phase: energy jumps to 8.8 Mcal ME per day, about 6.5 Mcal net energy, with SID lysine rising to 0.56 to 0.60 percent and a daily lysine target quoted around 12 to 14 g.

That is a 49 percent increase in daily energy in the last three weeks, and it is not for the piglets alone. It funds mammary epithelial proliferation, the physical building of the milk-producing tissue she will use for the next three weeks, plus the mineral loading for milk. Inadequate late gestation nutrition leads to reduced colostrum volume and quality, and thin colostrum with lower immunoglobulin concentration is directly linked to higher piglet mortality in the first three days of life.

Now turn the energy targets into a feed scoop. Suppose your gestation diet works out at about 3,000 kcal, or 3.0 Mcal, of ME per kilogram. Estimate this from your ingredients using the methods in Module 7, or ask your supplier.

  1. Days 0 to 90: 5.9 Mcal divided by 3.0 Mcal per kg = 1.97 kg, call it 2.0 kg per sow per day.
  2. Days 90 to 112: 8.8 divided by 3.0 = 2.93 kg, call it 2.9 kg per sow per day.
  3. Total for the gestation: 90 days x 2.0 = 180 kg, plus 24 days x 2.9 = 69.6 kg, giving about 250 kg per gestation.

Check the lysine. At 2.0 kg of a 0.50 percent SID lysine diet she gets 0.005 x 2,000 = 10 g a day, right on the published 8 to 10 g band. In late gestation, 0.58 percent of 2,900 g is 16.8 g, which is above the 12 to 14 g quoted for the stage. That gap is a safety margin built into the diet percentage, and it is not a place to economise: mammary tissue built badly cannot be fixed after farrowing.

Three more specifications matter. Total protein around 12 to 13 percent. Calcium 0.85 to 0.95 percent with STTD phosphorus 0.30 to 0.35 percent, held at a calcium to digestible phosphorus ratio of 2.0 to 2.3 to 1. And crude fibre of 6 to 8 percent, which is acceptable in gestation and genuinely useful: a restricted-fed sow is a hungry sow, and fibre gives her gut fill without energy. This is the one class of pig where cheap bulky local ingredients are an advantage rather than a compromise.

Remember the aflatoxin limit for pregnant sows is 20 ppb, and zearalenone above 30 ppm causes early embryo loss and reduced litter size. Mouldy grain fed in the first month of gestation shows up as an empty farrowing pen four months later, by which time nobody remembers the grain.

Worked example. A producer outside Kampala, Uganda, runs twelve sows. She used to feed a flat 2.2 kg all through gestation. Her gestation feed use was 114 days x 2.2 = 250.8 kg per sow, almost exactly the same total as the staged plan above. But the distribution was wrong: her sows were over-fed for ninety days and under-fed for the twenty-four days that build udders. Moving to 2.0 kg then 2.9 kg cost her nothing in total tonnage, 250 kg against 250.8 kg, and required only a second scoop mark on the bucket and a chalk date on the pen card. At 0.40 USD per kg her gestation feed bill is 250 x 0.40 = 100 USD per sow per gestation either way. The change was free; the colostrum was not.

Lesson 5.4~12 min

Lactating Sow Nutrition

In this lesson
  • State the daily energy, lysine and intake targets for a high-producing lactating sow
  • Calculate the diet lysine percentage needed at your own measured feed intake
  • Adjust a lactation diet for heat stress using published nutritional levers

Lactation is the most extreme nutritional demand in pig farming. A sow in her third week of lactation is producing more nutrients per day than a finishing pig deposits in a week, and unlike the finisher she cannot simply grow more slowly if you underfeed her. She takes the shortfall out of her own body.

The published targets for a high-producing sow with a large litter are: a diet supplying about 3,362 kcal ME per kg, or 2,488 Mcal net energy, and a target intake of about 6.4 kg per day across a 21-day lactation. That gives roughly 6.4 x 3,362 = 21,517 kcal ME a day. Lysine is specified as about 60 g of SID lysine per day, corresponding to a diet range of 0.77 to 1.34 percent depending on how much she eats. Crude protein sits at 14 to 16 percent, with digestible protein of at least 14 percent. Calcium is 0.85 to 0.95 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.38 to 0.42 percent with a minimum of 22 g of digestible phosphorus a day, and crude fibre drops to 3 to 4 percent because every kilogram of trough space now has to carry energy, not bulk.

Why is the diet lysine given as a range rather than a number? Because it is a division. Diet lysine percentage equals daily lysine requirement divided by daily feed intake.

  1. A sow eating the target 6.4 kg: 60 g divided by 6,400 g = 0.94 percent.
  2. A sow eating only 4.5 kg: 60 divided by 4,500 = 1.33 percent.
  3. A sow eating 5.5 kg: 60 divided by 5,500 = 1.09 percent.

That is the whole logic of the published 0.77 to 1.34 percent band. Measure your sows' real intake for three days, then set your lysine specification from your own division, not from a textbook average.

Feeding must be ad libitum. Remove every restriction from the day she farrows. Sows eating under about 6.4 kg a day at peak lactation risk inadequate litter growth and a higher incidence of mastitis, metritis and agalactia. Water must be unlimited too, because dehydration cuts feed intake faster than almost anything else. Expect her to mobilise body tissue anyway: a normal 21-day lactation costs a sow in the range of 50 to 150 pounds, roughly 23 to 68 kg, of body weight. Of her total energy use, 65 to 80 percent goes into milk, 15 to 25 percent into maintenance, and 5 to 15 percent comes out of her own back.

Two fatty acids are specified for lactation: 125 g of linoleic acid a day, which supports milk fat and immunity, and 10 g a day of alpha-linolenic acid, which reaches the piglet through colostrum and supports neurological development.

Heat is the enemy. Above about 29 degrees Celsius, sow feed intake falls 15 to 30 percent. The published response is threefold: raise energy density from 3,362 to 3,500 or more kcal ME per kg by adding 2 to 3 percent fat; raise sodium by 0.1 to 0.2 percent and potassium by 0.1 percent to support appetite signalling; and consider betaine at 0.1 to 0.2 percent for osmotic balance. Cooling and clean water do the rest.

Worked example. A producer in Kumasi, Ghana, farrows through a hot season. Her sows normally eat 6.0 kg but drop to 4.5 kg in the heat, a 25 percent fall within the published range. Her energy delivery falls from 6.0 x 3,362 = 20,172 kcal to 4.5 x 3,362 = 15,129 kcal, a shortfall of 5,043 kcal a day. Raising density to 3,500 kcal per kg gets her to 4.5 x 3,500 = 15,750 kcal, recovering only 621 kcal, about 12 percent of the gap. The lesson is honest: diet density helps but cannot close a heat gap on its own. She also needs shade, wet floors or drip cooling, night feeding and cold water.

Her lysine, meanwhile, must move. At 4.5 kg she needs 60 divided by 4,500 = 1.33 percent, close to the top of the published band. A sow eating less needs a richer diet, not the same one.

Lesson 5.5~12 min

Post-Weaning Sow Recovery

In this lesson
  • Explain why the weaning-to-service period is fed differently from gestation
  • Calculate the cost of non-productive sow days on your own farm
  • Build a simple condition-based feeding rule for weaned sows

The day you wean is the day your next litter starts. A sow walks out of the farrowing pen having lost somewhere between 23 and 68 kg of body weight over 21 days, with 5 to 15 percent of her lactation energy having come out of her own tissue. What you feed her in the next week decides how quickly she cycles, how many eggs she sheds, and whether her next litter is a good one or a disappointment.

The standard practice, widely used and worth adopting, is to keep the sow on the lactation diet, fed generously, from weaning until she is served. Do not drop her onto the restricted gestation allowance the moment the piglets leave. The gestation figure of 5.9 Mcal ME a day is a maintenance-plus-pregnancy allowance for a sow who is already in condition. A sow who has just been stripped by lactation is not in that position, and feeding her as if she were simply extends the time before she cycles.

How much? Our reference standards do not publish a weaning-to-service allowance, so treat any figure as approximate and manage by condition. A practical starting point on many farms is to offer the lactation ration free choice and expect a dry sow to settle around 3.5 kg a day, adjusting up for thin sows and down for fat ones. What matters is the direction, not the decimal: more than gestation, on the richer diet, for a short period.

Use condition scores to make it a rule rather than a habit:

  1. Score every sow on the nine-point scale on the day she is weaned and write it on her card.
  2. Sows at 2.5 or below get the lactation ration free choice until served, and stay on a raised gestation allowance for the first 28 days of pregnancy.
  3. Sows at 3.0 to 3.5 get the lactation ration free choice until served, then the normal 5.9 Mcal gestation allowance.
  4. Sows above 4.0 go straight onto the gestation allowance. Over-conditioned sows farrow badly and eat poorly in the next lactation.
  5. Re-score at day 30 and day 90 of gestation and correct then, not at farrowing when it is too late.

The nutrients that matter here are the same ones that matter in the boar. Choline is specified at 1.00 to 1.25 g per kg for breeding sows against 0.30 to 0.50 for finishers, and deficiency is linked to fatty liver and reduced reproductive performance. Vitamin E sits at 44 IU per kg and selenium at 0.20 to 0.30 mg per kg for sows, the antioxidant pair that supports colostrum quality. Vitamin D3 is 800 IU per kg for breeding animals against 150 to 220 for growing pigs. None of these is expensive; all of them are commonly left out of a farm-mixed sow ration.

And keep the mycotoxin discipline. A sow served on mouldy grain is a sow with a small litter. Zearalenone at 5 to 10 ppm reduces fertility and causes early embryo loss, and above 30 ppm in sows it cuts litter size outright.

Worked example. A producer in Nakuru, Kenya, keeps twenty sows and notices her weaning-to-service interval averages twelve days instead of the five to seven she expects. That is roughly seven extra non-productive days per litter. At 2.2 litters per sow per year, that is 7 x 2.2 = 15.4 lost days per sow per year, and across twenty sows, 15.4 x 20 = 308 sow-days a year.

Put a price on it. Her total annual sow feed is about 900 kg at an average 0.44 USD per kg, or 396 USD, which is 396 divided by 365 = 1.08 USD per sow per day just in feed. So 308 days costs her 308 x 1.08 = 332.64 USD in feed alone, before counting the litters she did not produce. Feeding the lactation ration for five extra days per sow costs 5 x 3.5 kg x 0.50 USD = 8.75 USD per sow, or 175 USD across twenty sows. Spending 175 to recover 333 in feed, plus the throughput, is an easy decision once it is written down.

Lesson 5.6~12 min

Longevity and Herd Health

In this lesson
  • Calculate how gilt replacement cost per litter changes with sow lifetime parity
  • List the mineral and vitamin specifications that differ most between breeding and growing pigs
  • Set a replacement rate target and a culling rule for your own herd

Every sow on your farm carries a debt. She cost money to buy or raise before she produced anything, and that cost has to be repaid out of the litters she goes on to wean. How many litters she achieves before she is culled is one of the biggest single levers on the cost of a weaner, and almost nobody calculates it.

Do the arithmetic. Take the gilt cost of about 215 USD developed in Lesson 2, counting the weaner value, the feed to 130 kg and the breeding mineral upgrade.

  1. Culled after 3 litters: 215 divided by 3 = 71.67 USD carried by each litter.
  2. Culled after 6 litters: 215 divided by 6 = 35.83 USD per litter.
  3. The difference is 71.67 minus 35.83 = 35.84 USD per litter.

On a twenty-sow herd producing 2.2 litters per sow per year, that is 20 x 2.2 = 44 litters a year, so 44 x 35.84 = 1,576.96 USD a year, purely from sows lasting six litters instead of three. No extra feed, no extra buildings, no extra labour. It is the highest-return change available to most small breeding herds.

What ends sow careers? Lameness, reproductive failure, and body condition collapse. Nutrition touches all three.

Bone first. Breeding animals are fed a genuinely different mineral profile. Manganese is 25 mg per kg for sows and boars against just 2 to 4 mg per kg for growing pigs, because it supports bone development and reproductive function. Calcium in gestation is 0.85 to 0.95 percent for gilts and first-parity sows, with multiparous sows able to run at 0.70 to 0.75 percent if condition holds. Total phosphorus is 0.41 to 0.62 percent with STTD phosphorus 0.30 to 0.35 percent in gestation and 0.38 to 0.42 percent in lactation, held at a calcium to digestible phosphorus ratio of 2.0 to 2.3 to 1. That ratio is not decorative: wide calcium to phosphorus ratios interfere with phosphorus absorption, and calcium above 1.0 percent also reduces zinc absorption.

Vitamins next. Vitamin D3 is 800 IU per kg for breeding stock against 150 to 220 for growers. Vitamin A is 2,000 to 4,000 IU per kg against 1,300 to 1,600 for finishers. Vitamin E is 44 IU per kg, iron 80 mg per kg, copper 15 ppm, zinc 100 to 125 ppm, iodine 0.14 to 0.20 mg per kg and vitamin K 0.50 mg per kg. Selenium is 0.20 to 0.30 mg per kg for sows. Write these on a card and check any premix you buy against them, because a growing-pig premix used in a sow ration will be short on exactly the nutrients that determine longevity.

Phosphorus deserves one practical note. Plant feeds hold most of their phosphorus as phytate, roughly 70 to 80 percent of it unavailable to the pig. Microbial phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg releases an additional 0.10 to 0.20 percent available phosphorus, which is both a cost saving and a genuine bone-health measure in maize and oilseed cake rations.

Set a culling rule so decisions are made on evidence rather than mood. A workable one: cull for lameness that does not resolve, for two consecutive returns to service, for a litter weaned below a threshold you set from your own records, and for condition below 2.0 that does not recover within one cycle. Everything else stays.

Worked example. A producer in Lusaka, Zambia, reviews her records and finds her average sow leaves after 3.4 litters, and that lameness accounts for more than half her culls. Her premix turns out to be a general pig premix supplying growing-pig manganese levels. Upgrading to a breeding premix costs her an estimated 0.03 USD per kg of sow feed. Her herd eats 20 sows x 900 kg = 18,000 kg of sow feed a year, so the upgrade costs 18,000 x 0.03 = 540 USD a year. If it moves average parity from 3.4 to only 4.4, her replacement cost per litter falls from 215 divided by 3.4 = 63.24 to 215 divided by 4.4 = 48.86, a saving of 14.38 per litter across 44 litters, or 632.72 USD. It pays, narrowly, on that assumption alone, and the lameness and welfare gain comes free.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A mature boar eating 2.5 kg per day on a 0.62% SID lysine diet receives how much digestible lysine daily?

0.62 percent of 2,500 g is 15.5 g, matching the published target of 15 to 16 g per day.

2. In published trials, supplementing 0.5 ppm selenium changed sperm motility from 60.4% to what?

Motility rose to 87.9 percent. The 99 percent figure in the same trial was the fertilisation rate, not motility.

3. Why is the boar's requirement for methionine plus cystine, as a share of lysine, higher than a sow's?

Published boar specifications set methionine plus cystine at about 70 percent of lysine and threonine at about 74 percent, both notably higher than sow ratios.

4. Which selenium source is preferred for breeding boars and why?

Inorganic selenite turns over rapidly with minimal tissue retention, while organic selenium is incorporated into tissue reserves and mobilised during stress.

5. At roughly what dietary level does zearalenone begin to reduce boar libido and sperm quality?

Published thresholds place boar libido and sperm quality decline above about 40 ppm, although gilts are affected at far lower levels.

6. What body condition score is published as the target for gilts entering first gestation on a nine-point scale?

Gilts are targeted at 3.5 to 4.0, above the 2.5 to 3.5 acceptable for older sows, because they are still growing while carrying a litter.

7. Why do gilts and first-parity sows receive higher dietary calcium than multiparous sows?

Published guidance sets 0.85 to 0.95 percent calcium for gilts on the basis of continuing skeletal development; mature sows may be fed 0.70 to 0.75 percent if condition holds.

8. A gilt needs 105 kg of gain at a feed conversion ratio of 3.2. How much feed is that?

105 multiplied by 3.2 gives 336 kg of feed for that period of growth.

9. The aflatoxin limit for breeding animals and pregnant sows is:

Breeding animals and pregnant sows are held to 20 ppb, while finishers over roughly 45 kg tolerate 200 ppb.

10. Which supplement is specifically noted as most justified in gilt development and premium programmes because of its cost premium?

The vitamin D metabolite 25(OH)D3 improves bone mineralisation and immune status but costs more than plain D3, so its use is targeted at gilt development and premium programmes.

11. What is the published daily metabolisable energy target for a sow between days 90 and 112 of gestation?

Late gestation rises to 8.8 Mcal ME per day, up from 5.9 Mcal for days 0 to 90, to fund mammary development.

12. On a gestation diet supplying 3.0 Mcal ME per kg, how much feed delivers the 8.8 Mcal late gestation requirement?

8.8 divided by 3.0 gives 2.93 kg, rounded to about 2.9 kg per sow per day.

13. What is the main consequence of under-feeding a sow between days 90 and 112?

Days 90 to 112 build mammary tissue and colostrum. Inadequate feeding here produces thin colostrum with lower immunoglobulin content and more piglet deaths in the first three days.

14. Why is 6 to 8 percent crude fibre useful specifically in gestation diets?

Gestating sows are fed restricted amounts and stay hungry. Fibre supplies bulk and supports gut function, which is why cheap bulky local ingredients suit this class of pig.

15. A sow eats 2.0 kg per day for 90 days and 2.9 kg per day for 24 days. What is her total gestation feed?

90 x 2.0 = 180 kg and 24 x 2.9 = 69.6 kg, giving about 250 kg for the gestation.

16. A sow eats 4.5 kg per day and needs 60 g of SID lysine. What diet lysine percentage does she need?

60 g divided by 4,500 g is 1.33 percent, near the top of the published 0.77 to 1.34 percent lactation band.

17. What share of a lactating sow's total energy use goes into milk production?

Milk takes 65 to 80 percent, maintenance 15 to 25 percent, and 5 to 15 percent is met by mobilising her own body tissue.

18. Above roughly 29 degrees Celsius, how far does lactating sow feed intake typically fall?

Published heat stress guidance puts the decline at 15 to 30 percent against thermoneutral conditions.

19. Why does crude fibre drop to 3-4% in lactation when gestation tolerates 6-8%?

A lactating sow is already struggling to eat enough energy. Bulky fibre displaces energy she cannot afford to lose, so the specification tightens.

20. Which combination is published as the nutritional response to heat stress in lactating sows?

The published levers are higher energy density through added fat, electrolyte adjustment to support appetite signalling, and betaine for osmoregulation.

21. Why should a newly weaned sow stay on the lactation diet until she is served?

The gestation allowance assumes a sow already in condition. A sow that has lost 23 to 68 kg needs generous feeding of the richer diet until service.

22. What is the published choline requirement for breeding sows?

Sows need 1.00 to 1.25 g/kg, markedly above the 0.30 to 0.50 g/kg for finishers, largely because of colostrum choline demands.

23. Seven extra non-productive days per litter at 2.2 litters per year equals how many lost days per sow per year?

7 multiplied by 2.2 gives 15.4 lost sow-days per year for each sow affected.

24. A weaned sow scores 4.5 on the nine-point scale. What should she receive?

Over-conditioned sows do not need recovery feeding. They farrow badly and eat poorly in the following lactation, so they go directly onto the gestation allowance.

25. Which mycotoxin most directly threatens a sow served on poor-quality grain?

Zearalenone is oestrogenic; 5 to 10 ppm reduces fertility and causes early embryo loss, and above 30 ppm it reduces litter size.

26. A gilt costs $215 to develop. What replacement cost does each litter carry if she weans six litters?

215 divided by 6 is 35.83 USD per litter, against 71.67 USD if she only reaches three litters.

27. How does the manganese requirement of breeding stock compare with growing pigs?

Sows and boars are specified at 25 mg/kg to support bone and reproductive function, against only 2 to 4 mg/kg for growing pigs.

28. Approximately how much of the phosphorus in plant-based feeds is bound as phytate and unavailable?

Roughly 70 to 80 percent is phytate-bound, which is why microbial phytase releasing an extra 0.10 to 0.20 percent available phosphorus is worth using.

29. Why does a wide calcium to phosphorus ratio matter in a sow diet?

Wide ratios impair phosphorus absorption and dietary calcium above 1.0 percent reduces zinc uptake, so the 2.0 to 2.3 to 1 target is a working constraint.

30. Which vitamin D3 level is specified for breeding swine?

Breeding sows and boars are specified at 800 IU/kg, well above the 150 to 220 IU/kg for growing pigs.

Module 5 capstone

Build a Breeding Herd Nutrition Plan for your own farm using your own animals and your own market prices. Step 1: list every breeding animal you own with its parity, an estimated body condition score on a nine-point scale, and the date of its last farrowing or service. Step 2: weigh out, for three consecutive days, exactly what each of three sows in gestation and three sows in lactation actually eats, and record the leftovers, so you know your real intakes rather than your intended ones. Step 3: price each ingredient in your gestation, lactation and boar rations at today's local market rate in USD and calculate the cost per kilogram of each diet. Step 4: using the daily energy and lysine targets in this module, calculate the feed allowance in kilograms per day for early gestation, late gestation and lactation on your own diets, and compare those numbers with what you measured in Step 2. Step 5: calculate your sow's total annual feed cost and divide it by the number of piglets she weaned in the last twelve months to get your cost per weaner. Step 6: write a one-page plan naming the three changes that would most improve piglets weaned per sow per year, each with a cost in USD and the parity target you expect it to buy you. Carry this page into Module 8 for the breeding herd economics work.
