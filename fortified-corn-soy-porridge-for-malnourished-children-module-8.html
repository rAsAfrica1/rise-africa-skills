1. Calculating the cost of making one packet of porridge

To know if your porridge business is making money, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one packet. This is called your cost of production. Start by listing all the ingredients you use for a batch. For example, to make 10 packets of 500g each, you might use 5kg of maize, 2kg of soybeans, 1kg of sugar, 200g of salt, and 100g of vitamin premix. Write down the price you paid for each. Suppose maize costs 500 shillings per kg, soybeans 800 per kg, sugar 300 per kg, salt 100 per kg, and premix 2000 per kg. Your ingredient cost for the batch is: maize 5x500=2500, soy 2x800=1600, sugar 1x300=300, salt 0.2x100=20, premix 0.1x2000=200. Total ingredients = 4620 shillings. Divide by 10 packets = 462 shillings per packet for ingredients.

Next, add other costs. These include packaging (bags, labels), fuel (charcoal, gas, or electricity for roasting and milling), labour (if you pay someone to help), and transport (to buy ingredients or deliver). For one batch, packaging might cost 500 shillings, fuel 800, labour 1000, transport 300. Total other costs = 2600 shillings, or 260 per packet. So total cost per packet = 462 + 260 = 722 shillings.

Remember to include a portion of your equipment cost. If your grinding mill cost 200,000 shillings and will last for 5 years (about 1000 batches), then each batch should set aside 200 shillings for equipment replacement. That adds 20 shillings per packet. So final cost per packet = 742 shillings.

Always recalculate when prices change. Keep a small notebook and write down every purchase. This way, you always know your true cost and can set a fair price that covers everything and leaves profit.

2. Setting a selling price that gives you profit

Once you know your cost per packet, you can set a selling price that gives you a profit. Profit is the money left after you subtract all costs from your sales. A simple rule is to aim for a profit margin of at least 20-30% on each packet. If your cost is 742 shillings, a 25% profit means you add 186 shillings, giving a selling price of 928 shillings. You might round it to 950 shillings for easy handling.

But you must also check what customers are willing to pay. Ask other sellers, visit local markets, and see what similar products cost. If competitors sell at 900 shillings, you may need to reduce your cost or find a way to add value, such as better packaging or a health claim. Never set a price below your cost, even to attract customers, because you will lose money and soon close down.

Consider offering different sizes. A 250g packet might cost you 371 shillings to make; selling at 500 shillings gives a good profit and is affordable for poor families. You can also offer a discount for bulk buys, like 10 packets for 9,000 shillings instead of 9,500. This encourages larger sales.

Review your price regularly. If ingredient prices rise, adjust your selling price. Tell your customers why: 'The price of soy has gone up, so I must increase slightly.' Honesty builds trust. Keep a small sign or tell customers your price clearly. Always calculate your profit per packet: selling price minus cost. If you sell 50 packets a day at 950 shillings, your daily profit is 50 x (950-742) = 10,400 shillings. That is your reward for good business.

3. Keeping simple records of income and expenses

Keeping records is like keeping a map of your business. Without it, you cannot know if you are making a profit or where your money is going. You do not need a computer or an accountant. A simple exercise book and a pen are enough. Divide each page into two columns: one for income (money coming in) and one for expenses (money going out). Every time you sell porridge, write the date, the number of packets, and the total money received. Every time you buy ingredients, pay for transport, or pay a helper, write the date, what you bought, and the amount.

At the end of each day, add up your income and expenses. Subtract expenses from income to get your daily profit or loss. At the end of the week or month, do the same for the whole period. This helps you see patterns: maybe sales are higher on market days, or expenses go up when you buy from a certain supplier. You can then make better decisions, like buying more on market days or finding a cheaper supplier.

Also keep receipts and bills in a separate envelope or folder. If you buy ingredients in bulk, keep the receipt. This is proof of your expenses and helps if you need to apply for a loan or show a tax officer. Even a simple notebook is accepted by many microfinance institutions if it is neat and consistent.

Finally, record your own drawings (money you take out of the business for personal use). This is not an expense but it reduces your cash. By tracking it, you avoid confusing business money with personal money. Good records lead to good decisions and business growth.

4. Managing cash flow when sales are slow

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. When sales are slow, money coming in is less than money going out, and you can run out of cash to buy ingredients or pay bills. This is a common problem, especially during planting season or when schools are closed. To manage it, you need to plan ahead and be flexible.

First, reduce your production. If you normally make 100 packets a week, cut back to 50. This lowers your ingredient costs and avoids spoilage. Second, find alternative markets. Can you sell to health centres, churches, or during community events? Offer small samples to attract new customers. Third, consider selling on credit to trusted customers, but only if you are sure they will pay. Keep a list of debts and follow up politely.

You can also negotiate with suppliers. Ask if you can buy ingredients on credit and pay after you sell. Some will agree if you have a good relationship. Another strategy is to diversify: if porridge sales are slow, maybe you can also sell roasted soy snacks or maize flour. This brings in extra cash.

Finally, build a small cash reserve during good times. When sales are high, set aside 10% of your profit in a safe place. This reserve can cover you during slow periods. Remember, cash flow problems are temporary. With careful planning, you can keep your business alive and ready for when sales pick up again.

5. Saving money to buy ingredients in bulk

Buying ingredients in bulk saves you money. For example, a 50kg bag of maize might cost 40,000 shillings, while buying 1kg at a time costs 500 shillings per kg, totaling 25,000 shillings for 50kg? Wait, that would be cheaper per kg? Actually, bulk is usually cheaper per kg. Let's say 1kg costs 500, so 50kg would cost 25,000 if bought separately. But bulk price might be 22,000 for 50kg, saving 3,000 shillings. Similarly, soybeans, sugar, and premix are cheaper in larger quantities. But to buy in bulk, you need a lump sum of money. That is why saving is essential.

Start a savings plan. Every day or week, put aside a small amount, even 500 shillings, into a separate container, bank account, or mobile money savings. If you save 500 shillings daily for 30 days, you have 15,000 shillings. In two months, you can buy a bulk bag of maize. You can also join a savings group (like a village savings and loan association) where members contribute regularly and take turns receiving the lump sum. This is common in many African communities and helps you access bulk-buying funds.

When you have enough, buy from a reliable supplier. Check quality: maize should be dry and free of mould, soybeans clean. Store properly in airtight containers to avoid pests. Buying in bulk reduces your cost per packet, increasing your profit. For instance, if you save 3,000 shillings on a 50kg bag, that is 60 shillings per kg, and if you use 5kg per batch, you save 300 shillings per batch. That adds up.

Make saving a habit. Treat it like a business expense. Even during slow sales, try to save something. Over time, your savings will grow and your business will become more profitable.

6. Understanding taxes and licenses for food businesses

As a food producer, you must follow certain rules to sell legally and safely. Taxes are money you pay to the government to support public services. Licenses are permits that show your business is registered and meets health standards. In many African countries, small businesses like porridge producers may qualify for simplified tax regimes or even be exempt if turnover is below a certain threshold. Visit your local tax office or revenue authority to ask about your obligations. For example, in Kenya, small businesses with turnover below 5 million shillings may pay a presumptive tax. In Nigeria, you may need to register with the Corporate Affairs Commission and obtain a tax identification number.

For food businesses, health licenses are crucial. You will likely need a food handling certificate or a health permit from your local government or public health department. This often involves an inspection of your production area to ensure it is clean, has proper water, and safe storage. You may also need a business permit from your local council. Fees vary, but they are usually affordable for small producers. Do not avoid these licenses: they protect your customers from foodborne illnesses and protect you from fines or closure.

Also consider product certification if you want to sell in larger markets. For example, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) or NAFDAC in Nigeria may require testing and approval for packaged foods. This can be costly, so start small and expand as your business grows. Keep all your licenses and tax receipts in a safe place. Display them if required.

Paying taxes and getting licenses may seem like a burden, but they build trust with customers and open doors to bigger buyers like schools, hospitals, and supermarkets. They also give you legal protection. Budget for these costs in your expenses. If you are unsure, ask other food producers or visit a small business support centre for guidance.

3. Keeping Simple Records of Income and Expenses

You do not need a computer. A hardcover exercise book with two columns â Money In and Money Out â is enough. Record every sale, every purchase, every transport payment, and every loan repayment. At the end of each week, subtract Money Out from Money In. If the answer is positive, you made a profit. If negative, you made a loss and must find out why.

Keep separate records for: (1) daily sales, (2) ingredient purchases, (3) labour payments, (4) transport, (5) licences and taxes, and (6) savings. Use a simple receipt book for customers who buy in bulk. This protects you if a customer claims they paid or did not pay.

4. Managing Cash Flow When Sales Are Slow

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. You can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash to buy ingredients. Slow sales happen during school holidays, planting season, or when a health worker changes supplier. Prepare by keeping a cash reserve of at least two weeks of operating costs, reducing production batches, selling smaller packet sizes, and finding institutional buyers such as clinics, schools, and NGOs.

â  SAFETY WARNING â DO NOT CUT CORNERS TO SAVE MONEY

Never reduce the vitamin-mineral premix, milk powder, or oil to lower costs. Fortified corn-soy porridge is a complementary food for malnourished children. Removing or reducing micronutrients can cause serious harm, including vitamin A deficiency, anaemia, and poor growth. Always follow the approved fortification recipe and buy premix from a certified supplier. Store ingredients off the floor, away from chemicals, and use clean, dry packaging. If a batch is contaminated or expired, destroy it â do not sell it cheaply to poor families.

5. Saving Money to Buy Ingredients in Bulk

Buying in bulk lowers your unit cost. A 50 kg bag of maize usually costs less per kg than five 10 kg bags. Join a Village Savings and Loan Association (VSLA) or a SACCO to save weekly and access a small loan for bulk purchase. Set a rule: save at least 10% of every sale into a separate bulk-buying fund. Buy together with other producers to negotiate better prices.

6. Understanding Taxes and Licences for Food Businesses

Food businesses must be registered and licensed. Requirements vary by country, but common ones include: business name registration, local council trading licence, food handling certificate, health inspection certificate, and tax registration. In many African countries, small businesses below a turnover threshold pay a simplified presumptive tax. Visit your local council, revenue authority, and bureau of standards to confirm what applies to you. Keep all receipts â they are proof of compliance and can be used as business expenses.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate your unit cost before setting any price.
  2. Set a selling price with at least a 30% margin above unit cost.
  3. Record every sale and every expense in one book, daily.
  4. Keep a cash reserve equal to two weeks of operating costs.
  5. Save at least 10% of every sale for bulk ingredient purchase.
  6. Never reduce fortification premix or milk powder to cut costs.
  7. Register your business and keep all licence and tax receipts.
  8. Review your records weekly and adjust prices or costs monthly.
