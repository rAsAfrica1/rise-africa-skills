1. Calculating the True Cost per Brick Including Labour and Fuel

To know if you are making a profit, you must calculate the true cost of every single brick you produce. Many makers only count the cement and sand, but that is a mistake. Your true cost includes materials, labour, fuel, and a share of your overheads. Let's break it down with a real example.

Suppose you make 1,000 solid bricks in a week. You use 10 bags of cement at $8 each = $80. You use 2 tonnes of sand at $15 per tonne = $30. You pay two workers $10 each per day for 5 days = $100. You use 20 litres of diesel for your mixer at $1.20 per litre = $24. You also use water and small tools, add $10. Your total direct cost is $80 + $30 + $100 + $24 + $10 = $244. That gives a cost of $0.244 per brick, or about 25 cents per brick.

But you also have overheads: rent for your yard ($50 per month), depreciation on your mixer ($20 per month), and your own salary or profit. If you produce 4,000 bricks per month, your overhead per brick is ($50+$20)/4000 = $0.0175, roughly 2 cents. So your full cost per brick is about 27 cents. Now you know your break-even price.

To calculate your own cost, keep a notebook. Every time you buy materials, write down the date, quantity, and price. At the end of each week, count how many bricks you made. Divide total costs by number of bricks. That number is your true cost. Never sell below that. If you sell at 27 cents, you only cover costs; you make no profit. You need to add a profit margin. A common mistake is to forget fuel and labour, then wonder why money is missing. Always include everything.

Also, remember that costs change. Cement prices go up. Fuel prices rise. Recalculate your cost every month. If you use a brick machine, include maintenance and spare parts. If you dry bricks in the sun, your fuel cost is zero, but you still have labour to turn them. Be honest with yourself. Only then can you set a price that keeps you in business.

2. Setting a Competitive Price for Local Markets

Once you know your true cost per brick, you can set a price that is competitive but profitable. Start by checking what others charge. Visit local hardware stores, talk to builders, and ask other brick makers. In many African towns, common clay bricks sell for 30 to 50 cents each, while cement bricks may sell for 40 to 70 cents. Your price must be in that range, but not below your cost.

A simple formula: Price = Cost + Profit Margin. If your cost is 27 cents and you want a 30% profit margin, your price is 27 + (0.30 x 27) = 35 cents. That is your minimum selling price. You can round up to 40 cents for easy cash handling. But check the market: if everyone else sells at 35 cents, you may need to sell at 35 cents and find ways to reduce your cost. If your bricks are stronger or better shaped, you can charge a premium. For example, if you use a vibrating table and your bricks are uniform, builders may pay 50 cents.

Consider your target customer. Hardware stores buy in bulk and expect a wholesale discount. They might pay 30 cents per brick if they buy 5,000 at once. Direct builders might pay 40 cents for smaller orders. You can have two price lists: retail (small quantities) and wholesale (bulk). Always calculate your cost for each order to ensure you still profit.

Also, think about transport. If you deliver, include delivery in your price or charge separately. A common mistake is to quote a low price, then lose money on fuel. For example, delivering 1,000 bricks 10 km away might cost $20 in fuel and labour. That adds 2 cents per brick. So your delivered price should be 40 + 2 = 42 cents. Be transparent with customers. Show them the quality difference. In the rainy season, demand may drop, so you might lower prices slightly to keep cash flowing, but never below cost. In dry season, demand rises, so you can increase prices. Adjust to local conditions.

3. Finding Customers: Builders, Contractors, and Hardware Stores

You have bricks to sell, but who will buy them? Your customers are builders, contractors, and hardware stores. Each has different needs, so you must approach them differently. Start with local builders. Visit construction sites in your area. Introduce yourself to the foreman or site engineer. Show samples of your bricks. Explain your production capacity: how many bricks per day, your strength, and your price. Leave a business card or a written quote. Follow up after a few days. Builders often need a steady supply, so reliability matters.

Contractors work on larger projects like schools, hospitals, or roads. They buy in bulk and often require formal quotes and invoices. To win their business, you need to be professional. Prepare a simple quotation on paper with your company name, contact, brick type, quantity, unit price, and total. Include your delivery terms. Contractors may ask for credit, so be careful. Start with small orders to build trust. You can also register with local government tender boards to get notified of upcoming projects.

Hardware stores are another good outlet. They sell building materials to the public. Visit stores in your town and speak to the owner. Offer them a wholesale price, for example 30 cents per brick if they buy 5,000 or more. They will add their own profit and sell at retail. This gives you steady orders without chasing many small customers. However, hardware stores may want credit or consignment. Only agree if you can afford it.

Other customers include individual home builders, schools, churches, and NGOs. Advertise your bricks on local radio, WhatsApp groups, and notice boards. Word of mouth is powerful: give a small discount to customers who refer others. Keep a list of past customers and call them when you have a new batch. Always deliver on time and maintain quality. A happy customer will come back and tell others. In Africa, relationships are key. Be respectful, keep your promises, and your business will grow.

4. Negotiating Bulk Orders and Offering Discounts

When a customer wants to buy many bricks, they will ask for a discount. You need to negotiate wisely so you don't lose money. First, know your cost per brick. Suppose your cost is 27 cents. You can offer a discount only if you still make a profit. For example, for 10,000 bricks, you might reduce your price from 40 cents to 35 cents. That still gives you 8 cents profit per brick, or $800 total. But if you go below 30 cents, your profit is only 3 cents, which may not be worth the effort.

Before you negotiate, calculate your break-even point for the order. Consider extra costs: delivering 10,000 bricks may require two trips, costing $100 in fuel and labour. That adds 1 cent per brick. So your delivered cost is 28 cents. If you sell at 35 cents, your profit is 7 cents per brick, or $700. That is good. But if the customer demands 30 cents delivered, your profit is only 2 cents per brick, or $200. You might accept if you need the cash flow, but be careful.

Use a tiered discount structure. For example: 1-999 bricks: 40 cents each. 1,000-4,999: 38 cents. 5,000-9,999: 35 cents. 10,000+: 32 cents. This encourages larger orders. Always put the agreement in writing, even if it's a simple handwritten note signed by both parties. State the quantity, unit price, total, delivery date, and payment terms.

When negotiating, don't be afraid to say no. If the price is too low, politely explain that you cannot go below your cost. Offer alternatives: maybe they can pick up the bricks themselves to save delivery cost, or pay cash upfront for a better price. Sometimes a customer will ask for a discount but then pay late. To protect yourself, offer the discount only for upfront payment. For credit orders, charge a higher price to cover the risk. Remember, your goal is to build long-term relationships, not win one deal at a loss. Be firm but fair.

5. Delivering Bricks and Pavers to Construction Sites

Delivering bricks and pavers is part of the job, but it can eat your profits if not planned. First, decide if you will deliver or if customers will pick up. Many small makers offer delivery for a fee. Calculate your delivery cost: fuel, driver, and vehicle maintenance. For example, a pickup truck can carry about 1,000 bricks. If you deliver 5 km, fuel might cost $10, and driver $5, total $15. That is 1.5 cents per brick. So you should charge at least $20 for delivery to make a small profit.

When loading, stack bricks carefully to avoid breakage. Use wooden pallets or old tyres to cushion. Do not overload the vehicle; it is dangerous and can damage bricks. Tie the load with ropes. Drive slowly on rough roads. On arrival, help the customer unload. Count the bricks together with the customer or site manager. Get a signature on a delivery note. That note should list quantity, date, and any damages. Keep a copy for your records.

For large orders, you may need multiple trips. Plan your route to avoid traffic and bad roads. If the site is far, consider hiring a larger truck. A 7-tonne truck can carry about 3,000 bricks. Hiring might cost $100 per trip, but it saves time. Always agree on who pays for loading and unloading. Usually, the seller loads and the buyer unloads, but you can negotiate.

Also, think about the rainy season. Wet roads can make delivery difficult. Protect bricks with tarpaulins during transport. If you cannot deliver, offer a discount for customer pickup. Some customers prefer to arrange their own transport. In that case, you just load and they take responsibility. Always be clear about delivery times. If you promise a morning delivery, be there. Reliability builds trust. Keep a logbook of deliveries: date, customer, quantity, vehicle, and cost. That helps you calculate your true delivery cost and adjust your prices.

6. Handling Payments, Invoices, and Customer Credit

Getting paid is the most important part of selling. You can make all the bricks you want, but if customers don't pay, you have no business. Start by setting clear payment terms. For small retail sales, insist on cash on delivery. For larger orders, ask for a deposit, say 50% upfront, and the balance on delivery. This protects you from customers who disappear.

For contractors and hardware stores, you may need to offer credit. Before you do, check their reputation. Ask other suppliers if they pay on time. Get a written agreement stating the amount, interest if late, and payment date. Always provide an invoice. An invoice is a simple document with your business name, customer name, date, description of goods, quantity, unit price, total, and payment terms. You can buy an invoice book from a stationery shop or make your own. Number each invoice sequentially. Keep a copy.

If a customer fails to pay, follow up politely. Send a reminder after the due date. If they still don't pay, visit them in person. In Africa, personal relationships matter, so be respectful but firm. You can offer a small discount if they pay immediately. If they continue to delay, stop supplying them and consider legal action, though that can be costly. Prevention is better: don't give credit to new customers unless they pay a deposit.

Keep good records. Use a cash book to record all money in and out. Note who owes you and how much. At the end of each month, review your debtors. If a customer owes you more than 10% of your monthly sales, you may have a cash flow problem. You can also use mobile money for payments, which is fast and safe. Many customers prefer to pay via M-Pesa or similar services. Always give a receipt. A receipt shows you received payment and avoids disputes. Remember, your business runs on cash. The faster you collect, the more bricks you can make.

<h2>Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing, and Selling Your Bricks and Pavers</h2>

<p>This practical reference is a working tool for brick and paver producers in Africa who want to move from "I think I'm making money" to "I know my numbers." It covers the full commercial chain: calculating true cost per unit, setting prices that win local contracts without destroying margin, finding buyers, negotiating bulk orders, delivering to site, and managing payments and credit. The focus is on decisions you can act on this week, not theory.</p>

<p>Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, construction demand is rising, but so is competition from both formal factories and informal yard producers. The producers who survive are those who understand their cost structure and price with discipline. A common failure mode is pricing by matching a neighbour's rate without knowing whether that neighbour is covering fuel, labour, and transport. This module gives you the structure to avoid that trap.</p>

<h3>Step 1: Calculating the True Cost per Brick</h3>

<p>Your selling price means nothing until you know your cost per unit. True cost includes materials, labour, fuel or energy, water, moulds and equipment wear, transport, and a share of overheads. Many small producers only count cement and sand, then wonder why cash disappears. Build a cost sheet per batch, then divide by the number of saleable units after rejects.</p>

<p>Worked example. Suppose a batch uses 10 bags of cement at KES 750 each (KES 7,500), 3 tonnes of sand at KES 1,200 (KES 3,600), water and additives KES 400, fuel for curing KES 900, and 4 casual workers for one day at KES 800 each (KES 3,200). Equipment wear and overheads add KES 1,400. Total batch cost = KES 17,000. If the batch yields 1,200 good bricks after a 5% reject rate, cost per brick = KES 14.17. That is your floor. Anything you sell below it is a loss, no matter how busy you feel.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Setting a Competitive Price for Local Markets</h3>

<p>Price has three reference points: your cost floor, the local market rate, and the value you offer (strength, consistency, delivery, credit terms). If your cost floor is above the market rate, you must cut cost or differentiate; you cannot win on price alone. Differentiation in African markets often comes from reliability bricks delivered on the agreed day, uniform size, and a producer who answers the phone.</p>

<p>Set two prices: ex-yard (customer collects) and delivered (you transport). Delivered pricing must include fuel, driver, loading and offloading labour, and vehicle wear. Never quote delivered at the ex-yard price.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Finding Customers Builders, Contractors, and Hardware Stores</h3>

<p>Your best customers are repeat buyers with predictable demand. Build a simple customer list with name, type, typical order, payment behaviour, and last contact date. Visit active construction sites in your area weekly and speak to the site foreman or clerk of works. Hardware stores give you volume and steady cash but lower margin; contractors give bigger orders but often demand credit.</p>

<p>Practical outreach: register your business, get a simple one-page price list, and use WhatsApp Business to share photos and prices. Attend local contractor association meetings and county tender briefings. Word of mouth from one satisfied foreman is worth more than any advertisement.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Negotiating Bulk Orders and Offering Discounts</h3>

<p>Discounts must be earned, not given away. Structure them on volume and payment terms. Example: 5% off for orders above 5,000 pieces paid on delivery; 10% off for above 20,000 pieces with 50% deposit. Never discount below your cost floor. When a buyer pushes for a lower price, offer a smaller quantity at the lower rate or a longer delivery window instead of cutting margin.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Delivering Bricks and Pavers to Construction Sites</h3>

<p>Delivery is where hidden costs live. Calculate cost per trip: fuel, driver, loader, offloader, and vehicle maintenance. Confirm site access can a lorry reach the offloading point? Poor access means more labour and time. Always agree who offloads and where bricks are stacked, and get the delivery note signed on site.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0; color:#b00020; font-weight:bold;"> SAFETY WARNING Manual Handling and Vehicle Loading</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#7a0016;">Loading and offloading bricks and pavers causes serious back, hand, and foot injuries. Workers have been killed by falling stacks and by vehicles rolling during loading. Always: (1) use gloves and closed safety shoes; (2) train workers to lift with legs, not the back, and to work in pairs on heavy units; (3) chock vehicle wheels and switch off engines before loading; (4) never stack bricks above shoulder height on unstable ground; (5) keep children and bystanders clear of loading zones. In many African jurisdictions, employers are legally required to provide basic PPE under national occupational safety and health laws (for example, Kenya's Occupational Safety and Health Act, 2007).</p>
</div>

<h3>Step 6: Handling Payments, Invoices, and Customer Credit</h3>

<p>Cash flow kills more brick businesses than low margins. Use a numbered invoice for every sale showing quantity, unit price, total, deposit paid, and balance. For credit customers, set a written limit and payment date, take a deposit, and keep a simple ledger. Mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) reduces cash handling risk and gives you a transaction record. Follow up overdue balances within seven days politely but consistently.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Recalculate cost per brick every time cement, fuel, or labour prices change.</li>
<li>Know your cost floor and never sell below it, even for bulk orders.</li>
<li>Quote ex-yard and delivered prices separately; never mix them.</li>
<li>Keep a customer list with order history and payment behaviour.</li>
<li>Structure discounts on volume and payment terms, not on pressure.</li>
<li>Calculate delivery cost per trip before quoting a delivered price.</li>
<li>Use numbered invoices and accept mobile money for traceable payments.</li>
<li>Set written credit limits and follow up overdue balances within 7 days.</li>
</ul>
