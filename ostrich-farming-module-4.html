1. Nutritional Requirements by Age and Production Stage

Ostriches have different nutritional needs as they grow and when they are breeding. A newly hatched chick weighs about 1 kg and needs a high-protein, high-energy diet to grow fast. From day 1 to 8 weeks, feed a starter mash with 20-22% protein and 2,800-3,000 kcal/kg metabolizable energy. You can buy commercial ostrich starter or mix your own. At this stage, they also need plenty of calcium (1.2-1.5%) and available phosphorus (0.6-0.8%) for strong bones. A common mistake is feeding chicken starter, which lacks the right balance and can cause leg problems.

From 8 to 24 weeks, growers need 16-18% protein and 2,600-2,800 kcal/kg. They are no longer growing as fast, so you can reduce protein but keep energy up for steady weight gain. From 24 weeks to slaughter (usually 10-14 months), finishers need 14-16% protein and 2,500-2,700 kcal/kg. If you are raising breeders, from 24 weeks onward they need 14-16% protein but with higher calcium (2.5-3.5%) for eggshell production. Breeders also need extra vitamin E and selenium for fertility.

During laying season, a hen produces 40-80 eggs. She needs a diet with 16-18% protein, 2,700-2,800 kcal/kg, and 3-4% calcium. If calcium is low, she will use her own bone calcium and become weak. In Africa, where heat stress is common, add 0.5-1% sodium bicarbonate to the feed or water to help with eggshell quality. Always provide free-choice oyster shell or limestone grit.

Water is critical: an adult ostrich drinks 8-12 liters per day in hot weather. Make sure water is clean and cool. Adjust feed amounts based on body condition: a thin bird needs more, a fat breeder may need less to avoid egg-laying problems. Weigh a sample of birds every month using a livestock scale or a bathroom scale (weigh yourself, then hold the bird). Record weights to track progress.

2. Feed Ingredients: Alfalfa, Corn, Soybean Meal, and Supplements

The main ingredients for ostrich feed in Africa are alfalfa (lucerne), corn (maize), soybean meal, and mineral-vitamin supplements. Alfalfa is a legume hay or meal, high in protein (15-20%) and calcium. It is excellent for breeders and growers. You can buy alfalfa pellets or chop dried alfalfa. In countries like South Africa, alfalfa is common; in West Africa, you may use moringa leaves or cowpea hay as alternatives. Always ensure alfalfa is not moldymold causes health problems.

Corn is the main energy source. It has about 3,300 kcal/kg and 8-9% protein. Use yellow corn for extra vitamin A. Grind it to a medium texture; too fine causes dust, too coarse causes sorting. In East Africa, you can use maize bran or hominy chop as a cheaper energy source, but they have more fiber and less energy. Soybean meal is the best plant protein, with 44-48% protein. It must be toasted or heat-treated to destroy trypsin inhibitors, which block protein digestion. If soybean meal is expensive, use cottonseed meal (but limit to 10% because of gossypol toxicity) or sunflower meal (30-35% protein).

Supplements are essential. A good premix should provide vitamins A, D3, E, K, B-complex, and minerals like calcium, phosphorus, zinc, manganese, copper, iodine, and selenium. For ostriches, pay special attention to calcium and phosphorus in a 2:1 ratio. Add salt (0.3-0.5%) for sodium and chloride. In hot climates, add extra vitamin C (200-300 mg/kg) and electrolytes. You can buy commercial ostrich premixes or make your own using agricultural supply stores.

A simple starter mix for 100 kg: 45 kg corn, 25 kg soybean meal, 15 kg alfalfa meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.2 kg premix. Mix thoroughly on a clean concrete floor using a shovel, or use a small feed mixer. Store in dry, ventilated sacks away from rodents. Never use spoiled or wet ingredients.

3. Formulating Rations for Chicks, Growers, and Breeders

Formulating rations means mixing ingredients to meet the bird's needs at the lowest cost. You can use the Pearson Square method for simple two-ingredient mixes, or software like Excel. For chicks (0-8 weeks), aim for 20-22% protein. Example for 100 kg: 40 kg corn, 30 kg soybean meal, 10 kg alfalfa meal, 10 kg fishmeal (60% protein), 5 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.2 kg premix. This gives about 21% protein and 2,900 kcal/kg. Mix well and feed as crumbles or mash.

For growers (8-24 weeks), reduce protein to 16-18%. Example: 50 kg corn, 20 kg soybean meal, 15 kg alfalfa meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.2 kg premix. This gives about 17% protein. You can replace some soybean meal with sunflower meal to cut costs, but adjust for lower lysine. Add 0.1% methionine if using sunflower meal. For finishers (24 weeks to slaughter), use 55 kg corn, 15 kg soybean meal, 15 kg alfalfa meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.2 kg premix. This gives 15% protein.

For breeders, protein should be 16-18% but calcium much higher (3-4%). Example: 45 kg corn, 20 kg soybean meal, 15 kg alfalfa meal, 10 kg wheat bran, 6 kg limestone, 2 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.2 kg premix, and 1 kg oyster shell. Also add 0.1 kg vitamin E-selenium premix. During laying, provide free-choice oyster shell. Always weigh ingredients accurately using a scale; a 10% error in premix can be toxic. Mix for at least 5 minutes until uniform color.

Test your mix by sending a sample to a laboratory for protein and calcium analysis. In Africa, many agricultural universities offer this service for a small fee. Keep records of formulations and bird performance. Adjust based on weight gain and egg production. If birds are too thin, increase energy; if too fat, reduce energy. Never change ration suddenlytransition over 5-7 days to avoid digestive upset.

4. Feeding Schedules and Methods

Ostriches are grazers and need a routine. Feed them at the same times daily: early morning (6-7 am) and late afternoon (4-5 pm) when it is cooler. Chicks (0-8 weeks) need feed available all day; use shallow troughs to prevent spillage. Feed 4-5 times a day for the first 2 weeks, then 3 times. A chick eats about 100-150 g per day by week 4, increasing to 300-400 g by week 8. Use feeders that are easy to clean and have lips to stop birds from stepping in feed.

Growers (8-24 weeks) can be fed twice daily. Give about 1-1.5 kg per bird per day of grower mash. If they are on pasture, reduce to 0.8-1 kg because they graze grass and insects. Use linear troughs 1.5 m long for every 10 birds. Place feeders away from water to keep feed dry. Breeders need feed once or twice daily. A laying hen eats 1.5-2 kg per day. Provide feed in the afternoon to help eggshell formation overnight. Always provide fresh water before feeding.

Methods: You can feed on the ground, but this causes waste and disease. Better to use troughs or self-feeders. For large farms, use automatic chain feeders. On small farms, use plastic or wooden troughs. Clean troughs daily to prevent mold. For free-range systems, rotate pastures to avoid parasite buildup. In Africa, many farmers use a 'feed call'a whistle or shoutto train birds to come to the feeding area. This makes management easier.

Monitor feed intake: if birds leave feed, they may be sick. Weigh feed offered and leftover to calculate intake. A sudden drop means stress or disease. Adjust feed based on body condition score: run your hand along the breastbone; if it's sharp, increase feed; if you can't feel it, reduce. Provide grit (small stones) for digestiona handful per bird per week. In hot weather, feed early and late, and provide shade. Never feed moldy or wet feed. Store feed in a cool, dry place for no more than 4 weeks.

5. Water Quality and Supply

Water is the most important nutrient. An ostrich can survive weeks without food but only days without water. A chick drinks 0.5-1 liter per day; a grower 3-5 liters; an adult 8-12 liters in hot weather. In Africa, where temperatures often exceed 35C, water intake can double. Always provide clean, fresh water. If water smells or looks dirty, birds won't drink enough. Use a simple test: fill a glass, look through itif you can't see clearly, clean the system.

Water quality: test for bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella), minerals (iron, manganese), and pH. Ideal pH is 6.5-8.5. High iron (above 0.3 mg/L) can cause slime and bad taste. If you use borehole water, test annually. For surface water (dams, rivers), treat with chlorine at 2-5 mg/L, but let it stand for 30 minutes before giving to birds. You can also use hydrogen peroxide or UV filters. In rural areas, boiling water is not practical for large flocks, but for chicks you can use boiled and cooled water.

Supply systems: Use plastic drinkers for chicksclean and refill 3-4 times daily. For older birds, use automatic bell drinkers or troughs. Troughs must be cleaned daily; a dirty trough grows algae and bacteria. Place drinkers in shade; cool water is more appealing. In hot weather, add ice blocks to large troughs or use insulated containers. Ensure drinkers are at the right height: chick drinkers on the ground, adult drinkers at 60-70 cm so birds don't step in them.

Emergency supply: if water is cut, have a reserve tank. Store 50 liters per adult bird for one day. In drought, you can offer succulent plants like cactus or watermelon as a water source, but not as a replacement. Always have a backup plan. Dehydration signs: sunken eyes, dry skin, lethargy. If you see these, offer water with electrolytes immediately. Prevention is simple: check water twice daily, clean drinkers, and never let them run dry.

6. Common Nutritional Disorders and Prevention

Nutritional disorders in ostriches are common when feeding is unbalanced. The most frequent is leg weakness in chicks, caused by calcium-phosphorus imbalance or vitamin D3 deficiency. Signs: chicks sit a lot, legs bend, and they struggle to walk. Prevent by feeding a starter with 1.2-1.5% calcium and 0.6-0.8% available phosphorus, and ensure vitamin D3 (2000-3000 IU/kg). If you see signs, add limestone or dicalcium phosphate to the feed and provide sunlight (birds synthesize vitamin D from UV light).

Another disorder is curled toe paralysis, caused by riboflavin (vitamin B2) deficiency. Chicks walk on their hocks and toes curl inward. Prevent by adding a B-complex vitamin premix. In Africa, where maize is a staple, riboflavin can be low; supplement with 4-6 mg/kg. Also, watch for perosis (slipped tendon) due to manganese or choline deficiency. Add manganese sulfate (100 g/ton) and choline chloride (500 g/ton) to the premix.

In breeders, fatty liver syndrome can occur if energy is too high and protein too low. Hens become fat, lay fewer eggs, and may die. Prevent by keeping energy at 2,700-2,800 kcal/kg and protein at 16-18%. Also, provide methionine and choline. Eggshell defects like thin shells or soft shells are due to calcium deficiency or excess phosphorus. Ensure calcium is 3-4% and phosphorus 0.4-0.5%. If shells are rough, add vitamin C (200 mg/kg) and zinc (80 mg/kg).

Gout is caused by too much protein (above 20%) in adults, leading to uric acid deposits in joints. Reduce protein to 14-16% and provide plenty of water. In hot weather, heat stress reduces feed intake and causes electrolyte loss. Add sodium bicarbonate (1 kg/ton) and potassium chloride (1 kg/ton) to feed or water. Always transition feeds over 5-7 days to avoid digestive upsets. Keep records: if you see a problem, check the feed formulation first. Many disorders are prevented by using a good commercial premix and testing your ingredients. Consult a veterinarian or extension officer if problems persist.

<h2>Practical Reference: Ostrich Nutrition &amp; Feeding for African Producers</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core decisions an ostrich producer must make daily: what to feed, how much, in what form, and at what cost per bird per day. Ostrich farming in Africa is a business before it is a hobby. Feed represents <strong>6075% of total production cost</strong> in intensive ostrich systems, so every gram of feed must convert into live weight gain, egg output, or feather and skin value. This module gives you the numbers, the ingredients, and the schedules to run a profitable operation from chick to breeder.</p>

<p>Ostriches are not chickens. They are hindgut fermenters with a long, efficient large intestine that extracts energy from fibre far better than poultry. This single biological fact drives every formulation decision below: you can and should use cheaper fibrous ingredients alfalfa, lucerne hay, crop residues that monogastric poultry cannot use. That is your competitive cost advantage in Africa.</p>

<h3>3. Formulating Rations for Chicks, Growers, and Breeders</h3>

<p>You do not need a feed mill to formulate. You need a scale, a calculator, and a fixed set of ingredients. Below are three practical rations you can mix on-farm using ingredients available in most African markets. All figures are percentages by weight.</p>

<p><strong>Chick Starter (08 weeks):</strong> Maize meal 45%, soybean meal 28%, lucerne meal 8%, wheat bran 12%, fish meal 4%, limestone 1.2%, dicalcium phosphate 1.2%, premix 0.4%, salt 0.2%. Crude protein 20%. Mix fresh every 7 days; store in dry, ventilated bags.</p>

<p><strong>Grower Ration (824 weeks):</strong> Maize meal 38%, soybean meal 18%, lucerne meal 18%, wheat bran 20%, fish meal 2%, limestone 1.5%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, premix 0.4%, salt 0.2%. Crude protein 16%. This ration uses more fibre and less protein exactly what the ostrich caeca are built for.</p>

<p><strong>Breeder Ration:</strong> Maize meal 40%, soybean meal 20%, lucerne meal 20%, wheat bran 12%, limestone 4%, dicalcium phosphate 2%, premix 0.5%, salt 0.3%. Crude protein 17%, calcium 2.8%. Breeder hens need this high calcium for eggshell formation; without it, you will see thin-shelled eggs, broken eggs, and reduced hatchability.</p>

<p><strong>Cost tip:</strong> In many African markets, lucerne meal is cheaper per unit of protein than soybean meal. Push lucerne to the upper limit of each ration (up to 22% for growers) before buying more soybean. This is the single fastest way to cut feed cost.</p>

<h3>4. Feeding Schedules and Methods</h3>

<p>Ostriches are continuous grazers. In the wild they spend 6070% of daylight hours foraging. In captivity, this means you should feed small amounts frequently rather than one large meal. A practical schedule:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Chicks (08 weeks):</strong> Feed 45 times daily, starting at 06:00. Provide feed in shallow trays. Never let feed sit more than 12 hours in hot weather it moulds.</li>
<li><strong>Growers (824 weeks):</strong> Feed 3 times daily. Use troughs with a lip to prevent spillage. Allow 1520 cm of trough space per bird.</li>
<li><strong>Finishers and adults:</strong> Feed 2 times daily (morning and late afternoon). Supplement with chopped lucerne or pasture grazing between meals.</li>
<li><strong>Breeders:</strong> Feed 2 times daily, plus free-choice oyster shell in a separate hopper. Do not mix oyster shell into the ration hens will self-regulate.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Method matters as much as formulation.</strong> Ostriches are messy eaters. Use troughs with a rolled lip and a height of 6070 cm for adults. Feed on the ground only for chicks, and only on clean, dry surfaces. Wet feed grows Aspergillus and Clostridium both can kill birds within 48 hours.</p>

<h3>5. Water Quality and Supply</h3>

<p>An adult ostrich drinks 812 litres of water per day in hot African conditions. Chicks drink 0.51.5 litres. Water is not optional it is the cheapest input and the most common cause of poor performance. A bird that drinks 20% less than it should will eat 1520% less and grow 20% slower.</p>

<p><strong>Quality standards:</strong> Total dissolved solids below 3,000 ppm; nitrate below 100 ppm; pH between 6.5 and 8.5. In many African boreholes, nitrate and salinity exceed these limits. Test your water at least twice yearly at a government or university laboratory. If nitrate is high, provide an alternative source for chicks and breeders they are most sensitive.</p>

<p><strong>Supply:</strong> Provide water in clean, shallow troughs that birds cannot step into. Ostriches defecate in water; this is a major disease pathway. Elevate troughs to 4050 cm for growers and 6070 cm for adults. Clean and refill at least twice daily. In temperatures above 35C, check water three times daily.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b30000;background:#fff0f0;padding:16px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-size:16px;font-weight:bold;color:#b30000;"> SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE FEEDING</p>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:14px;color:#4a0000;"><strong>Never feed mouldy maize, damp hay, or spoiled silage to ostriches.</strong> Mouldy feed produces aflatoxins and mycotoxins that cause liver damage, sudden death, and permanent stunting. In Africa's humid and hot conditions, feed spoils within 2448 hours of exposure to moisture. Always smell your feed before use a musty or sour smell means discard it immediately. <strong>Do not feed cottonseed cake above 5% inclusion</strong> gossypol accumulates in the liver and reduces fertility in breeders. <strong>Never feed poultry layer mash designed for chickens</strong> its calcium and energy profile is wrong for ostriches and can cause kidney damage in chicks. When in doubt, throw it out. A bag of feed costs far less than a dead breeder.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Common Nutritional Disorders and Prevention</h3>

<p>Most nutritional problems in African ostrich farms are not exotic deficiencies they are management failures. The five most common:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Leg weakness / slipped tendon in chicks:</strong> Caused by calcium-phosphorus imbalance or vitamin D3 deficiency. Prevent by using a correct chick starter and providing 23 hours of direct sunlight daily (or supplemental D3 in premix).</li>
<li><strong>Impacted gizzard:</strong> Caused by feeding coarse, fibrous feed to chicks under 4 weeks, or by birds eating sand, wire, or plastic. Prevent by grinding all ingredients finely for chicks and keeping pens clear of foreign objects.</li>
<li><strong>Thin-shelled eggs in breeders:</strong> Calcium deficiency or excess phosphorus. Prevent by feeding the breeder ration above and providing free-choice oyster shell.</li>
<li><strong>Rickets in growers:</strong> Vitamin D3 or phosphorus deficiency. Prevent by ensuring premix is fresh (vitamins degrade in storage) and by providing sunlight.</li>
<li><strong>Aflatoxicosis:</strong> Mouldy feed. Prevent by buying fresh maize, storing in dry conditions, and never using feed older than 30 days.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-size:15px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Weigh your feed.</strong> Do not estimate. A 10% overfeed on 100 birds wastes enough money to buy a new water trough every month.</li>
<li><strong>Match protein to age.</strong> Chicks need 2022% CP; finishers need 1214%. Feeding chick starter to finishers wastes money and causes fatty carcasses.</li>
<li><strong>Push lucerne to the limit.</strong> Up to 22% in grower rations. It is your cheapest protein and fibre source.</li>
<li><strong>Test your water twice a year.</strong> Nitrate and salinity above safe limits silently cut growth by 1520%.</li>
<li><strong>Feed 25 times daily depending on age.</strong> Never one large meal.</li>
<li><strong>Smell every bag of feed before use.</strong> Musty = discard. No exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>Provide free-choice oyster shell to breeders.</strong> Separate hopper, never mixed into the ration.</li>
<li><strong>Clean water troughs twice daily.</strong> Ostriches defecate in water; this is the number one disease pathway on most farms.</li>
</ol>
