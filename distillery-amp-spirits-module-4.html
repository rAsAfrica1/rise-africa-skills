Lessons

1. Mashing maize and sorghum with local malted grains

To make a good mash from maize or sorghum, you need to gelatinize the starch first. Crack 5 kg of maize or sorghum into a coarse grit using a hammer mill or a clean mortar and pestle. In a large pot, bring 20 liters of water to a boil. Slowly add the cracked grain while stirring to avoid lumps. Cook for 45"60 minutes until you get a thick porridge. This breaks down the starch so enzymes can reach it. Let the porridge cool to 65"68C. If you don't have a thermometer, drip a little on your wrist"it should feel very hot but not scalding. Now add your malted grain. You can malt your own sorghum or maize: soak grain for 24 hours, spread it in a thin layer, keep it damp, and let it sprout for 3"5 days until the sprouts are 1"2 cm. Then dry and crush it. Use 1 kg of malted grain for every 5 kg of unmalted grain. Stir the malt into the porridge and hold at 65C for 1"2 hours. The enzymes in the malt convert starch to sugar. Test with iodine: a drop of mash on a white plate with a drop of iodine should not turn blue-black; if it does, keep mashing longer. After conversion, cool the mash to 30C, add yeast, and ferment for 3"5 days. This mash can be distilled in a pot still. For sorghum, you may need to add a commercial enzyme or use more malt because sorghum starch is harder to gelatinize. Always use clean water and sanitize your tools to avoid spoilage.

2. Using cassava (garri) for a smooth, gluten-free spirit

Cassava, especially in the form of garri, is a great raw material for a gluten-free spirit. Garri is already partially gelatinized and dried, so it's easier to work with than raw cassava. Start with 5 kg of fine garri (white or yellow). In a large pot, mix the garri with 20 liters of water to form a smooth slurry. Heat the mixture to 75"80C while stirring constantly. Do not boil vigorously; you want to gelatinize the starch without burning. Hold at that temperature for 20 minutes. The mixture will thicken. Now cool it to 65C. Add 1 kg of crushed malted grain (barley, sorghum, or maize) or 5 ml of commercial alpha-amylase enzyme. Stir well and keep at 65C for 1"2 hours. The enzymes will break down the starch into fermentable sugars. Check conversion with an iodine test: if it turns blue-black, continue mashing. Once converted, cool to 30C and add yeast. Ferment for 4"6 days. Cassava mash can foam a lot, so leave headspace in your fermenter. After fermentation, distill in a pot still. Discard the first 50 ml of distillate (foreshots) as they contain methanol. Collect the middle run (heart) until the alcohol strength drops below 20% ABV. Cassava spirit is smooth and slightly sweet. Note: raw cassava contains cyanogenic compounds, but garri processing removes most of them. Still, always use food-grade garri and avoid bitter varieties. This method yields about 1.5"2 liters of 40% spirit per 5 kg of garri, depending on efficiency.

3. Extracting juice from sugarcane for rum-style spirits

Sugarcane is the classic raw material for rum. To extract juice, you need a cane crusher or a strong press. If you don't have a crusher, you can chop the cane into small pieces and pound it in a wooden mortar, then squeeze the pulp through a clean cloth. A more efficient way is to use a manual sugarcane juicer, which can be bought or rented in many African markets. For a small batch, take 20 kg of fresh sugarcane. Remove the leaves and cut the stalks into 30 cm lengths. Pass them through the crusher, collecting the juice in a clean bucket. You should get about 10"12 liters of juice. Strain the juice through a cloth to remove bits of bagasse. Now measure the sugar content with a refractometer or a hydrometer. You want a Brix reading of 12"18%. If it's higher, add clean water; if lower, add a little sugar. Adjust the pH to 4.5"5.0 using lemon juice or citric acid. Boil the juice for 10 minutes to sterilize it, then cool to 30C. Add yeast (1 g per liter) and ferment for 3"5 days. The juice will bubble and then settle. Once fermentation stops, distill in a pot still. Rum gets much of its flavor from the cane juice and from aging. For a white rum, distill twice and filter through charcoal. For a golden rum, age in charred oak chips for a few weeks. You can also use molasses, a byproduct of sugar production, which is cheaper and gives a darker rum. But fresh cane juice gives a more aromatic spirit. Always clean your equipment to avoid bacterial contamination.

4. Preparing palm sap for distillation (toddy)

Palm sap, also called toddy, is a traditional African beverage that can be distilled into a strong spirit. You can tap sap from oil palm or raffia palm. The sap is collected from the inflorescence (flower stalk) before it opens. A tapper cuts the tip of the stalk and ties a calabash or plastic container to collect the dripping sap. This is usually done early morning and evening. Fresh sap is sweet and ferments quickly. To prepare it for distillation, you need to ferment it. If you want a consistent product, you can pasteurize the sap first: heat it to 70C for 10 minutes to kill wild yeasts, then cool and add a known yeast strain. But traditional toddy is often left to ferment naturally for 1"2 days. The sap contains 10"15% sugar. It will ferment to about 5"8% alcohol. For distillation, you need at least 5 liters of fermented sap. Distill in a pot still. Discard the first 50 ml (foreshots) and collect the heart until the distillate drops below 20% ABV. The result is a clear, strong spirit, often called "palm wine brandy" or "toddy spirit". In some countries, it's illegal to distill toddy without a license, so check local laws. Also, palm sap can contain bacteria that produce off-flavors, so keep everything clean. If you want a smoother spirit, distill twice. You can also add a little sugar to the sap before fermentation to increase yield. But be careful: too much sugar can stress the yeast. A good yield is about 1 liter of 40% spirit from 10 liters of sap.

5. Enzymatic conversion with termite hill enzymes or commercial alpha-amylase

Enzymes are the key to breaking starch into sugar. In many African communities, termite hills are a source of natural enzymes. The termite hill material contains bacteria and fungi that produce amylase. To use it, collect dry, fresh termite hill soil (about 1 kg). Mix it with 5 liters of warm water (60C) and let it steep for 2 hours. Strain the liquid through a cloth. This liquid contains enzymes. Add this to your cooled mash (65C) and stir. However, termite hill enzymes are less predictable than commercial ones. For reliable results, use commercial alpha-amylase. It's sold as a liquid or powder in brewing shops or online. Dosage: 1 ml of liquid alpha-amylase per 10 kg of grain, or 1 g of powder per 10 kg. Add it when the mash is at 65"70C. For glucoamylase (which breaks down dextrins to glucose), add it at 60C after alpha-amylase. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions. You can also use malted grain as an enzyme source, as described in Lesson 1. To test enzyme activity, do an iodine test. If the mash turns blue-black, enzymes are still needed. If it stays brownish, conversion is complete. Remember that enzymes are proteins and can be denatured by high heat. So never add them to boiling mash. Also, adjust pH to 5.0"5.5 for best activity. If using termite hill enzymes, you may need to add more or combine with a little malt. Commercial enzymes are more expensive but give consistent yields. For a small distillery, it's worth investing in a bottle of alpha-amylase and glucoamylase.

6. Calculating mash efficiency and yield per kilogram of grain

Mash efficiency tells you how much sugar you actually extracted from the grain compared to the maximum possible. To calculate it, you need to measure the specific gravity (SG) of your mash before fermentation. Use a hydrometer. For example, if you mashed 5 kg of maize, the theoretical maximum extract is about 80% of the grain's weight as sugar. Maize is about 70% starch, and each kg of starch yields 1.11 kg of sugar. So 5 kg maize gives 5 * 0.70 * 1.11 = 3.885 kg of sugar. If you dissolve that in 20 liters of water, the total volume is about 23 liters (since sugar adds volume). The SG would be about 1.065 (using the formula: SG = 1 + (sugar in kg / (volume in L * 0.004))). But in reality, you might get SG 1.050. Your efficiency is (actual sugar / theoretical sugar) * 100. Actual sugar = (SG - 1) * volume * 1000 / 4. For SG 1.050 and 23 L, actual sugar = 0.050 * 23 * 1000 / 4 = 287.5 g? Wait, that's not right. The correct formula: sugar (g/L) = (SG - 1) * 1000 * 2.5? Actually, the extract in points per gallon (PPG) is common. But for simplicity, use a refractometer or hydrometer chart. A simpler method: after fermentation, measure the alcohol by volume (ABV) with a hydrometer or by distillation. Each kg of sugar yields about 0.51 kg of ethanol. So if you got 3 kg of sugar, you get 1.53 kg ethanol, which is about 1.94 liters of pure alcohol. That's about 4.85 liters of 40% spirit. So yield per kg of grain: for 5 kg maize, you might get 4 liters of 40% spirit. That's 0.8 L per kg. If you get less, your efficiency is low. To improve, grind finer, cook longer, use more enzymes, and control pH. Keep records of grain weight, water volume, SG, and final spirit volume. This helps you calculate cost and profit. For cassava, yield is similar but depends on starch content. For sugarcane, yield is based on sugar content of juice. Always aim for at least 70% efficiency.

<h2>Practical Reference: Measuring Mash Efficiency and Spirit Yield in African Distilleries</h2>

<p>Every litre of spirit that leaves your still represents money spent on grain, fuel, labour and time. Yet most small and medium distilleries in Africa run their mash house on instinct rather than measurement. This reference lesson gives you a simple, field-tested framework to calculate how efficiently your mash converts starch into fermentable sugar, and how much saleable spirit each kilogram of raw material actually delivers. Master these numbers and you can negotiate better grain prices, spot theft and spoilage faster, and price your bottles with confidence.</p>

<p>Whether you are mashing maize, sorghum, cassava (garri), sugarcane juice or palm sap, the underlying logic is identical: starch or sugar in, ethanol out. The variables are the conversion losses in between. This lesson focuses on the two metrics that matter most to your bottom line <strong>mash efficiency</strong> and <strong>yield per kilogram</strong> and shows you how to track them with equipment you already own.</p>

<h3>Why Efficiency Is a Business Metric, Not a Technical Curiosity</h3>

<p>If your mash efficiency drops from 85% to 70%, you have just lost 15% of your grain budget to waste and grain is often 4060% of production cost in African distilleries. On a 500 kg daily maize mash at KES 55/kg, that gap is roughly KES 4,100 per day, or over KES 1.2 million a year. Measuring efficiency weekly turns this hidden loss into a visible, fixable number.</p>

<h3>How to Calculate Mash Efficiency</h3>

<p>The formula is simple and works for any feedstock:</p>

<p style="background:#eef5e6;padding:12px;border-left:4px solid #7a9e3f;font-family:monospace;">
Mash efficiency (%) = (Actual ethanol produced Theoretical ethanol possible) 100
</p>

<p><strong>Worked example 100 kg maize mash:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Maize starch content: 72% 72 kg starch</li>
<li>Theoretical glucose from starch: 72 1.11 = 79.9 kg glucose</li>
<li>Theoretical ethanol from glucose: 79.9 0.511 = 40.8 kg ethanol</li>
<li>Ethanol density at 20 C: 0.789 kg/L 51.7 L pure ethanol</li>
<li>You distil and measure 38 L of pure ethanol equivalent (accounting for proof)</li>
<li>Mash efficiency = (38 51.7) 100 = <strong>73.5%</strong></li>
</ol>

<p>A well-run African distillery using commercial alpha-amylase and glucoamylase typically hits 8088%. Traditional malted-grain or termite-hill enzyme mashes often sit at 6575%. Anything below 60% signals a problem: under-milled grain, wrong mash pH, insufficient enzyme, or a stuck fermentation.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING METHANOL AND DISTILLATION HAZARDS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Distilling any fermented mash concentrates not only ethanol but also <strong>methanol</strong>, a highly toxic alcohol that can cause blindness or death at doses as low as 10 mL. Methanol boils at 64.7 C, lower than ethanol (78.4 C), so it comes off in the <strong>heads</strong> (foreshots). <strong>Never</strong> sell or consume the first 50100 mL per 20 L of wash. Discard heads safely do not pour into drains, soil or animal feed. Also: never distil in a sealed vessel (pressure explosion risk), never use lead-soldered or galvanised containers (lead and zinc poisoning), and always distil in a well-ventilated area away from open flames. In Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania and Nigeria, production of spirits for sale requires a licence from the respective revenue authority and compliance with KEBS, UNBS, TBS or NAFDAC standards. WHO (2014), <em>Global Status Report on Alcohol and Health</em>, documents methanol poisoning outbreaks linked to informal spirits across Africa.</p>

<h3>Tracking Efficiency in Your Own Distillery</h3>

<p>You do not need a laboratory. You need three measurements taken consistently:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Specific gravity (SG) of the mash before fermentation.</strong> A hydrometer costs under USD 10. Record the reading.</li>
<li><strong>SG after fermentation.</strong> The drop tells you how much sugar was consumed. A stuck ferment shows a small drop.</li>
<li><strong>Volume and strength of distillate.</strong> Use an alcoholmeter (hydrometer calibrated for ethanol) and record total pure alcohol equivalent.</li>
</ol>

<p>Log these three numbers for every batch in a simple notebook or spreadsheet. After 20 batches you will know your true baseline, and any deviation becomes an early warning of spoilage, enzyme failure or ingredient substitution.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>1. Weigh every batch of grain.</strong> Eyeballing sacks is the number one source of yield error.</li>
<li><strong>2. Test SG before pitching yeast and after fermentation ends.</strong> Two readings, every batch, no exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>3. Mill grain to the right particle size.</strong> Too coarse = unconverted starch; too fine = stuck sparge and burnt mash.</li>
<li><strong>4. Verify mash pH between 5.0 and 5.5</strong> before adding enzymes outside this range, conversion collapses.</li>
<li><strong>5. Record enzyme type, dose and temperature</strong> for every batch so you can reproduce good results.</li>
<li><strong>6. Measure distillate volume and ABV</strong> with a calibrated alcoholmeter, not by taste.</li>
<li><strong>7. Calculate efficiency monthly</strong> and compare against the benchmark table above.</li>
<li><strong>8. Discard heads safely and never sell foreshots.</strong> Methanol is a criminal liability, not just a health risk.</li>
</ul>
