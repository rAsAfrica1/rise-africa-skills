Lesson 1.1~12 min

Amino Acid Requirements and Protein Quality

In this lesson
  • Convert an as-fed protein analysis to a dry matter basis and test it against the AAFCO minimum for the species and life stage
  • Identify the limiting amino acid in a formulation instead of judging protein by the crude protein number alone
  • Compare two protein ingredients on cost per kilogram of protein rather than cost per kilogram of ingredient

Crude protein on a bag tells you how much nitrogen the laboratory found, multiplied by a factor. It does not tell you whether the animal can build tissue from it. Two foods can both declare 26 percent protein and one can leave a cat slowly losing muscle. What separates them is the amino acid profile and the digestibility of the protein carrying it.

Start with the floor. On a dry matter basis, AAFCO requires crude protein of at least 22.5 percent for dogs at growth and reproduction and 18.0 percent for adult maintenance. For cats the same two figures are 30.0 percent and 26.0 percent. Cats sit roughly eight percentage points above dogs at both life stages, and that gap is the single most expensive fact in feline formulation. Expressed against energy instead of dry matter, the minima are 56.3 grams of protein per 1000 kcal for dog growth and reproduction, 45 grams for adult dogs, 75 grams for cat growth and reproduction and 65 grams for adult cats.

The individual amino acids are where formulations quietly fail. The AAFCO dog profile sets adult minima including lysine 0.63 percent, methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent, threonine 0.48 percent and tryptophan 0.16 percent of dry matter, rising at growth to lysine 0.90, methionine plus cystine 0.70 and threonine 1.04. The cat adult profile sets lysine 0.83, methionine plus cystine 0.40, threonine 0.73 and arginine 1.04 percent, with arginine climbing to 1.24 percent at growth against just 1.00 percent for a growing dog. Whichever of these sits closest to its minimum is your limiting amino acid, and the whole formulation performs at that level no matter how high crude protein reads.

Work the conversion by hand once and you will never trust an as-fed number again.

  1. Your laboratory reports the finished food at 24.0 percent crude protein and 8.0 percent moisture, as fed.
  2. Dry matter is 100 minus 8.0 = 92.0 percent, so the dry matter fraction is 0.92.
  3. Protein on a dry matter basis is 24.0 divided by 0.92 = 26.1 percent.
  4. Against the adult dog minimum of 18.0 percent that passes comfortably. Against the adult cat minimum of 26.0 percent it passes by one tenth of a percentage point, which is not a margin, it is a coin toss.
  5. The same food reports lysine at 0.62 percent as fed. On a dry matter basis that is 0.62 divided by 0.92 = 0.67 percent, above the adult dog minimum of 0.63 but below the growth minimum of 0.90. This food may be sold for adult dogs and may not be sold for puppies.

Now check it against energy. If the food carries 3690 kcal of metabolisable energy per kilogram, then 240 grams of protein per kilogram divided by 3.69 thousand kcal gives 65 grams per 1000 kcal, comfortably above the adult dog figure of 45 and exactly at the adult cat figure of 65. The energy basis is the harder test, because a very energy-dense food dilutes protein relative to what the animal actually eats.

Protein quality also depends on what heat has done to the ingredient. Rendered animal meals vary widely with rendering temperature, and over-processing damages lysine in ways a crude protein assay cannot see. That is why an amino acid assay, not a protein assay, is what you send to the laboratory when a batch behaves badly. The digestibility side of this is taken further in lesson 4 of this module.

Buy protein, not ingredients. A fish meal offered at USD 1.40 per kilogram with a declared minimum of 60 percent crude protein costs 1.40 divided by 0.60 = USD 2.33 per kilogram of protein. A soybean meal at USD 0.55 per kilogram with a declared minimum of 44 percent costs 0.55 divided by 0.44 = USD 1.25 per kilogram of protein. The soybean looks like the obvious buy until you remember that a systematic review of adult dog diets found soybean meal reduced dry matter digestibility in most studies and reduced gross energy digestibility across most trials, and that it carries none of the taurine or preformed vitamin A a cat needs.

A producer in Nakuru, Kenya buying tilapia processing offcuts from lake processors at USD 0.85 per kilogram wet is buying roughly a quarter dry matter, so the real cost of the dried meal is closer to USD 3.40 per kilogram before drying fuel. Run that arithmetic before you sign a supply agreement, and insist on a certificate of analysis for every delivery, because the price you agreed was for protein, not for water.

Do this today: take one bag of food you already make or sell, write down its declared crude protein and moisture, and convert the protein to a dry matter basis by dividing by the dry matter fraction. Write the result beside the AAFCO minimum for the species and life stage on the label, and decide in one sentence whether your margin is real or imaginary.

Lesson 1.2~12 min

Fat Soluble vs Water Soluble Vitamins

In this lesson
  • Separate the vitamins that accumulate to toxic levels from those the animal excretes daily, and formulate accordingly
  • Calculate a premix inclusion rate that meets the vitamin A and vitamin D minima without approaching either ceiling
  • Cost a vitamin premix per kilogram of finished food and decide what a safety margin is worth

Vitamins split into two groups that behave in opposite ways, and the difference dictates how you formulate, how you store and how you get sued. Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat and are stored in the body, so an excess accumulates. The B group and vitamin C dissolve in water and are excreted, so an excess is wasted money rather than a hazard, but a shortfall shows up quickly and processing losses matter.

The fat soluble numbers you must hold in your head. For dogs, AAFCO sets vitamin A at a minimum of 5000 IU per kilogram of dry matter at both life stages with a maximum of 250,000, vitamin D at a minimum of 500 IU per kilogram with a maximum of 3000, and vitamin E at a minimum of 50 IU per kilogram. For cats, preformed vitamin A is a minimum of 6668 IU per kilogram at growth and reproduction and 3332 for adults with a maximum of 333,300, vitamin D a minimum of 280 IU per kilogram with a maximum of 30,080, and vitamin E a minimum of 40 IU per kilogram. Note the word preformed. Cats lack the intestinal enzyme needed to cleave beta-carotene, so a plant carotenoid is a colourant in a cat food, not a vitamin A source.

Vitamin D is the nutrient you are most likely to poison an animal with by accident. AAFCO cut the dog safe upper limit from 5000 to 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter in 2016 after Great Dane puppies fed 4000 IU per kilogram showed disrupted endochondral ossification. The European legal ceiling under the FEDIAF tabulation is lower still, 227 IU per 100 grams of dry matter, that is 2270 IU per kilogram, for both species. A diet that is legal in the United States can be illegal in Europe. Real world vitamin D poisonings come from premix and mixing errors, not from raw ingredients, which is why your scale calibration record is a safety document.

The water soluble side is where dogs and cats diverge hardest. Cat niacin is 60 milligrams per kilogram of dry matter against 13.6 for the dog, more than four times as much, and a cat cannot make up a shortfall from tryptophan the way most species can. Thiamine is 5.6 for cats against 2.25 for dogs, pyridoxine 4.0 against 1.5, folic acid 0.8 against 0.216 and choline 2400 against 1360. Run a dog premix into a cat food and you will be short on the four nutrients that matter most.

Work out an inclusion rate step by step. Suppose your premix supplier declares 500,000 IU of vitamin A and 50,000 IU of vitamin D per kilogram of premix, and you are building an adult dog food.

  1. Target vitamin A of 5000 IU per kilogram of finished dry matter.
  2. Inclusion needed is 5000 divided by 500,000 = 0.01 kilograms of premix per kilogram of food, that is 1 percent, or 10 kilograms per tonne.
  3. Check vitamin D at that rate: 0.01 multiplied by 50,000 = 500 IU per kilogram, exactly the minimum, and well below the 3000 IU ceiling.
  4. Now suppose an operator double-doses one batch. Vitamin A becomes 10,000 IU per kilogram, still far below the 250,000 maximum. Vitamin D becomes 1000 IU per kilogram, still legal. Double it again by mistake and vitamin D reaches 2000, inside AAFCO but close to the European 2270 limit. A triple dose breaches both.
  5. The lesson is that vitamin A gives you a wide margin for error and vitamin D gives you almost none. Design your dosing procedure around vitamin D.

Cost. A premix at USD 4.50 per kilogram included at 10 kilograms per tonne adds USD 45 per tonne, that is USD 0.045 per kilogram of finished food. On a product retailing at USD 1.60 per kilogram that is under three percent of the price, which makes buying a properly assayed premix the cheapest insurance in the entire operation. Do not try to save money by blending your own vitamins from bulk single ingredients unless you have gram-accurate scales and a written procedure.

Storage is the other half. Both groups degrade with heat, moisture and time, and extrusion adds heat. Premix suppliers add an overage for this, but the size of that overage is a supplier specification, not a published standard, so ask for it in writing. A producer in Kampala, Uganda storing premix in an uninsulated iron-roofed store is losing potency between delivery and mixing. Buy quantities you will use within the stated shelf life, keep them sealed and off the floor, and write the delivery date on every bag.

Vitamin K and biotin were not captured in the profile extract our verified reference set is built on. Where a nutrient is missing from your reference, obtain the current AAFCO Official Publication rather than filling the gap with a figure you found on a forum.

Do this today: find your vitamin premix bag or supplier data sheet, write down the declared IU per kilogram of vitamin A and vitamin D, and calculate what your current inclusion rate delivers per kilogram of finished food. Compare the vitamin D figure with the 3000 IU per kilogram AAFCO dog ceiling and the 2270 IU per kilogram European limit, and write the margin down.

Mineral Balance: Calcium, Phosphorus, and Beyond

In this lesson
  • State the calcium and phosphorus minima and the calcium to phosphorus ratio limits for each species and life stage
  • Calculate a limestone or dicalcium phosphate addition that corrects a mineral shortfall without breaching a ceiling
  • Recognise which trace minerals carry maximum limits and where your reference set has gaps

Minerals are the part of a formulation where a small arithmetic slip does lasting skeletal damage, and where cheap ingredients carry loads you did not order. Calcium and phosphorus matter both as absolute amounts and as a ratio to each other, which is why you cannot fix a calcium shortfall by simply adding more of something.

The dog figures on a dry matter basis are calcium 1.2 percent and phosphorus 1.0 percent at growth and reproduction, and calcium 0.5 percent and phosphorus 0.4 percent for adult maintenance. Phosphorus has a maximum of 1.6 percent. The calcium to phosphorus ratio must sit between 1 to 1 and 2 to 1, with an optimum commonly given as 1.2 to 1.4 to 1. For cats the minima are calcium 1.0 and phosphorus 0.8 percent at growth and calcium 0.6 and phosphorus 0.5 percent for adults. A calcium to phosphorus ratio row was not captured for cats in the profile extract behind our reference set, so use the FEDIAF nutritional maxima of 2 to 1 for adult cats and 1.5 to 1 for growing cats and confirm against the current published profile before you print a label.

The calcium ceiling is the constraint that catches small producers. A diet labelled for growth including large size dogs, meaning those that will weigh 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, must not exceed 1.8 percent calcium on a dry matter basis. For every other life stage, including growth diets that are not for large breeds, the maximum is 2.5 percent. Excess supplementation above 3 percent of dry matter has been reported to cause severe osteochondrosis, and the practical recommendation is to stay within 1 to 3 percent. Module 2 lesson 2 takes the growth side of this much further.

Now the arithmetic that goes wrong. Suppose your finished cat food analyses at 0.45 percent calcium on a dry matter basis and you need 0.60 for adult maintenance.

  1. The shortfall is 0.60 minus 0.45 = 0.15 percentage points of dry matter.
  2. Your limestone supplier's certificate of analysis declares 38 percent calcium.
  3. Limestone needed is 0.15 divided by 38, multiplied by 100 = 0.395 kilograms per 100 kilograms of food, that is about 4 kilograms per tonne.
  4. Limestone at USD 0.14 per kilogram adds 4 multiplied by 0.14 = USD 0.56 per tonne, effectively nothing.
  5. Check the ratio afterwards. If phosphorus is 0.50 percent, calcium to phosphorus is 0.60 divided by 0.50 = 1.2 to 1, inside the acceptable band and at the commonly cited optimum.

Watch what your protein sources bring with them. Meat and bone meal and bone protein meals carry calcium and ash from bone, and the amount varies with the raw material and the rendering process. Our verified reference set does not contain inclusion limits or typical calcium figures for generic meat and bone meal, so you must get a certificate of analysis for every consignment rather than assuming last month's figure still holds. A batch of bone-heavy meal can push a growth diet through the 1.8 percent large breed ceiling without any calcium supplement being added at all.

Beyond calcium and phosphorus, the dog trace minima on a dry matter basis are iron 88 milligrams per kilogram at growth and 40 for adults, copper 12.4 and 7.3, manganese 7.2 and 5.0, zinc 100 and 80, iodine 1.0 at both stages with a maximum of 11, and selenium 0.35 at both stages with a maximum of 2. For cats they are iron 80 at both stages, copper 15 for extruded growth diets and 5 for adults, manganese 7.6, zinc 75, iodine 1.8 at growth and 0.6 for adults, and selenium 0.3 with the same discipline around excess. Selenium and iodine are the two where over-addition is a real hazard rather than a waste, so weigh them from a premix rather than adding them separately.

Interactions matter as much as amounts. High dietary phytate from plant ingredients binds zinc and iron, and high calcium competes with zinc. A formulation can meet every number on paper and still produce a zinc-responsive skin problem. That is a bioavailability question, taken up in the next lesson.

A producer in Arusha, Tanzania buying limestone from a local quarry at USD 0.11 per kilogram against USD 0.19 for the imported feed-grade product is saving real money, but quarry limestone varies in calcium content and can carry lead or other contaminants. Send the first delivery from any new source for a calcium assay and a heavy metal screen. A single assay costing USD 60 protects a year of production and converts an assumption into a batch record entry.

Finally, magnesium, potassium, sodium and chloride were not captured in the profile extract behind our reference set. Treat those as open items to close with the current official publication, not as nutrients that do not matter.

Add up the calcium each contributes to 100 kilograms of your growth formulation and check the total against the 1.8 percent large breed ceiling.

Digestibility and Bioavailability

In this lesson
  • Calculate metabolisable energy for a formulation using the five-step modified Atwater route
  • Set practical inclusion ceilings for rendered by-product meals using published digestibility and faecal quality data
  • Rank carbohydrate sources by digestibility and choose one on evidence rather than marketing

Digestibility is the difference between what you put in the bag and what the animal keeps. Two foods with identical guaranteed analyses can differ by ten percentage points in energy digestibility, and the owner notices only through stool quality and the amount they have to feed. Bioavailability goes one step further and asks whether an absorbed nutrient can actually be used, which is why a formulation can meet every mineral number on paper and still fail.

The predictive route from composition to energy has five steps. It is the calculation that lets you put a defensible kcal per kilogram figure on your specification sheet.

  1. Gross energy in kcal per 100 grams equals 5.7 multiplied by grams of protein, plus 9.4 multiplied by grams of fat, plus 4.1 multiplied by the sum of nitrogen-free extract and crude fibre.
  2. Energy digestibility for dogs as a percentage equals 91.2 minus the quantity 1.43 multiplied by crude fibre percent multiplied by 100, divided by 100 minus percent moisture.
  3. For cats the same equation uses 87.9 minus the quantity 0.88 multiplied by crude fibre percent multiplied by 100, divided by 100 minus percent moisture.
  4. Digestible energy equals gross energy multiplied by digestibility.
  5. Metabolisable energy for dogs equals digestible energy minus 1.04 multiplied by grams of protein; for cats it is digestible energy minus 0.77 multiplied by grams of protein.

Work one through. Take a dog food at 25 grams protein, 12 grams fat, 46 grams nitrogen-free extract, 3 grams crude fibre and 9 percent moisture per 100 grams as fed. Gross energy is 5.7 multiplied by 25 = 142.5, plus 9.4 multiplied by 12 = 112.8, plus 4.1 multiplied by 49 = 200.9, total 456.2 kcal per 100 grams. Digestibility is 91.2 minus 1.43 multiplied by 3 multiplied by 100 divided by 91, that is 91.2 minus 4.71 = 86.5 percent. Digestible energy is 456.2 multiplied by 0.865 = 394.6. Metabolisable energy is 394.6 minus 1.04 multiplied by 25 = 394.6 minus 26.0 = 368.6 kcal per 100 grams, that is 3686 kcal per kilogram. Typical dry foods run 2800 to 4800 kcal per kilogram and wet foods 700 to 1300, so this sits mid-range.

Notice what crude fibre does in step two. Each additional percentage point of crude fibre removes about 1.57 percentage points of energy digestibility at this moisture level. Fibre is a tool, not a filler, and it costs energy.

Real trial data gives you inclusion ceilings. In a Beagle study, bone protein meal was tested at 0, 6, 12 and 24 percent and feather meal at 0, 5, 10 and 20 percent. Apparent crude protein digestibility held up well, 78.0 percent at zero bone protein meal and 82.4 percent at 24 percent, and feather meal moved only from 82.4 to 81.1 percent between zero and 20 percent inclusion. Digestibility was not the limit. Faecal quality was. The authors concluded that bone protein meal could be included up to 12 percent and feather meal up to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores. Those two numbers are the practical ceilings to write into your formulation rules, and they exist because customers judge you by what they pick up in the yard.

Fibre choice behaves the same way. In a 10 percent inclusion trial, beet pulp gave dry matter digestibility of 81.3 percent against 78.2 for Miscanthus grass and 77.2 for cellulose, and organic matter 86.1 against 82.1 and 80.8. But beet pulp slightly depressed protein digestibility, 84.5 percent against 87.9 and 87.6, and produced softer stools, a score of 3.15 against 3.68 and 3.64. A moderately fermentable fibre buys you energy digestibility and costs you stool firmness.

Carbohydrates rank cleanly. In a six-source comparison in extruded dog diets, brewer's rice and cassava flour gave the greatest digestibility of dry matter, organic matter and gross energy, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil diets lowest. Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent across all six, which disposes of the claim that dogs cannot digest grain.

Now the money. Compare foods on cost per 1000 kcal of metabolisable energy, not per kilogram. At 3686 kcal per kilogram and an ingredient cost of USD 0.94 per kilogram, you are at 0.94 divided by 3.686 = USD 0.255 per 1000 kcal. A cheaper mix at USD 0.86 per kilogram but only 3100 kcal per kilogram costs 0.86 divided by 3.1 = USD 0.277. The cheap bag is the expensive food.

A producer in Tamale, Ghana with cassava flour at USD 0.30 per kilogram against imported brewer's rice at USD 0.52 is in a strong position, because cassava sits in the top digestibility group in that same trial. Run a simple in-house check before you switch: feed twenty animals for two weeks and score stools daily on a fixed scale. It will catch a disaster before your customers do.

Do this today: write out your main formulation's protein, fat, nitrogen-free extract, crude fibre and moisture per 100 grams and run all five modified Atwater steps to get metabolisable energy in kcal per kilogram. Then divide your ingredient cost per kilogram by that figure in thousands to get cost per 1000 kcal, and compare it with one competitor bag.

Lesson 1.5~13 min

Life Stage Nutritional Needs

In this lesson
  • Calculate resting energy requirement and apply the correct life stage multiplier for a dog or a cat
  • Explain why a single all life stages formulation must be built to the growth minima, not the adult ones
  • Price the daily feeding cost of the same animal at three different life stages

A life stage is not a marketing segment, it is a set of numbers. Growth and reproduction raise nutrient minima and multiply energy needs; adult maintenance lowers both; and the very old cat surprises most people by needing more energy per kilogram, not less. If you sell one product to every animal, you are either overfeeding the adults or underfeeding the puppies, and usually both.

Energy starts with resting energy requirement. The equation valid at any weight is 70 multiplied by body weight in kilograms raised to the power 0.75. Between 2 and 45 kilograms a linear approximation, 30 multiplied by body weight plus 70, is close enough for a feeding guide. For a 20 kilogram dog the exponential form gives 70 multiplied by 9.457 = 662 kcal and the linear form gives 600 plus 70 = 670 kcal, a difference of about one percent.

Maintenance energy requirement is resting energy multiplied by a life stage factor. For dogs the factors are 3.0 from weaning to four months, 2.0 from four months to adult, 1.8 for an intact adult, 1.6 for a neutered adult, 1.2 to 1.4 for the inactive or obesity-prone animal, 1.0 for weight loss and 2.0, 3.0 and 4.0 to 8.0 for light, moderate and heavy work. For cats the factors are 2.5 for a kitten from weaning to adult, 1.4 to 1.6 for an intact adult, 1.2 to 1.4 for a neutered adult, 1.1 to 1.4 for a cat aged seven to eleven years, 1.1 to 1.6 for a cat over eleven, 1.0 for the obesity-prone and 0.8 for weight loss. Note the direction of the feline senior rows: they trend upward in the very old cat, reflecting declining digestive efficiency, which is the opposite of what most people assume.

Follow one animal through its life. Take a dog that will mature at 20 kilograms, and read the energy at each stage using the resting figure for its weight at the time.

  1. At four months and 10 kilograms, resting energy is 70 multiplied by 10 to the power 0.75 = 394 kcal. At the growth factor of 2.0 that is 787 kcal per day.
  2. As a neutered adult at 20 kilograms, resting energy is 662 kcal and the factor of 1.6 gives 1059 kcal per day.
  3. As an inactive older animal at the same weight with a factor of 1.3, the requirement falls to 861 kcal per day.
  4. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram, those three figures are 207, 279 and 227 grams per day.
  5. At USD 1.60 per kilogram of finished food, that is USD 0.33, USD 0.45 and USD 0.36 per day. Over a year the adult maintenance stage alone costs the owner about USD 163.

Now the nutrient side. The growth and reproduction minima are higher than the adult ones for almost everything that matters: dog protein 22.5 against 18.0 percent of dry matter, dog fat 8.5 against 5.5, cat protein 30.0 against 26.0, dog calcium 1.2 against 0.5, dog zinc 100 against 80 milligrams per kilogram, cat vitamin A 6668 against 3332 IU per kilogram. Cat fat is the exception that catches people out: the minimum is 9.0 percent at both life stages and does not drop for adult maintenance.

That is why a single all life stages product must be formulated to the growth and reproduction column throughout. It is a legitimate product for a small producer with one extruder, and it simplifies your inventory, but it has two consequences. First, you are feeding growth-level nutrients to adults, which costs more per kilogram than a maintenance formula needs to. Second, if you want the label to cover large breed puppies you are simultaneously bound by the 1.8 percent calcium ceiling, so your calcium must sit between 1.2 and 1.8 percent of dry matter with no room for a bone-heavy consignment.

Reproduction and lactation multiply demand further, up to 3.0 times resting energy in the last three weeks of canine gestation and 3.0 to more than 6.0 during lactation. Those are covered properly in module 11, and diets for animals with a diagnosed disease belong in module 9. Keep your everyday range clean: growth, adult maintenance, and a reduced-energy option for the inactive animal.

A producer in Lusaka, Zambia running one 300 kilogram per hour extruder launched with exactly two dry products, an all life stages formula built to the growth column and a lower-energy adult formula at about 3400 kcal per kilogram. Two formulations, two premixes, two label texts, one machine. That is a realistic first year, and the temptation to add a third product before the first two sell reliably has killed more small feed businesses than any nutritional error.

Whether a particular animal should move between stages is a clinical judgement as much as a nutritional one. Where an animal is losing condition without an obvious dietary cause, the right advice to a customer is to see a veterinarian, not to sell them a different bag.

Do this today: pick one real customer animal you know, write down its species, weight and life stage, calculate its resting energy requirement with 70 times weight to the power 0.75, apply the right multiplier, and divide by your food's energy density to get grams per day. Compare that with what the owner is actually feeding.

Alternative Proteins and Sustainability

In this lesson
  • State the regulatory position of black soldier fly larvae and cultured protein before you put either on a label
  • Calculate the cost per kilogram of digestible protein for an alternative source against a conventional one
  • Explain why a plant-based cat diet is a different proposition from a plant-based dog diet

Alternative proteins are the most interesting opportunity available to an African pet food producer and the easiest place to make a claim you cannot defend. Insect meal in particular can be produced locally on vegetable by-products, in climates that suit it, at a fraction of the cost of imported fish meal. The nutrition is promising. The regulation is narrower than the marketing suggests.

Black soldier fly larvae is the only insect product that has successfully gone through the AAFCO ingredient definition process. Dried black soldier fly larvae was tentatively approved in August 2021 for use in salmonid, poultry and swine feed and in adult dog food. Read that scope carefully. Adult dog food only. It does not cover puppy or growth diets, and AAFCO was not at that time reviewing black soldier fly larvae for adult cat food. Cricket, mealworm and other species are not AAFCO-defined at all. The definition requires the ingredient to be dried by artificial means to no more than 10 percent moisture and to be labelled with minimum crude protein and minimum crude fat, and it specifies no maximum inclusion level. Whether the status has changed since 2021 is something you must verify for yourself before printing a bag.

Nutritionally, insect meal typically runs around 55 percent protein with an average digestibility of about 80 percent, varying by species and by processing. Both dogs and cats will eat it, with different palatability preferences between the species. Two cautions matter: chitin contributes nitrogen and therefore inflates crude protein without contributing usable amino acids, and the methionine and lysine profile varies by species and process. Neither is quantified in our verified source, so treat both as reasons to run an amino acid assay.

Do the arithmetic before the enthusiasm. Suppose local black soldier fly meal is offered at USD 2.80 per kilogram.

  1. Protein content 55 percent gives 0.55 kilograms of protein per kilogram of meal.
  2. At 80 percent digestibility, digestible protein is 0.55 multiplied by 0.80 = 0.44 kilograms per kilogram.
  3. Cost per kilogram of digestible protein is 2.80 divided by 0.44 = USD 6.36.
  4. Imported fish meal at USD 1.40 per kilogram with 60 percent protein and, say, an assayed 85 percent digestibility gives 0.60 multiplied by 0.85 = 0.51 kilograms of digestible protein, at 1.40 divided by 0.51 = USD 2.75.
  5. At today's prices the insect meal is more than twice as expensive per unit of digestible protein. The case for it is not cost today, it is supply security, a local value chain and a story customers will pay a premium for. Make that case honestly rather than pretending the economics already work.

Cultured or single-cell protein is earlier still. A methanotroph bacterial protein meal was tested in adult dogs at 0, 4, 6 and 8 percent inclusion over a six-month safety phase, with apparent total tract digestibility above 80 percent for organic matter, protein and energy, and the authors concluded that up to 8 percent of the total diet of adult dogs is safe with no long-term effects across a range of health parameters. One figure deserves attention: copper digestibility was around 20 percent, which is a formulation problem because copper minima still have to be met. The study did not establish an independent ingredient definition, so treat single-cell protein as pre-regulatory.

Plant-based diets divide sharply by species. For cats the veterinary position is blunt: cats are obligate carnivores, and it is much harder or even impossible to meet their nutritional needs without feeding them meat. The reasons are the four nutrients covered in module 4 of this course. Taurine is absent from plants. Preformed vitamin A cannot be replaced by beta-carotene, which cats cannot cleave. Arachidonic acid is absent from vegetable oils and cats cannot synthesise enough of it. Niacin at 60 milligrams per kilogram cannot be met through tryptophan. Each can in theory be supplied synthetically, but the objection is bioavailability and the absence of large-scale long-term studies. For dogs the position is that it is not established as safe but not categorically impossible; the veterinary warning is that it is much easier to get the balance of essential nutrients wrong than to get it right, and that the jury is still very much out.

One more claim to avoid: do not market grain-free as a health benefit and do not treat grain as protective. The investigation into diets and canine dilated cardiomyopathy received 524 reports between January 2014 and April 2019, but no causal link has been demonstrated and it was paused in December 2022. Module 3 lesson 5 covers what that means for your formulation choices.

A producer in Kigali, Rwanda rearing black soldier fly larvae on brewery spent grain and market vegetable waste has a genuinely defensible sustainability story: a local substrate, a short supply chain and no import duty. Build the claim around what you can document, the substrate, the drying method, the moisture specification and an amino acid assay, and label the product for adult dogs only until you can show a current definition covering anything wider.

File the reply. You now have the beginning of a specification instead of a rumour.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A food analyses at 23 percent crude protein as fed with 8 percent moisture. What is the dry matter protein?

Dry matter is 92 percent, so 23 divided by 0.92 equals 25.0 percent. Dividing by the dry matter fraction always raises the figure; multiplying by it is the error to avoid.

2. Which pair of minima applies to an adult cat maintenance diet on a dry matter basis?

Adult cat maintenance is 26.0 percent protein and 9.0 percent fat. The 30.0 percent figure is cat growth and reproduction; the two dog figures are the first two options.

3. What does the term limiting amino acid mean in practice?

Protein synthesis proceeds only as far as the scarcest essential amino acid allows, so a high crude protein figure does not rescue a formula short of one amino acid.

4. Fish meal at USD 1.40 per kilogram declares 60 percent protein. What is the cost per kilogram of protein?

1.40 divided by 0.60 equals USD 2.33 per kilogram of protein. Comparing ingredients on cost per kilogram of protein rather than cost per kilogram of ingredient is the basic buying discipline.

5. Why is a crude protein assay alone a weak test of protein quality?

Crude protein is nitrogen multiplied by a factor. It cannot reveal that lysine has been damaged by over-rendering or that one essential amino acid is short, which is why an amino acid assay is the follow-up test.

6. Why is vitamin D the fat soluble vitamin that most often causes accidental harm?

The dog minimum is 500 IU per kilogram and the maximum 3000, so a few multiples of a dosing error cross the line. Vitamin A by contrast runs from 5000 to 250,000.

7. A cat food contains beta-carotene but no preformed vitamin A. What is the consequence?

Cats lack the intestinal enzyme needed to cleave beta-carotene to retinol, so they require preformed vitamin A from animal sources. Beta-carotene in a cat food acts as a colourant or antioxidant.

8. Premix declares 500,000 IU vitamin A per kilogram. What inclusion meets a 5000 IU per kilogram target?

5000 divided by 500,000 equals 0.01, that is 1 percent, or 10 kilograms per tonne.

9. Which statement about water soluble vitamins is correct?

The B group is excreted rather than stored, so a daily supply is needed. Cats require far more niacin, thiamine, pyridoxine, folic acid and choline than dogs.

10. Your reference does not contain a figure for vitamin K in cat food. What should you do?

A gap in a reference is a gap, not a licence to substitute. Buy the current published profile rather than filling the space with an unverified number.

11. A growth diet labelled for dogs maturing at 70 pounds or more must not exceed what calcium level?

The 1.8 percent ceiling applies specifically to formulas that may be fed to large size puppies. Other life stages, including non-large-size growth diets, cap at 2.5 percent.

12. Your food is at 0.45 percent calcium and you need 0.60. Limestone declares 38 percent calcium. How much per tonne?

The shortfall is 0.15 percentage points; 0.15 divided by 38 multiplied by 100 gives 0.395 kg per 100 kg, that is roughly 4 kg per tonne.

13. Which two trace minerals carry maximum limits in the dog profile that make over-addition a genuine hazard?

Iodine has a maximum of 11 milligrams per kilogram and selenium a maximum of 2. Both should be delivered through an assayed premix rather than added separately.

14. Why can a bone-heavy meat meal breach a calcium ceiling without any supplement being added?

Calcium and ash load from bone vary with the raw material and the rendering process, so a certificate of analysis is required for every consignment rather than an assumed figure.

15. Which minerals were not captured in the profile extract behind this course's reference set?

Those were not captured and must be closed against the current official publication. A gap in a reference is not permission to invent a figure.

16. In the modified Atwater route, what is the last step for a dog food?

Metabolisable energy for dogs is digestible energy minus 1.04 multiplied by grams of protein. The 0.77 factor is the cat version.

17. What limited bone protein meal to 12 percent and feather meal to 5 percent in the Beagle trial?

Digestibility stayed acceptable at higher levels; faecal scores were reduced at 24 percent bone protein meal and 20 percent feather meal, which set the practical ceiling.

18. Which carbohydrate sources gave the greatest dry matter and energy digestibility in the six-source trial?

Brewer's rice and cassava flour ranked highest, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil lowest. Starch digestibility still exceeded 98 percent in every diet.

19. Ingredient cost is USD 0.86 per kilogram and energy density is 3100 kcal per kilogram. Cost per 1000 kcal?

0.86 divided by 3.1 equals USD 0.277 per 1000 kcal, which is more expensive than a USD 0.94 mix at 3686 kcal per kilogram.

20. What did the 10 percent beet pulp trial show about stool quality?

Beet pulp scored 3.15 against 3.68 for cellulose and 3.64 for Miscanthus, that is softer, while raising dry matter and organic matter digestibility.

21. What is the resting energy requirement of a 10 kilogram dog?

70 multiplied by 10 to the power 0.75 equals 394 kcal. The 787 figure is that resting energy at the growth factor of 2.0.

22. An all life stages food must be formulated to which column?

It has to satisfy the highest requirement it claims to cover, which is growth and reproduction. Formulating to the adult column and labelling for all life stages is a false claim.

23. Which cat life stage factor is highest?

The kitten factor is 2.5 times resting energy. Neutered adults sit at 1.2 to 1.4, obesity-prone at 1.0 and weight loss at 0.8.

24. Why does the cat fat minimum catch producers out?

The dog fat minimum falls from 8.5 percent at growth to 5.5 percent for adults, but the cat minimum is 9.0 percent at both stages.

25. A 20 kg neutered dog eats a 3800 kcal per kilogram food. Roughly how much per day?

Resting energy 662 kcal times 1.6 is 1059 kcal; 1059 divided by 3800 equals 0.279 kg, that is 279 grams.

26. What is the scope of the AAFCO tentative approval for dried black soldier fly larvae in pet food?

The tentative approval from August 2021 covered salmonid, poultry and swine feed and adult dog food. Cats and growing dogs were not covered.

27. Why does chitin complicate the crude protein figure for insect meal?

Crude protein is calculated from nitrogen, and chitin nitrogen inflates the result without supplying usable amino acids. An amino acid assay is the honest check.

28. Which nutrient made cultured protein a formulation problem in the adult dog study?

Organic matter, protein and energy digestibility were all above 80 percent, but copper apparent total tract digestibility was around 20 percent, so copper minima need separate attention.

29. Which four nutrients make a plant-based cat diet a supplementation-dependent formulation?

Those four are the cat-specific essentials that plants either lack entirely or supply in a form cats cannot use, and their absence is why the veterinary position on vegan cat diets is so firm.

30. What is the defensible position on grain-free diets and dilated cardiomyopathy?

Reports were received but adverse event numbers alone do not establish causation, and the investigation was paused in December 2022 pending meaningful new scientific information.

Module 1 capstone

Build a verified nutrient specification sheet for one dog food and one cat food you intend to sell. Step 1: choose the species and the life stage you will claim on the pack, and write the AAFCO dry matter minima for crude protein, crude fat, lysine, methionine plus cystine, calcium and phosphorus at the top of the page. Step 2: collect a written certificate of analysis, or a supplier declaration, for every ingredient in the formulation, and record moisture, crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre, ash, calcium and phosphorus for each. Step 3: build the recipe as percentages summing to 100 and calculate the weighted average of each nutrient as fed. Step 4: convert every as-fed figure to a dry matter figure by dividing by the dry matter fraction, and compare each one against the minima you wrote in step 1. Mark every line pass or fail. Step 5: calculate metabolisable energy using the five-step modified Atwater route, and then express crude protein as grams per 1000 kcal so you can check it against the energy-basis minimum of 45 grams for adult dogs and 65 grams for adult cats. Step 6: cost the recipe per kilogram at your real local ingredient prices, and calculate cost per 1000 kcal of metabolisable energy so you can compare it honestly with the imported product on the shelf. Step 7: list every nutrient you could not verify from a certificate of analysis, and write beside each one the laboratory or supplier you will use to close the gap. Hand the finished sheet to a veterinarian or an animal nutritionist for comment before you print a single bag.
