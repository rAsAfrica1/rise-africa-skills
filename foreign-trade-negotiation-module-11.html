1. Foundations of Foreign Trade Negotiation in Africa

Foreign trade negotiation is the structured process by which buyers, sellers, intermediaries, and public authorities from different countries agree on the terms of cross-border exchange. In Africa, this process carries extra weight because trade is often the fastest route to job creation, industrial learning, and foreign exchange. A negotiation that closes a container of processed cashew from Benin to a European roaster, or a services contract between a Kenyan fintech and a Rwandan bank, can change the trajectory of a small firm. This lesson gives you the practical foundations you need before you sit at any table. First, understand that trade negotiation is not only about price. It covers product specification, quality standards, delivery terms, payment security, currency risk, logistics, insurance, dispute resolution, and compliance with rules of origin. In Africa, the most common mistake is to focus on unit price while ignoring landed cost, payment delays, and non-tariff barriers. A supplier may quote a lower FOB price but lose the deal because the buyer must pay for expensive inland transport, port congestion, and certification that the supplier cannot provide. Start every negotiation with a trade map. Identify the actual decision makers, the regulatory bodies, the logistics chain, and the financial institutions involved. For intra-African trade under the African Continental Free Trade Area, also identify rules of origin, tariff schedules, and standards harmonization requirements. Second, know your walk-away point. This is the worst acceptable outcome before you choose no deal. Calculate it from your cost structure, not from emotion. If you are an Ethiopian coffee exporter, your walk-away price must cover farmgate purchase, processing, export documentation, inland transport to Djibouti, port charges, and a margin for currency movement. If you are a Nigerian importer of machinery, your walk-away point must include duty, clearing, installation, and the cost of downtime if delivery slips. Third, prepare a negotiation brief. It should include the other side profile, their likely interests, their alternatives, your alternatives, the issues on the table, and the order in which you will discuss them. A good brief for an African trade negotiation also includes cultural and linguistic notes. Africa is not one market. A meeting in Casablanca, Kigali, Lagos, or Nairobi has different rhythms, hierarchy expectations, and attitudes to time. Do not stereotype, but do prepare. Fourth, understand the legal and institutional environment. Most African countries are members of the World Trade Organization and regional bodies such as ECOWAS, SADC, COMESA, the East African Community, and the AfCFTA. These frameworks shape tariffs, customs procedures, and dispute settlement. Know which trade agreements apply to your transaction. If you are exporting under a preference scheme such as AGOA or the EU Everything But Arms arrangement, you must document origin correctly. A missing certificate can turn a zero-tariff shipment into a full-duty liability. Fifth, build a negotiation team with clear roles. One person leads, one records concessions, one watches numbers, and one handles technical or legal questions. In small African firms, the owner often does everything, which weakens focus. Even a two-person team is better than one person trying to remember every promise. Sixth, practice active listening. Many negotiations fail because one side talks past the other. Ask open questions: What does your ideal delivery schedule look like? What quality certificate does your regulator require? What payment terms have worked for you in the past? Then summarize what you heard before responding. This builds trust and uncovers interests behind positions. Seventh, manage information carefully. Do not reveal your cost structure, your alternative buyers, or your internal deadlines unless it serves a clear purpose. At the same time, do not lie about facts that can be verified. African trade relationships are long-term and reputation travels fast across business networks. Eighth, prepare for payment risk. Common instruments include letters of credit, documentary collections, open account, advance payment, and mobile money for smaller transactions. Each has a cost and a risk profile. A letter of credit reduces non-payment risk but adds bank charges and document strictness. Open account is cheaper but exposes the exporter. For intra-African trade, consider regional payment systems and local currency arrangements where available. Ninth, plan logistics and Incoterms. Incoterms define who arranges carriage, insurance, and customs clearance. EXW, FOB, CIF, and DAP allocate risk differently. Choose the term that matches your capacity. A small exporter should usually avoid DDP because it requires handling import clearance in a foreign country. Tenth, document every agreement. A written contract should cover specification, quantity, price, currency, payment terms, delivery dates, inspection, force majeure, governing law, and dispute resolution. In Africa, enforcement can be slow, so include arbitration clauses with a recognized center and consider mediation first. Finally, treat negotiation as a repeat game. The goal is not to win once but to build a trading relationship that survives currency swings, port delays, and political changes. Prepare thoroughly, listen carefully, calculate honestly, and document clearly. These foundations will carry you into the more advanced tactics covered in the rest of this module.

2. Preparing Your Negotiation Strategy and BATNA for African Markets

A strategy is a plan for how you will move from your opening position to an acceptable agreement. Your BATNA is your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. In African trade, where logistics and payments can be unpredictable, a strong BATNA is your insurance policy. This lesson shows you how to build both. Start with interests, not positions. A position is what you say you want, such as a price of 2,000 US dollars per metric ton. An interest is why you want it, such as covering rising input costs or protecting a margin for reinvestment. When you understand interests, you can create options. For example, a Ghanaian cocoa buyer may insist on a lower price, but the real interest may be predictable supply during the main crop season. You can offer a volume commitment or a forward contract instead of a price cut. Map the issues. List every item that matters: price, quantity, quality grade, packaging, delivery window, payment method, currency, inspection, warranty, after-sales support, and dispute resolution. Then rank each issue by importance to you and estimate its importance to the other side. This issue map prevents you from trading away something cheap for you but valuable to them, or vice versa. Build your BATNA before you negotiate. Ask: if this deal fails, what will I do? For an exporter, alternatives include selling to another buyer, processing further, storing for a later season, or selling domestically. For an importer, alternatives include another supplier, a substitute product, or delaying the purchase. Estimate the value of each alternative in money, time, and risk. Your BATNA sets your reservation value, the point beyond which you should walk away. If your BATNA is weak, improve it before negotiating. You can develop a second buyer, obtain a financing facility, or join an export consortium to increase volume. Improve the other side BATNA only if it helps you close a deal that you want. Sometimes a buyer with no alternative will demand harsh terms because they feel trapped. Helping them find a workable alternative can make the relationship healthier. Research the counterparty. In Africa, business intelligence often comes through networks, trade associations, chambers of commerce, and embassies. Check payment history, delivery reliability, and reputation. Use official sources for regulatory information, but use personal networks for practical intelligence. Prepare a negotiation agenda. Send it in advance. A simple agenda shows professionalism and reduces surprises. It also lets you control the sequence. Start with easy issues to build momentum, then move to harder ones. Save the most difficult issue for when trust has grown, unless you need to test the other side early. Decide your opening, target, and walk-away for each issue. Your opening should be ambitious but justifiable. If you open too low, you leave money on the table. If you open absurdly high, you damage credibility. Use objective criteria to justify your numbers: market prices, industry benchmarks, production costs, freight rates, and quality premiums. Objective criteria are especially useful in African trade because they reduce the perception of exploitation. Plan your concessions. Never give a concession without getting something in return. Trade issues, not just money. You might accept a longer payment term if the buyer accepts a higher volume or a longer contract. You might accept a lower price if the buyer pays for certification or provides technical training. Make concessions gradually and label them as concessions so the other side values them. Prepare for cultural and communication differences without stereotyping. In many African business settings, relationship building precedes transaction details. Allow time for introductions, tea, and informal conversation. In others, especially in fast-moving tech and commodity trading, meetings are direct and time-bound. Observe and adapt. Language matters. If you are negotiating in a second or third language, confirm numbers and technical terms in writing. Use interpreters carefully and brief them beforehand. Prepare for common African trade challenges. These include foreign exchange shortages, port congestion, customs delays, power outages, and sudden changes in regulation. Build contingencies into your strategy. For example, if currency volatility is high, agree on a pricing formula or a currency adjustment clause. If port delays are likely, build buffer time into delivery schedules and define demurrage responsibility. If regulation may change, include a change-in-law clause. Consider the role of government. In many African countries, the state is a major buyer, regulator, and sometimes a competitor. Public procurement negotiations have specific rules, anti-corruption requirements, and transparency obligations. Prepare documentation carefully and avoid any improper payment. Reputation is a long-term asset. Practice your negotiation. Role-play with a colleague. Record the session and review it. Identify filler words, emotional triggers, and weak justifications. Practice saying no politely. Practice asking for a break when you need to think. A short pause is better than a rushed concession. Set a team protocol. Agree who speaks on which issue, who can make concessions, and who can call a timeout. If you are negotiating remotely, test the technology, prepare shared documents, and confirm time zones. Remote negotiation is common in African trade because travel costs are high and visas can be difficult. Finally, write a one-page strategy summary. Include your interests, their likely interests, your BATNA, your reservation value, your opening, your target, your concession plan, and your red lines. Keep it visible during the negotiation. A clear strategy does not guarantee success, but it dramatically improves your odds. In the next lesson, we will apply these ideas to pricing, terms, and contract design.

3. Pricing, Terms, and Contract Design in African Trade

Price is the most visible issue in trade negotiation, but it is rarely the only one. In African trade, the terms around price often determine whether a deal is profitable. This lesson covers pricing methods, payment terms, delivery terms, and contract design. Start with cost-based pricing. Calculate your total cost to the point of delivery. For an exporter, this includes production, processing, packaging, inland transport, port handling, documentation, certification, insurance, and financing costs. Add a margin that reflects risk and reinvestment. For an importer, calculate landed cost: purchase price, freight, insurance, duty, clearing, inland transport, and installation. Landed cost is the only number that matters for resale or production. Use market-based pricing when comparable transactions exist. Check regional and international prices for commodities, freight rates, and insurance premiums. For differentiated products, such as specialty coffee, shea butter, or handcrafted textiles, use value-based pricing. Identify what the buyer values: origin story, organic certification, fair trade labeling, unique design, or reliable supply. Then price accordingly. Consider currency. Many African trade contracts are denominated in US dollars or euros, but local currency costs are real. If you earn in local currency and pay in dollars, you face exchange risk. Options include invoicing in a stable currency, using a forward contract, agreeing on a currency adjustment clause, or sharing the risk with the buyer. Some African central banks restrict foreign currency access, so confirm availability before signing. Payment terms are critical. Advance payment is safest for the exporter but may be unacceptable to the buyer. Letters of credit offer security but require strict document compliance. Documentary collections are cheaper but riskier. Open account is common in long-term relationships but requires credit insurance or a strong balance sheet. For intra-African trade, consider regional payment platforms and local currency settlement where available. Always specify the payment timeline, the documents required, and the consequences of delay. Delivery terms should be chosen deliberately. Incoterms 2020 defines responsibilities. EXW means the buyer collects from your premises. FOB means you deliver to the port and load. CIF means you arrange carriage and insurance to the destination port. DAP means you deliver to a named place, with import clearance handled by the buyer. DDP means you handle everything, including import duty. For most small and medium African exporters, FOB or CIF is practical. Avoid DDP unless you have a reliable customs agent in the destination country. Contract design turns agreement into enforceable rights. A good contract includes parties, recitals, definitions, scope, specification, quantity, price, currency, payment terms, delivery terms, inspection, warranties, force majeure, termination, confidentiality, governing law, and dispute resolution. In African trade, pay special attention to force majeure. Events such as strikes, port closures, currency controls, and political instability can disrupt performance. Define what counts as force majeure and what happens if it occurs. Include a change-in-law clause. If a government changes tariffs, standards, or export rules, the contract should say how the parties will adjust. Include an inspection clause. Specify who inspects, where, when, and according to what standard. Use recognized inspection companies or agreed laboratories. For agricultural products, define moisture content, foreign matter, and defects. For manufactured goods, define tolerances and testing methods. Include a quality dispute mechanism. Consider arbitration. The New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards has many African signatories, which makes arbitration awards more enforceable than court judgments. Choose a neutral seat and a recognized institution. Include mediation as a first step to preserve relationships. Address intellectual property if you are sharing designs, brands, or technology. Address anti-corruption and sanctions compliance. Many African governments and international buyers require warranties that no improper payments were made. Include audit rights where appropriate. Address data protection if personal data crosses borders. More African countries now have data protection laws, and non-compliance can create liability. Negotiate payment security. If the buyer is creditworthy, open account may be fine. If not, require a letter of credit, a bank guarantee, or credit insurance. For smaller transactions, mobile money and fintech platforms are increasingly viable, but confirm limits and regulatory coverage. Build a pricing model that shows the impact of each term. A simple spreadsheet can show how a 30-day delay in payment, a 2 percent currency depreciation, or a 10 percent increase in freight changes your margin. Use this model during negotiation to test offers quickly. Do not accept a price without checking the terms. A higher price with a 90-day payment term may be worse than a lower price with advance payment. Practice making and receiving offers. When you make an offer, state the price, the terms, and the validity period. When you receive an offer, repeat it back, ask clarifying questions, and take time to evaluate. Do not reject immediately. Counter with a package, not just a number. For example, you might accept the price if the buyer increases volume, shortens payment, or pays for certification. Document all changes. Use a term sheet or memorandum of understanding before the full contract. This reduces misunderstandings and speeds up legal drafting. Finally, review the contract with a lawyer who knows the relevant jurisdictions. Legal fees are an investment, not a cost. A poorly drafted contract can cost far more than the lawyer who would have fixed it. In the next lesson, we will cover cross-cultural communication and relationship management.

4. Cross-Cultural Communication and Relationship Management in African Trade

Africa is a continent of 54 countries, thousands of languages, and diverse business cultures. Effective trade negotiation requires cultural intelligence, not stereotypes. This lesson helps you communicate clearly, build trust, and manage relationships across African markets. Start with the concept of high-context and low-context communication. In high-context cultures, much meaning is conveyed through tone, timing, hierarchy, and non-verbal cues. In low-context cultures, meaning is explicit and written. Many African business settings blend both. A meeting may begin with extended greetings and relationship building, then move to detailed technical discussion. Your job is to observe and adapt, not to assume. Learn basic greetings in the local language. In Kenya, a simple habari or jambo goes a long way. In Senegal, nanga def. In Nigeria, good morning in the local language of your counterpart. This effort signals respect. Use titles and formal address until invited to be informal. In many African contexts, age and position carry authority. Address the most senior person first, but include everyone in the conversation. Avoid embarrassing anyone publicly. If you must correct a factual error, do it privately or frame it as a question. Build trust through consistency. Deliver what you promise, even in small matters. Reply to messages. Arrive on time or send word if delayed. Trust in African trade is often personal and network-based. A referral from a mutual contact is more valuable than a cold email. Use trade fairs, chambers of commerce, industry associations, and diaspora networks to build relationships. Understand the role of time. In some contexts, meetings start late and run long. In others, especially in commercial hubs, punctuality is expected. Ask your local contact about norms. Do not interpret lateness as disrespect without evidence. At the same time, protect your own schedule by setting clear agendas and confirming meetings in writing. Negotiate relationship and task separately. Spend time on the relationship first. Ask about family, health, and travel, but avoid intrusive questions. Share something about yourself. Then move to business. Rushing this phase can be seen as disrespectful, but spending too long can frustrate task-oriented counterparts. Read the room. Manage language barriers. If you are not fluent in the working language, use a professional interpreter. Brief the interpreter on technical terms and your objectives. Speak in short sentences. Avoid idioms, humor, and sarcasm, which do not translate well. Confirm important numbers and dates in writing. Use simple visuals and samples. For products, physical samples are powerful. For services, case studies and references work well. Understand hierarchy and decision-making. In some African firms and government agencies, decisions are made at the top. In others, technical committees recommend and leadership approves. Identify the real decision maker and the influencers. Do not bypass the local representative or agent, as this can cause loss of face and sabotage. If you are working with a local partner, define roles, commissions, and exclusivity clearly in writing. Address conflict carefully. In many African contexts, direct confrontation is avoided. Use indirect language, private meetings, and third-party mediators. Frame problems as shared challenges. Preserve dignity. If a dispute escalates, consider mediation before arbitration. Relationship repair is often cheaper than legal action. Understand gift-giving norms. In some cultures, small gifts are appropriate. In others, especially in public procurement, gifts can be seen as bribery. Follow the law and your company policy. When in doubt, do not give. Instead, offer value through training, market information, or introductions. Manage remote communication. Email is common but may be formal. WhatsApp is widely used for quick coordination in many African markets. Use the channel your counterpart prefers, but keep contractual communications in email or a formal system. Confirm receipt. Avoid sending large attachments without warning. Be mindful of data costs. Use voice notes sparingly and only when appropriate. Build a communication plan. Identify who speaks to whom, how often, and about what. Assign a local liaison if possible. Keep a record of commitments. Send a short summary after each meeting. This reduces misunderstandings and creates a paper trail. Develop cultural intelligence continuously. Read about the country, its history, and its business environment. Talk to people who have worked there. Learn from mistakes. Do not rely on a single source. Cultures are dynamic, and urban business cultures differ from rural ones. Finally, remember that relationships are the infrastructure of African trade. Contracts matter, but trust makes contracts work. A supplier who is reliable during a port strike, a buyer who pays during a currency crunch, and a partner who warns you about a regulatory change are worth more than a small price advantage. Invest in relationships before you need them. In the next lesson, we will cover negotiation tactics, ethics, and closing.

5. Tactics, Ethics, and Closing in African Trade Negotiations

Tactics are the moves you use within a negotiation to influence outcomes. Ethics are the principles that guide those moves. Closing is the process of turning agreement into commitment. This lesson covers all three in the African trade context. Start with common tactics and how to respond. The nibble is a request for a small extra after agreement, such as free shipping or an extra week of payment. Respond by asking for something in return or by referring to the agreed package. The flinch is a visible reaction to your offer. Do not change your offer just because the other side looks shocked. Ask what specifically is unacceptable. The good cop bad cop tactic uses one person to be tough and another to be reasonable. Identify the pattern and address the decision maker directly. The deadline tactic uses time pressure. Test whether the deadline is real. Ask what happens if the deadline slips. The limited authority tactic claims the negotiator must check with a boss. Ask to speak with the decision maker or agree on a process for approval. The walkout is a dramatic exit. Do not chase. Let them return or follow up calmly. The bogey is pretending an issue is unimportant to extract concessions elsewhere. Test priorities by asking questions and observing trade-offs. The salami is taking concessions one slice at a time. Keep a running total and ask for a package deal. The exploding offer has a short validity. Evaluate it on its merits, not on pressure. Use tactics ethically. Manipulation can win a deal but lose a relationship. In African trade, reputation is a long-term asset. Avoid lying, false urgency, and hidden fees. Do not exploit a counterparty who lacks information or bargaining power. Fair dealing builds repeat business and referrals. Understand ethical and legal requirements. Anti-corruption laws in Africa and abroad prohibit bribery of public officials. Many countries also regulate facilitation payments. Follow your company policy and the law. Avoid conflicts of interest. Disclose any personal relationship that could affect the deal. Protect confidential information. Respect intellectual property. Pay taxes and duties correctly. These practices protect you from penalties and build trust. Prepare for difficult situations. If asked for an improper payment, refuse clearly and offer a legitimate alternative. If you suspect fraud, verify documents and use official channels. If a counterparty behaves aggressively, stay calm and take a break. If you make a mistake, acknowledge it and propose a fix. Closing begins before the end of the negotiation. Summarize agreements as you go. Confirm each point in writing. Use a term sheet to capture the deal. Then draft the contract. Do not leave key issues for the lawyers to guess. Closing techniques include the summary close, where you recap all agreed points and ask for confirmation. The concession close, where you offer a final small concession in exchange for immediate agreement. The alternative close, where you present two acceptable options. The deadline close, where you state a real deadline. The trial close, where you ask how the proposal looks so far. Choose the technique that fits the relationship and the culture. In many African contexts, a direct hard close can feel pushy. A summary close with room for questions often works better. If the other side needs approval from a board or government committee, agree on a timeline and provide the documents they need. After closing, implement carefully. Send a thank-you message. Confirm the contract and the first steps. Assign responsibilities. Set a kickoff meeting. Monitor performance. Address problems early. A successful negotiation is judged by execution, not by the signing ceremony. Build a negotiation review habit. After each major negotiation, document what worked, what failed, and what you would do differently. Share lessons with your team. Over time, this creates institutional knowledge that improves your trade performance. Consider the broader context. African trade negotiations take place within a changing landscape. The AfCFTA is expanding intra-African trade. Digital platforms are reducing transaction costs. Regional value chains are growing. Climate change is affecting agriculture and logistics. Geopolitical shifts are changing demand for African minerals and energy. A skilled negotiator watches these trends and adapts. Finally, remember that negotiation is a human activity. It requires preparation, empathy, creativity, and discipline. You will not win every deal, and you should not try to. The goal is to create agreements that are profitable, sustainable, and respectful. When you do that, you build a reputation that opens doors across Africa and beyond. This concludes Module 11. Use these lessons as a practical toolkit, and keep learning from every negotiation you enter.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Negotiation Benchmarks, Documentation, and Compliance for African Trade Deals

Foreign trade negotiation in Africa is not won in the boardroom alone. It is won in the details: the tariff line, the phytosanitary certificate, the payment instrument, the Incoterm, the inspection report, the dispute clause. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the numbers, documents, and checklists that turn a negotiated agreement into a bankable transaction. Use it as a desk reference before you sign, before you ship, and before you release funds.

Africa's trade architecture has changed materially in the last decade. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) entered into force in 2019 and began trading under its preferential rules in 2021, progressively liberalising tariffs on 90 percent of tariff lines. Regional Economic Communities (RECs) such as ECOWAS, SADC, EAC, and COMESA operate their own preferential regimes, and overlapping membership means a single consignment may qualify under more than one framework. For a negotiator, that means the tariff and rules-of-origin conversation is not a formality â it is a price lever worth single-digit to double-digit percentage points on landed cost.

Any negotiation involving food, feed, agricultural chemicals, or veterinary products must treat compliance as non-negotiable. Do not agree to delivery without a valid sanitary or phytosanitary certificate where required, and never waive maximum residue limits (MRLs) or withholding periods to close a price gap. Pesticide residue above Codex or importing-country limits can result in cargo destruction, mandatory recall, port blacklisting, and criminal liability. For veterinary medicines and vaccines, confirm registration with the national regulator (e.g., NAQIA in Nigeria, KEPHIS and the Veterinary Medicines Directorate in Kenya, DALRRD in South Africa) before shipment. Cold-chain breaches for vaccines are a product-safety failure, not a commercial dispute. When in doubt, stop the shipment and escalate to the competent authority. Source: FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius, MRLs and Code of Practice (2023); WOAH, Aquatic and Terrestrial Animal Health Standards (2023).

How to Use These Benchmarks in a Live Negotiation

First, convert every benchmark into a walk-away number. If post-harvest loss on your maize is 20 percent and your buyer's rejection tolerance is 2 percent, your price must absorb the gap or your contract must shift the risk. Second, anchor the Incoterm to the compliance burden: FOB shifts export clearance to you, DDP shifts import duty and SPS risk to you â do not accept DDP on perishables without a landed-cost model. Third, tie payment to inspection. A 30 percent advance plus 70 percent against a clean inspection certificate from a mutually agreed surveyor (SGS, Bureau Veritas, Cotecna) is the standard structure in many African commodity trades. Fourth, insert an arbitration seat you can actually use â Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Kigali, or an international seat â and name the rules (UNCITRAL, ICC, or a regional centre such as the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration).

Currency risk deserves its own clause. Many African currencies have seen double-digit depreciation against hard currencies in recent years, and a price fixed in local currency for a 90-day delivery can erode your margin entirely. Negotiate either a hard-currency denomination, a price-adjustment formula tied to a published rate, or a shorter validity window with a re-pricing right. Similarly, force majeure must be drafted for African realities: port congestion, border closures, power outages, and civil disruption are foreseeable enough that a generic clause will be contested. Specify notice periods, mitigation duties, and a termination right after a defined suspension period.

Documentation Checklist by Transaction Stage

Before signature: proforma invoice, draft contract, rules-of-origin determination, and a compliance opinion on SPS and labelling. Before shipment: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, certificate of origin, phytosanitary or health certificate, inspection certificate, insurance certificate, and any import permit required by the destination authority. Before payment release: clean inspection report, proof of shipment, and confirmation that documents match the letter of credit terms exactly â banks reject on discrepancies, not on intent.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the tariff regime: Identify whether the deal qualifies under AfCFTA, a REC, or MFN, and obtain the correct certificate of origin before quoting a landed price.
  • Quantify your BATNA in cash: Know the net margin of your best alternative buyer or supplier, not just its headline price.
  • Match Incoterm to risk capacity: Do not accept DDP or DAP on perishables unless you have modelled duty, SPS, and cold-chain exposure.
  • Secure payment before risk transfer: Use LC, escrow, or advance-plus-inspection structures; avoid open account with new counterparties.
  • Verify SPS and registration status: Check importing-country MRLs, labelling rules, and product registration for food, feed, and veterinary goods.
  • Name the inspection body and standard: Agree the surveyor, sampling method, and tolerance limits in the contract, not after a dispute.
  • Draft a usable dispute clause: Specify arbitration seat, rules, language, and governing law that both parties can enforce.
  • Stress-test FX and force majeure: Include a currency-adjustment mechanism and a specific force majeure list with notice and termination triggers.
