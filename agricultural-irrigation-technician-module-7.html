1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Agricultural Irrigation Systems in Africa

Welcome to Module 7 of the Agricultural Irrigation Technician training. In this first lesson, we lay the foundation for everything that follows by exploring what irrigation means in the African context, why it matters for food security and farm profitability, and how a technician fits into the broader agricultural value chain. Africa has roughly 60 percent of the world's uncultivated arable land, yet only about 6 percent of cultivated land is irrigated, compared with over 20 percent in Asia and around 15 percent globally. This gap represents both a massive opportunity and a serious risk. Rain-fed agriculture dominates the continent, and a single dry spell can wipe out an entire season's income for a smallholder family. As an irrigation technician, your work directly reduces that risk. You are not just fixing pipes; you are protecting livelihoods, stabilizing food supply, and helping farmers move from subsistence to business-oriented production. Irrigation in Africa takes many forms. At the smallest scale, farmers use watering cans, treadle pumps, and bucket kits on plots of a few hundred square meters. At the medium scale, we see motorized pumps, drip lines, and sprinkler systems serving one to twenty hectares. At the large commercial scale, center pivots, lateral move systems, and sophisticated drip networks cover hundreds of hectares. Each scale demands different technical skills, but the underlying principles of water delivery, pressure, filtration, and maintenance remain the same. Your role is to understand these principles deeply enough to diagnose problems, recommend improvements, and train farmers to operate and maintain their systems profitably. The business case for irrigation is straightforward. A well-designed drip system can increase yields by 30 to 100 percent depending on the crop, while using 30 to 60 percent less water than furrow irrigation. For a farmer growing tomatoes, that could mean the difference between 8 tonnes per hectare and 20 tonnes per hectare. At a farmgate price of 500 shillings per kilogram, the additional revenue is substantial. But irrigation also introduces new costs: pump fuel or electricity, replacement parts, filters, and labor for monitoring. A good technician helps the farmer calculate whether the investment pays back within two or three seasons. This is where business-first thinking separates a competent technician from a mere repair person. You must be able to talk about payback periods, cost per cubic meter of water, and yield response curves, not just pipe diameters and pump curves. Climate change makes this work urgent. Rainfall patterns across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa have become more erratic. The long rains in Kenya and Tanzania now start later and end earlier. In the Horn of Africa, consecutive failed rainy seasons have pushed millions into food insecurity. Irrigation is one of the most powerful adaptation tools available. But it must be done right. Poorly designed irrigation can salinize soils, deplete groundwater, and create new problems. A technician who understands soil-water-plant relationships, water quality, and sustainable extraction limits is a true professional. Throughout this module, we will cover system components, design principles, installation practices, troubleshooting, and farmer training. You will learn how to assess a water source, calculate crop water requirements, select pumps and pipes, install filters and emitters, and maintain systems through the season. You will also learn how to work with farmers as business partners, not just clients. By the end of Module 7, you should be able to walk onto a farm, evaluate an existing irrigation system, identify inefficiencies, propose practical improvements, and train the farmer and farm workers to keep the system running. Let us begin with the core components of an irrigation system. Every system, from a bucket kit to a center pivot, has four functional parts: water source, pump or pressurization device, distribution network, and application device. The water source may be a river, borehole, well, dam, or municipal supply. The pump moves water and creates pressure. The distribution network includes pipes, fittings, valves, and filters. The application device delivers water to the crop: drippers, sprinklers, or furrows. Understanding how these parts interact is the key to diagnosis. If a drip system has uneven pressure, the problem could be a clogged filter, an undersized pipe, a worn pump impeller, or a leak in the mainline. A systematic approach saves time and money. We will also discuss the importance of record-keeping. A farmer who records daily pump hours, fuel consumption, water applied, and crop performance can make informed decisions. As a technician, you should encourage simple logbooks or mobile phone apps. In Kenya, for example, some irrigation service providers use SMS-based systems to remind farmers to flush filters and check emitters. These low-cost tools improve reliability and build trust. Finally, remember that irrigation is a service business. Your income depends on repeat customers, referrals, and long-term maintenance contracts. Farmers talk to each other. If you install a system that works well and you respond quickly when something breaks, you will build a reputation. If you cut corners, word will spread. Treat every farm as a demonstration site. In the next lesson, we will dive into water sources and pumping systems, including how to measure flow, calculate head, and select the right pump for African conditions.

2. Lesson 2: Water Sources, Pumps, and Power for African Farms

In this lesson, we focus on the heart of any irrigation system: the water source and the pump that moves water from source to field. Africa's water sources are diverse, and each has unique technical and business implications. Shallow wells and hand-dug wells are common in many regions, often less than 10 meters deep. They are inexpensive to develop but can run dry in the dry season. Boreholes can reach 50 to 200 meters and provide more reliable supply, but drilling costs are high, often 5,000 to 20,000 US dollars depending on depth and geology. Rivers and streams are free but seasonal and may require legal water permits. Dams and ponds store rainwater but lose water to evaporation and seepage. Municipal supplies are convenient but expensive and unreliable in many towns. As a technician, your first job is to assess the water source. You need to know three things: how much water is available, how reliable it is through the year, and what quality it is. Flow rate is measured in liters per second or cubic meters per hour. A simple bucket and stopwatch method works for small sources: fill a 20-liter bucket and time it. For larger sources, use a weir or a flow meter. Reliability requires local knowledge. Ask the farmer about the driest month and the lowest water level. If the source is a borehole, check the pump test data or hire a hydrogeologist for a proper assessment. Water quality matters because salts, iron, and sediments can clog emitters and damage pumps. A basic water test for pH, electrical conductivity, iron, and hardness is worth the small cost. Now let us talk about pumps. The two main categories are surface pumps and submersible pumps. Surface pumps sit above the water and use suction to draw water up to about 7 meters at sea level. They are used for rivers, shallow wells, and tanks. Submersible pumps are lowered into the water, usually in boreholes, and push water to the surface. They are more efficient for deep sources. Within these categories, you will encounter centrifugal pumps, helical rotor pumps, diaphragm pumps, and solar pumps. Centrifugal pumps are common for high-flow, low-head applications like filling a tank from a river. Helical rotor pumps handle dirty water and high head, making them popular for boreholes. Diaphragm pumps are self-priming and good for low-flow, high-head situations. Solar pumps use photovoltaic panels to power a DC motor. They have become very popular in Africa because they eliminate fuel costs and require less maintenance. However, they only work when the sun shines, so storage tanks or batteries are needed for night or cloudy-day irrigation. When selecting a pump, you must calculate total dynamic head, or TDH. TDH is the sum of static lift, friction losses in pipes and fittings, and pressure required at the application device. Static lift is the vertical distance from the water level to the highest point of discharge. Friction loss depends on pipe diameter, length, flow rate, and material. A common mistake is to undersize pipes, which increases friction and wastes energy. For example, a 32 mm pipe carrying 2 liters per second over 100 meters may lose 3 to 4 meters of head, while a 50 mm pipe loses less than 1 meter. The pump must be able to deliver the required flow at the calculated TDH. Pump curves show the relationship between flow and head. You want to select a pump whose best efficiency point is close to your duty point. Oversizing a pump wastes money and can cause water hammer. Undersizing it means poor performance and crop stress. Power sources in Africa include diesel, petrol, electricity, and solar. Diesel and petrol pumps are portable and powerful but have high fuel and maintenance costs. Electricity is cheaper per unit of energy but grid connections are often unavailable or unreliable. Solar is increasingly the best choice for small and medium systems, especially where fuel supply is difficult. A 1 kilowatt solar array can pump about 30,000 liters per day from a 20-meter head, enough for a one-hectare drip system. The payback period compared to diesel can be as short as one to two years. However, solar pumps require proper sizing, and you must account for panel degradation, dust, and shading. Batteries add cost and maintenance, so many farmers use a tank instead. A storage tank allows you to pump during the day and irrigate in the evening or morning, which reduces evaporation losses and gives you a buffer against cloudy weather. Tank sizing depends on daily water requirement and the number of days of autonomy you want. For a one-hectare tomato crop requiring 5 millimeters per day, that is 50 cubic meters per day, or 50,000 liters. A 10,000-liter tank gives only a few hours of buffer, so you may need a larger tank or direct pumping. In practice, many African farmers use a combination: a borehole with a solar submersible pump filling a 20,000-liter tank, then a small booster pump to pressurize the drip lines. This hybrid approach balances cost and reliability. As a technician, you must also consider maintenance. Pumps need regular checks: impeller wear, seal condition, bearing lubrication, and electrical connections. Solar panels need cleaning and occasional testing. Diesel engines need oil changes, fuel filter replacement, and air filter cleaning. A simple maintenance schedule can prevent 80 percent of breakdowns. Teach the farmer to keep a log of pump hours and fuel consumption. If fuel consumption rises suddenly, the pump may be worn or the system may have a leak. Finally, think about water rights and regulations. Many African countries require permits for borehole drilling and surface water abstraction. Help your clients understand the law. Illegal abstraction can lead to fines or well closure. Also, consider the environmental impact. Over-pumping an aquifer can lower the water table and hurt neighboring farmers. Sustainable irrigation means using water at a rate that the source can recharge. In the next lesson, we will cover the distribution network: pipes, fittings, valves, and filters, and how to design them for efficiency and durability.

3. Lesson 3: Designing and Installing Distribution Networks

The distribution network carries water from the pump or tank to the field. It includes mainlines, sub-mains, laterals, fittings, valves, and filters. A well-designed network delivers the right amount of water at the right pressure to every plant, with minimal energy loss and minimal cost. In this lesson, we will cover the principles of hydraulic design, pipe selection, filtration, and installation practices suitable for African farms. Let us start with the basic layout. Most irrigation systems follow a tree structure: the pump or tank feeds a mainline, which branches into sub-mains, which feed laterals. Laterals carry emitters or sprinklers. The goal is to keep pressure variation across the system within acceptable limits, usually plus or minus 10 percent. If pressure is too low at the end of a lateral, the last emitters will deliver less water, and those plants will suffer. If pressure is too high, emitters may pop off or mist, and pipes may burst. To achieve even pressure, you must size pipes correctly. The key formula is the Hazen-Williams equation, but you do not need to memorize it. You can use simple tables or smartphone apps. The main factors are flow rate, pipe diameter, pipe length, and pipe material. As a rule of thumb, keep water velocity below 1.5 meters per second in mainlines and below 1.0 meter per second in laterals. Higher velocities cause friction loss and water hammer. For a small drip system, a 32 mm mainline can carry about 2 liters per second. A 50 mm mainline can carry about 5 liters per second. If you need more flow, go bigger. It is almost always cheaper to buy a larger pipe than to buy a bigger pump or pay for extra fuel. Pipe materials in Africa include PVC, HDPE, and galvanized steel. PVC is rigid, cheap, and easy to glue, but it becomes brittle in sunlight and can crack if not handled carefully. HDPE is flexible, UV-resistant, and comes in coils, which reduces the number of fittings. It is excellent for buried mainlines and for laterals that need to be moved. Galvanized steel is strong but expensive and corrodes. For most small and medium farms, HDPE is the best choice. When buying pipes, check the pressure rating. A pipe rated at 6 bar is fine for most surface systems, but if you have a deep borehole pump, you may need 10 bar or higher. Fittings are a common source of leaks. Use compression fittings for HDPE and solvent-weld fittings for PVC. Avoid cheap fittings that crack after one season. Always use the correct size and type. For example, a 16 mm barbed connector for drip tape is different from a 16 mm compression fitting for HDPE. Keep a supply of spare fittings on hand. Valves control flow and isolate sections for maintenance. You need a gate valve or ball valve at the pump outlet, and smaller valves on each sub-main or lateral. A ball valve is better for on/off control, while a gate valve is better for throttling. Install a check valve after the pump to prevent backflow, which can damage the pump and contaminate the water source. A pressure relief valve protects the system from surges. For drip systems, a pressure regulator is essential. It reduces the pressure from the mainline to the required level for the emitters, usually 1 to 2 bar. Without a regulator, emitters will pop off or clog. Filtration is the most important part of a drip system. Drip emitters have tiny openings, often less than 1 millimeter. Sand, silt, algae, and chemical precipitates can clog them. You need at least a screen filter or a disc filter. A screen filter has a mesh size measured in microns. For drip, use 120 to 200 mesh, which is about 75 to 125 microns. A disc filter is more robust and easier to clean. For dirty water, use a sand media filter before the screen filter. Always install the filter after the pump and before the mainline. Include a pressure gauge before and after the filter so you can monitor clogging. When the pressure difference exceeds 0.5 bar, clean the filter. Teach the farmer to flush the filter daily or every other day. A simple rule: if the water is not clear, filter it. If it is clear, still filter it. Now let us talk about installation. Good installation prevents 90 percent of future problems. Start by marking the layout with stakes and string. Dig trenches for buried pipes to a depth of 30 to 50 centimeters, deeper if vehicles will cross. Slope the trench slightly so you can drain it for maintenance. Lay pipes on a bed of sand or soft soil to protect them from rocks. Do not stretch HDPE pipes too tight; they expand and contract with temperature. Leave some slack. Use a pipe cutter or hacksaw to make clean cuts. Deburr the ends. For compression fittings, lubricate the rubber ring with soapy water and push the pipe in until it stops. For solvent-weld PVC, clean the surfaces with primer, apply cement, and twist a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. Allow 24 hours to cure before pressurizing. Install filters and valves in accessible locations. Do not bury them. Use a valve box if necessary. For drip laterals, lay them along the crop rows. Keep them straight and avoid kinks. Use stakes or clips to hold them in place. At the end of each lateral, install a flush valve. This allows you to flush debris out of the line. Flush valves are cheap and save hours of cleaning. For sprinkler systems, ensure the sprinkler spacing matches the manufacturer's recommendation. For example, if a sprinkler has a 12-meter diameter, space them 12 meters apart in a square pattern. Overlapping is essential for uniform coverage. Do not mix sprinkler types on the same lateral; they have different flow rates and pressure requirements. After installation, test the system. Pressurize it gradually. Check for leaks at every fitting. Measure pressure at the first and last emitter. If the difference is more than 10 percent, adjust the design. Measure flow at the pump and compare it to the design flow. If it is lower, check for clogged filters, closed valves, or a worn pump. If it is higher, check for leaks or open flush valves. Record all measurements. This baseline helps you troubleshoot later. Finally, think about cost. A typical drip system for one hectare in Africa costs between 1,500 and 4,000 US dollars, depending on the source, pump, and filtration. Sprinkler systems cost 1,000 to 3,000 dollars. These are significant investments for smallholders. Your design should minimize cost without sacrificing performance. Use the smallest pipe that meets pressure requirements. Use fewer valves by grouping laterals. Use gravity where possible. For example, if you have a tank on a hill, you can irrigate without a pump. A tank 10 meters above the field provides 1 bar of pressure, enough for drip. This simple approach saves fuel and maintenance. In the next lesson, we will cover application devices: drippers, sprinklers, and micro-sprinklers, and how to choose the right one for different crops and soils.

4. Lesson 4: Application Devices and Crop Water Management

The application device is where irrigation meets the plant. It determines how much water reaches the root zone, how evenly it is distributed, and how much is lost to evaporation or runoff. In this lesson, we will cover the main types of application devices used in Africa: drip, sprinkler, micro-sprinkler, and furrow. We will also discuss crop water requirements and scheduling, which are essential for profitable irrigation. Let us start with drip irrigation. Drip, also called trickle irrigation, delivers water directly to the root zone through emitters or drippers. It is the most efficient method, with application efficiencies of 85 to 95 percent. It works well for row crops, vegetables, orchards, and vines. It is also suitable for undulating land and for water-scarce areas. There are two main types: inline drip tape and online drippers. Inline drip tape is thin-walled and cheap, often used for one or two seasons. It has emitters built into the tape at fixed spacing, usually 20 to 30 centimeters. Online drippers are attached to a rigid lateral pipe at variable spacing. They are more durable and can be cleaned or replaced. Drip emitters have flow rates of 1 to 8 liters per hour. The most common are 2 and 4 liters per hour. The choice depends on soil type. Sandy soils need higher flow rates and closer spacing because water moves downward quickly. Clay soils need lower flow rates and wider spacing because water spreads laterally. A good rule: for sandy soil, use 4 liters per hour emitters spaced 30 centimeters apart. For clay soil, use 2 liters per hour emitters spaced 50 centimeters apart. Drip systems require clean water and good filtration. They also require regular flushing. If you install drip, you must commit to maintenance. A clogged drip system is worse than no system at all because the farmer thinks water is being applied when it is not. Teach farmers to check emitters weekly. Pull up a few emitters and see if water is coming out. If not, flush the lateral and clean the filter. For severe clogging, use a chlorine or acid treatment. Chlorine kills algae and bacteria. Acid dissolves mineral deposits. Use these chemicals carefully and follow label instructions. Sprinkler irrigation is another common method. It simulates rainfall by spraying water into the air. It is suitable for cereals, pulses, fodder, and some vegetables. It is less efficient than drip, with efficiencies of 70 to 85 percent, because of evaporation and wind drift. But it has advantages: it can be used for frost protection, it does not require filtration as fine as drip, and it can be moved from field to field. The main types are hand-move, solid-set, and traveling sprinklers. Hand-move systems are cheap but labor-intensive. Solid-set systems are permanent and convenient but expensive. Traveling sprinklers, like rain guns, are good for large fields but require high pressure and have poor uniformity. When selecting sprinklers, consider the application rate. If the application rate exceeds the soil infiltration rate, water will run off. For clay soils, infiltration rate may be 5 millimeters per hour. For sandy soils, it may be 20 millimeters per hour. Choose sprinklers with an application rate below the infiltration rate. Also consider droplet size. Small droplets are more prone to wind drift and evaporation. Large droplets can damage delicate crops. Micro-sprinklers are a hybrid between drip and sprinkler. They deliver water in a small spray or umbrella pattern, usually 1 to 2 meters in diameter. They are used for orchards, bananas, and some vegetables. They have flow rates of 20 to 100 liters per hour. They are more robust than drip emitters and easier to inspect. But they require more water and have higher evaporation losses than drip. They are a good choice for sandy soils where drip emitters clog easily. Furrow irrigation is the oldest method. Water is released into small channels between crop rows. It is cheap and simple but inefficient, with efficiencies of 40 to 60 percent. It is still used for rice, sugarcane, and some vegetables. It requires level land and careful water control. It can cause waterlogging and salinity if not managed well. As a technician, you should recommend improving furrow irrigation with surge flow or gated pipes, but for most smallholders, drip or sprinkler is a better investment. Now let us talk about crop water requirements. Plants need water for transpiration, which drives growth and cooling. The amount of water a crop needs depends on the crop type, growth stage, climate, and soil. The standard method is to calculate reference evapotranspiration, or ET0, and multiply by a crop coefficient, or Kc. ET0 can be estimated from pan evaporation or from weather data. In Africa, many extension services provide ET0 values. For example, in the Rift Valley of Kenya, ET0 may be 4 to 6 millimeters per day. A mature tomato crop has a Kc of about 1.0, so it needs 4 to 6 millimeters per day. That is 40 to 60 cubic meters per hectare per day, or 40,000 to 60,000 liters. A drip system with 2 liter per hour emitters spaced 30 centimeters apart and rows 1 meter apart has about 3.3 emitters per square meter. If each emitter runs for 2 hours, it applies 6.6 liters per square meter, which is 6.6 millimeters. That matches the requirement. You can adjust the run time to match the crop stage. Young seedlings need less water. Flowering and fruiting stages need more. Over-irrigation wastes water, leaches nutrients, and can cause root diseases. Under-irrigation stresses the crop and reduces yield. The best way to schedule irrigation is to use soil moisture sensors or tensiometers. These devices measure how much water is available to the plant. A tensiometer costs 50 to 100 US dollars and can save thousands of dollars in water and yield. If sensors are not available, use a simple feel method: dig a handful of soil and squeeze it. If it holds together and leaves a slight moisture mark on your palm, it is fine. If it crumbles, it is too dry. If it drips, it is too wet. Teach farmers to check soil moisture daily at 15 and 30 centimeters depth. Another useful tool is a simple rain gauge. If it rains 10 millimeters, you can skip one irrigation. But be careful: not all rain infiltrates. A heavy storm may run off. A light shower may evaporate. Use your judgment. Finally, consider fertigation. Fertigation is the injection of soluble fertilizers into the irrigation water. It is very efficient because nutrients go directly to the root zone. It saves labor and improves nutrient use efficiency. But it requires a fertilizer tank and a venturi injector or a dosing pump. It also requires careful calculation. Too much fertilizer can burn roots. Too little has no effect. Start with a simple program: apply 50 percent of the recommended nitrogen through fertigation during the growing season. Split applications weekly. Use a soil test to guide phosphorus and potassium. In the next lesson, we will cover troubleshooting, maintenance, and farmer training, which are the keys to long-term success.

5. Lesson 5: Troubleshooting, Maintenance, and Farmer Training

In this final lesson, we bring everything together. A well-designed and installed irrigation system will still fail if it is not maintained. Your job as a technician is not just to fix problems but to prevent them. You do this through systematic troubleshooting, scheduled maintenance, and effective farmer training. Let us start with troubleshooting. When a farmer calls you and says the system is not working, you need a logical approach. Do not guess. Follow the water. Start at the source. Is there water in the river, well, or tank? Is the pump running? Is it priming? Is the fuel tank full? Is the solar panel clean and producing power? Check the pump output. If it is low, the impeller may be worn, the suction line may have a leak, or the filter may be clogged. Check the pressure gauge at the pump. If pressure is high but flow is low, there is a blockage downstream. If pressure is low and flow is low, the pump is failing or the source is dry. Move to the filters. A clogged filter is the most common cause of poor performance. Check the pressure difference across the filter. If it is more than 0.5 bar, clean or replace the filter element. For sand filters, backwash them. For screen filters, remove the screen and rinse it. For disc filters, take apart the discs and clean them. Next, check the mainline and sub-mains. Look for leaks. A leak reduces pressure to the rest of the system. Common leak points are fittings, valves, and joints. Repair leaks immediately. A small leak can waste thousands of liters per day. Then check the laterals. For drip systems, check emitters. If some are clogged, flush the lateral. If all are clogged, the problem is upstream. For sprinklers, check for broken nozzles, worn seals, or misaligned heads. A single broken sprinkler can waste water and create dry spots. Use a pressure gauge and a flow meter to measure performance. Compare to the design specifications. If you do not have design specs, create them. Measure the flow at the pump, the pressure at the first and last emitter, and the application rate. Write it down. This baseline helps you diagnose future problems. Now let us talk about maintenance. Preventive maintenance is cheaper than emergency repairs. Create a maintenance schedule for each farm. Daily tasks: check pump oil and fuel, clean filters, inspect for leaks, and record pump hours. Weekly tasks: flush drip laterals, check emitters, clean solar panels, and inspect sprinklers. Monthly tasks: check pump impeller and seals, test pressure relief valves, clean tanks, and calibrate injectors. Seasonal tasks: before planting, test the entire system. Replace worn parts. After harvest, drain pipes, clean filters, and store pumps in a dry place. For solar pumps, check battery water levels if lead-acid, and test panel output. For diesel pumps, change oil and filters every 100 to 200 hours. Keep a spare parts inventory. Common spares include filter elements, emitters, sprinkler nozzles, fittings, and pump seals. A farmer who waits for a part to be delivered from the city may lose a week of irrigation. That can cost a season. Teach farmers to keep a small stock of critical spares. Now let us talk about farmer training. You can install the best system in the world, but if the farmer does not know how to use it, it will fail. Training is not a one-time event. It is a process. Start with a hands-on demonstration. Show the farmer how to start the pump, check the filter, flush the laterals, and adjust the pressure. Let them do it while you watch. Correct mistakes gently. Use simple language. Avoid jargon. Use local units: liters, buckets, hours. Explain why each step matters. For example, do not just say clean the filter. Say if you do not clean the filter, the emitters will clog, and your tomatoes will not get water. That will reduce your harvest and your income. Use visual aids. A simple drawing of the system with arrows showing water flow helps. A laminated card with daily, weekly, and monthly tasks is useful. Many farmers have mobile phones. Create a WhatsApp group for your clients. Send reminders about maintenance. Share photos of problems and solutions. This builds a community of practice. Also, train more than one person on the farm. If the owner is away, a worker should be able to operate the system. Train both men and women. In many African households, women do most of the farming but are excluded from technical training. Include them. Their participation improves adoption and success. Finally, think about business. Your training should include cost tracking. Teach the farmer to record fuel, parts, and labor costs. Calculate the cost per cubic meter of water. Compare it to the value of the crop. If the cost is too high, look for ways to reduce it. Maybe switch to solar. Maybe reduce run time. Maybe grow a higher-value crop. Irrigation is an investment. It must generate a return. As a technician, you can also offer service contracts. For a monthly fee, you visit the farm, check the system, clean filters, and make minor repairs. This gives you steady income and gives the farmer peace of mind. Many farmers are willing to pay for reliability. In conclusion, Module 7 has covered the full cycle of irrigation: understanding the context, assessing water sources, selecting pumps, designing distribution networks, choosing application devices, managing crop water, troubleshooting, maintaining, and training. You are now equipped to work as a professional irrigation technician in Africa. Remember that your work has a direct impact on food security, farmer incomes, and climate resilience. Do it with integrity, skill, and a business mindset. Keep learning. Attend refresher courses. Join professional associations. Share your knowledge. The demand for irrigation technicians in Africa is growing. By mastering these skills, you will not only build a successful career but also contribute to a more food-secure and prosperous continent. Thank you for your attention, and good luck in the field.

<h3>1. Commissioning Checks Before the First Irrigation Cycle</h3>
<p>Commissioning is the point where the installer's work is tested against the design. On African farms, the most common cause of early system failure is not poor equipment but poor commissioning: pumps run at the wrong duty point, filters are undersized, and pressure at the last emitter is never measured. The technician should complete the following before handing the system to the farmer.</p>

<h3>2. Water Quality Thresholds for Irrigation</h3>
<p>Water quality is a business issue, not just an agronomic one. Salinity, sodicity and specific ion toxicity can destroy soil structure and reduce the value of irrigated land within a few seasons. In African contexts, salinity is most common in arid and semi-arid zones (e.g. parts of Kenya, Ethiopia, Sudan, Namibia) and in areas with intensive groundwater use. <p>For African horticulture exporters, water quality testing is increasingly a market access requirement. Buyers supplying European supermarkets often require evidence of irrigation water testing against WHO and GlobalG.A.P. criteria. A technician who can sample, interpret and record water quality results adds direct commercial value to the farm.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:12px 16px;margin:18px 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Water, Chemicals and Health</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7a1f1f;">Irrigation water can carry <strong>faecal coliforms, Salmonella, E. coli, helminth eggs and pesticide residues</strong>. Never drink from or wash hands in irrigation canals, dams or pump outlets. When handling fertilisers, acids, chlorine or pesticides injected through the system, wear <strong>gloves, goggles and a respirator</strong>, and never mix chemicals in the field without a safe water source nearby. Chlorine and acid dosing must be done upwind, with the pump off, and with a Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) on site. If a worker shows symptoms of chemical exposure, move them to fresh air, remove contaminated clothing, wash with clean water and seek medical help immediately. Store all agrochemicals in a locked, ventilated store away from food, feed and children.</p>
</div>

<h3>3. Field Records That Protect Profit</h3>
<p>Record-keeping is the difference between a technician who fixes problems and one who prevents them. On commercial African farms, the following records are standard and should be kept for at least three seasons.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pump log:</strong> hours run, fuel or kWh used, flow rate, pressure at pump outlet.</li>
<li><strong>Water quality log:</strong> date, EC, pH, SAR, chloride, boron, lab reference.</li>
<li><strong>Maintenance log:</strong> filter cleaning, emitter flushing, valve checks, repairs.</li>
<li><strong>Irrigation schedule:</strong> crop, block, date, duration, mm applied, rainfall.</li>
<li><strong>Energy log:</strong> solar array output or diesel consumption per irrigation event.</li>
<li><strong>Yield and cost log:</strong> yield per block, input cost, gross margin per hectare.</li>
</ul>
<p>These records allow the farmer and technician to calculate cost per cubic metre of water applied, energy cost per hectare, and the payback period of the irrigation investment. In many African contexts, irrigation payback is 25 years for high-value horticulture and 510 years for staple crops, depending on energy source and market access.</p>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm pump duty point is within 10% of design flow and head before first irrigation.</li>
<li>Measure pressure and flow at the first and last emitter; record variation.</li>
<li>Test water for EC, pH, SAR, chloride, boron and, for vegetables, faecal coliform.</li>
<li>Check filter pressure loss; clean if above 0.7 bar.</li>
<li>Flush all laterals at 0.5 m/s until water runs clear.</li>
<li>Inspect every joint, valve and fitting for leaks; repair before handover.</li>
<li>Set up pump, water quality, maintenance and irrigation schedule logs.</li>
<li>Train the farmer and at least one worker on safe chemical handling and emergency response.</li>
</ol>
