Lessons

1. Common Onion Pests: Thrips, Onion Maggot, and Nematodes

Thrips are tiny, slender insects about 1-2 mm long. They feed by sucking sap from onion leaves, causing silvery streaks and curled tips. In Africa, the onion thrips (Thrips tabaci) is most common. Scout by shaking plants over a white sheet of paper; if you see 10 or more thrips per plant, treat. Use yellow sticky traps (10 traps per acre) to monitor. Spray with neem oil (3 ml per litre of water) every 7 days, or use insecticidal soap. For severe cases, apply imidacloprid (0.5 ml per litre) but rotate with other chemicals to avoid resistance.

Onion maggot (Delia antiqua) larvae tunnel into bulbs, causing wilting and rot. Adults are small grey flies that lay eggs near the base of plants. Prevent by rotating cropsdon't plant onions where onions or related crops grew in the last 3 years. Use row covers (fine mesh) immediately after planting. Apply beneficial nematodes (Steinernema feltiae) to soil at 1 million per square metre. If maggots appear, drench soil with spinosad (1 ml per litre) around plant bases.

Nematodes are microscopic worms that attack roots, causing stunted growth and yellowing. Root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) are common in sandy soils. Test soil before planting: take 20 soil cores per acre, mix, and send to a lab. If nematodes are present, solarize soil by covering with clear plastic for 6 weeks during hot season. Plant marigolds (Tagetes erecta) as a cover cropthey release compounds that kill nematodes. Apply neem cake (200 g per square metre) before planting. Chemical nematicides like fenamiphos are dangerous; use only as last resort with protective gear.

Always inspect your crop twice a week. Early detection saves harvest. Keep records of pest sightings to plan rotations and treatments.

2. Common Diseases: Downy Mildew, Botrytis, and Purple Blotch

Downy mildew (Peronospora destructor) appears as pale, elongated patches on leaves, often with a fuzzy grey-purple growth on the underside. It spreads fast in cool, humid weather (15-22C) with leaf wetness. To control, plant resistant varieties like 'Red Creole' or 'Texas Grano'. Space plants 10-15 cm apart for airflow. Water early in the day so leaves dry before evening. If symptoms appear, spray with copper oxychloride (3 g per litre) every 10 days. Remove and burn infected leaves. In severe cases, use metalaxyl (2 g per litre) but rotate with copper to prevent resistance.

Botrytis leaf blight and neck rot (Botrytis squamosa and B. allii) cause white spots on leaves and soft rot in bulbs during storage. The fungus survives in soil and crop debris. Practice 3-year rotation with cereals. Avoid overhead irrigation; use drip irrigation. Harvest when necks are dry and tops fall over. Cure bulbs in a warm, dry, ventilated area for 2-3 weeks. Store at 0-4C with low humidity. If botrytis appears in field, spray with iprodione (2 ml per litre) or Bacillus subtilis (5 g per litre) every 7-10 days. Remove infected plants immediately.

Purple blotch (Alternaria porri) causes small white spots that enlarge into purple lesions with concentric rings. It thrives in warm, wet conditions (25-30C). Use certified disease-free seed. Treat seeds with hot water at 50C for 20 minutes before planting. Apply potassium bicarbonate (5 g per litre) weekly as a preventive. For active infection, spray with mancozeb (2.5 g per litre) or chlorothalonil (2 g per litre) every 7 days. Alternate chemicals to avoid resistance. Remove crop debris after harvest and burn it.

Always wear gloves and a mask when handling fungicides. Keep a field diary to track disease outbreaks and treatments.

3. Scouting and Monitoring Techniques

Scouting means walking your onion field regularly to check for pests and diseases. Do it at least twice a week, early morning or late afternoon when pests are active. Use a systematic pattern: walk in a zigzag or 'W' shape across the field, stopping at 10 random spots per acre. At each spot, inspect 5 plants. Look at leaves, stems, and soil around the base. Carry a hand lens (10x magnification) to see tiny pests like thrips. Also bring a notebook, a white sheet of paper, and a small trowel.

For thrips, shake a plant over white paper and count the insects. Threshold: treat if you find 10 or more thrips per plant. For onion maggot, look for wilting plants and dig around the base to find larvae or pupae. For nematodes, check roots for galls or swellingsdig up a few plants and examine roots. For diseases, note leaf spots, fuzzy growth, or wilting. Take photos with your phone for records. Use yellow sticky traps (10 per acre) and pheromone traps for moths. Check traps weekly and count catches.

Keep a scouting log with date, location, pest/disease found, and severity (low, medium, high). This helps you decide when to act. For example, if you see downy mildew on 5% of plants, spray copper immediately. If you see only 1-2 thrips per plant, wait and monitor. Share your log with other farmers to track regional outbreaks. Use a smartphone app like 'Plantix' to identify diseases from photos.

Set action thresholds: thrips10 per plant; downy mildewany sign; botrytisfirst spots; purple blotchfirst lesions. Train a worker to scout when you are away. Remember, early detection reduces pesticide use and saves money. Clean your tools and boots between fields to avoid spreading pathogens.

4. Biological and Cultural Control Methods

Biological control uses living organisms to manage pests. For thrips, release predatory mites (Amblyseius cucumeris) at 100 mites per square metre every 2 weeks. Ladybugs (Coccinella septempunctata) eat aphids and thripsattract them by planting sunflowers or dill nearby. For onion maggot, apply beneficial nematodes (Steinernema feltiae) to soil at 1 million per square metre. For nematodes, grow marigolds (Tagetes spp.) as a cover crop before onions; their roots release a compound that kills nematodes. Also, spray Bacillus subtilis (5 g per litre) for botrytis and purple blotch. These methods are safe for you and the environment.

Cultural control means changing how you farm to prevent pests. Rotate crops: never plant onions after onions, garlic, or leeks for at least 3 years. Plant cereals like maize or sorghum in between. Use certified disease-free seeds and treat them with hot water (50C for 20 minutes) before sowing. Space plants 10-15 cm apart to improve airflow and reduce humidity. Water early in the day with drip irrigation instead of overhead sprinklers. Remove and burn crop debris after harvest. Solarize soil by covering with clear plastic for 6 weeks in hot season to kill nematodes and fungi.

Plant resistant varieties: 'Red Creole' resists downy mildew; 'Texas Grano' tolerates purple blotch. Use row covers (fine mesh) to keep out onion maggot flies. Mulch with straw to reduce soil splash and weed growth. Encourage natural enemies by planting border crops like marigolds and coriander. Keep field clean: remove weeds that host pests. Store bulbs properly: cure for 2-3 weeks in dry, ventilated shade, then store at 0-4C.

Combine these methods for best results. For example, rotate crops, use row covers, and release beneficial insects. This reduces chemical use and builds a healthy farm. Always observe your field to see what works.

5. Chemical Control: Safe Pesticide Use

Chemical pesticides can control severe pest outbreaks, but they must be used safely. Always read the label before buying. Choose pesticides registered for onions, like imidacloprid for thrips, spinosad for maggots, copper oxychloride for downy mildew, and mancozeb for purple blotch. Buy from reputable agro-dealers and check expiry dates. Never use banned chemicals like DDT or lindane. Store pesticides in a locked cabinet away from children, food, and animals.

Wear protective gear: gloves, long-sleeved shirt, trousers, boots, goggles, and a face mask. Mix chemicals outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Use clean water and measure accurately with a measuring cupnever guess. For example, mix 0.5 ml imidacloprid per litre of water for thrips. Spray early morning or late evening when bees are not active. Avoid spraying on windy days or before rain. Use a calibrated knapsack sprayer; rinse it three times after use and spray the rinse water on a non-crop area.

Follow pre-harvest intervals (PHI): imidacloprid7 days; spinosad3 days; copper1 day; mancozeb7 days. Do not harvest before PHI or you risk pesticide residues. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, alternate imidacloprid with spinosad every 10 days. Never mix more than two chemicals unless the label says it's safe. Keep a record of spray dates, chemicals, and rates.

Dispose of empty containers by triple-rinsing and puncturing them, then bury or take to a collection point. Never reuse pesticide containers. If you feel dizzy or sick after spraying, move to fresh air and seek medical help. Teach workers about safety. Remember, chemicals are a last resortuse them only when scouting shows thresholds are exceeded.

6. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Plan

An IPM plan combines all methodsscouting, cultural, biological, and chemicalto manage pests with minimal harm. Start by scouting twice a week. Keep a log. Set action thresholds: thrips10 per plant; downy mildewany sign; botrytisfirst spots; purple blotchfirst lesions. When threshold is reached, act. First, try cultural and biological controls. For example, if thrips are at 8 per plant, release predatory mites and spray neem oil. Only if they exceed 10, use imidacloprid. This saves money and protects beneficial insects.

Create a year-round plan. Before planting: test soil for nematodes, solarize if needed, and choose resistant varieties. Plant certified seed treated with hot water. Space plants 10-15 cm apart. Use drip irrigation and mulch. During growth: scout regularly, remove infected plants, and apply biocontrols. If disease appears, spray copper or Bacillus subtilis. After harvest: remove and burn debris, rotate to a cereal crop, and store bulbs properly. Keep records of every action.

Work with neighbors to manage pests regionally. If everyone rotates crops and destroys debris, pest pressure drops. Attend training sessions and share knowledge. Use smartphone apps like 'Plantix' for diagnosis. Set aside a small budget for biocontrols and safe chemicals. For a 1-acre onion field, an IPM plan might cost: neem oil (500 ml) $10; predatory mites $20; copper fungicide $15; sticky traps $5. Total $50, much less than weekly chemical sprays.

Review your plan each season. What worked? What didn't? Adjust. For example, if thrips were severe, plant more marigolds next time. IPM is flexible and site-specific. It protects your health, the environment, and your profits. Start small, be consistent, and teach others.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management in Onion Farming

Onion farming in Africa is a high-value enterprise, but pests and diseases can wipe out 30100% of a harvest if left unmanaged. For the commercial grower, every intervention must be justified by return on investment (ROI). This practical reference consolidates the key thresholds, control options, and decision rules you need to protect yield and profit. It is designed as a field-side companion to the seven lessons in this module.

Across Africa, the most economically damaging onion pests are thrips (Thrips tabaci), onion maggot (Delia antiqua), and root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.). The most destructive diseases are downy mildew (Peronospora destructor), Botrytis leaf blight and neck rot (Botrytis squamosa and B. allii), and purple blotch (Alternaria porri). Your management plan must integrate scouting, cultural methods, biologicals, and only when thresholds are exceeded safe chemical control.

Scouting and Monitoring Practical Protocol

Scouting is the backbone of any IPM plan. For a commercial onion farm, walk a W-pattern across each field weekly, inspecting at least 25 plants per hectare. Record pest counts, disease incidence, and weather data (temperature, humidity, rainfall) in a field notebook or mobile app. In Africa, the most common mistake is scouting only when damage is visible by then, thrips populations have exploded and downy mildew has already sporulated. Scout before symptoms appear, especially during cool, humid mornings when downy mildew is most active.

Use yellow sticky traps for thrips and maggot flies (1 trap per 0.5 ha). For nematodes, take soil samples pre-plant from 1015 random points per field and submit to a local lab or use a simple root-gall index at harvest. For diseases, check the undersides of leaves for fuzzy growth (downy mildew) and the leaf tips for purple-centered lesions (purple blotch). Keep a field history log this is your business intelligence. It tells you which fields are prone to which problems, and it justifies your input spend to buyers and lenders.

Biological and Cultural Control Methods

Cultural controls are the cheapest and most sustainable. Crop rotation with non-allium crops (maize, beans, sorghum) for at least 3 years breaks nematode and maggot cycles. Sanitation removing crop debris and controlling weeds reduces overwintering sites for Botrytis and purple blotch. Certified seed and seed treatment prevent maggot and smut. Resistant varieties (e.g., 'Red Creole', 'Bombay Red', 'Texas Grano') offer partial resistance to downy mildew and purple blotch. Drip irrigation instead of overhead irrigation reduces leaf wetness and downy mildew pressure.

Biological controls include Bacillus subtilis and Trichoderma harzianum for soil-borne diseases, neem-based products (Azadirachta indica) for thrips, and pheromone traps for maggot monitoring. These are compatible with IPM and often have lower residue risk, which matters for export markets. In Africa, neem is locally available and cost-effective; a simple neem seed extract spray can reduce thrips populations by 4060% when applied weekly.

Chemical Control: Safe Pesticide Use

Chemicals are a last resort. When thresholds are exceeded, choose selective, approved pesticides and rotate active ingredients to prevent resistance. For thrips, use spinosad or abamectin; for downy mildew, use metalaxyl or mancozeb; for purple blotch, use chlorothalonil or iprodione. Always follow the label dosage, interval, and pre-harvest interval (PHI). In Africa, many growers lose export contracts because of pesticide residues. Keep records of every spray: date, product, rate, and PHI.

SAFETY WARNING Pesticide Handling

Never spray without personal protective equipment (PPE): gloves, mask, long sleeves, and boots. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while spraying. Store pesticides in a locked, ventilated area away from food and children. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them before disposal never reuse them for water or food. Observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) strictly; harvesting before PHI can leave illegal residues and endanger consumers. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or have blurred vision after spraying, stop immediately and seek medical help. In case of poisoning, contact your local poison control centre or the nearest health facility.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Plan Business-First

Your IPM plan should be written, costed, and reviewed each season. It must include: (1) pre-season soil testing and field history review; (2) variety selection based on market and disease pressure; (3) a scouting calendar with assigned labour; (4) threshold-based action rules; (5) a list of approved inputs with costs; (6) a spray diary and PHI tracker; and (7) a post-season ROI evaluation. The goal is not zero pests it is maximum profit per hectare. A well-run IPM plan typically reduces pesticide costs by 3050% while maintaining or increasing marketable yield.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Scout fields weekly using a W-pattern; record pest counts and weather.
  • Know your economic thresholds do not spray below them.
  • Rotate crops with non-allium species for at least 3 years.
  • Use certified seed and treat seed before planting.
  • Remove crop debris and control weeds to break disease cycles.
  • Prefer biologicals (neem, Bacillus, Trichoderma) before chemicals.
  • If spraying, wear PPE, follow label rates, and respect PHI.
  • Keep a field log and calculate ROI after every season.
