Lessons

1. Cleaning and sorting produce for better prices

Buyers pay more for clean, uniform produce. Start by cleaning your harvest the same day. For vegetables like tomatoes, use a soft brush or cloth to remove soil. For root crops like carrots, wash in clean water with a little salt (1 tablespoon per 10 litres) to kill germs, then rinse. Never soak produce in water for longit causes rot. Remove leaves, stems, and any damaged parts. Sorting means separating produce by size, ripeness, and appearance. For example, grade tomatoes into three groups: large, medium, and small. Large ones fetch 20-30% more in urban markets. Use simple tools: a plastic basin, clean water, a soft brush, and sorting trays or mats. Work in the shade to avoid sun damage. After cleaning, let produce dry in a well-ventilated area. For leafy greens like kale, remove wilted leaves and tie into bundles of 5-10 stems. Sorting also removes diseased items that can spoil the whole batch. A farmer in Kenya who sorted and cleaned her mangoes increased her price from KES 20 to KES 35 per fruit. Do this consistently, and buyers will seek you out. Remember: clean produce looks professional and reduces waste. Aim to sort within 4 hours of harvest for best freshness.

2. Grading by size, colour, and quality

Grading is sorting produce into uniform categories based on size, colour, and quality. Buyers like uniformity because it simplifies pricing and packaging. For size, use simple tools: a measuring tape or a wooden template with holes of different diameters. For example, grade avocados by diameter: small (5-6 cm), medium (6-7 cm), large (7-8 cm). For colour, use a colour chart or your eye. Tomatoes: green (unripe), pink (half-ripe), red (ripe). Bananas: green, yellow, spotted. Quality grades: Grade A (no blemishes, uniform shape), Grade B (minor defects), Grade C (major defects, for processing). In many African markets, Grade A fetches 50% more than Grade C. For maize, grade by kernel size and moisture content (use a moisture meter if available; otherwise, bite testif it cracks, it's dry enough). For eggs, grade by weight: small (under 45g), medium (45-55g), large (55-65g), extra-large (over 65g). Always grade in a clean, shaded area. Use a grading table or mat. Label each grade clearly. A Ghanaian farmer who graded her pineapples by size and colour doubled her export sales. Start simple: use three grades. Be consistentbuyers trust farmers who deliver the same grade every time. Train your workers on the same standards. Grading adds value with little cost, just time and attention.

3. Packaging in sacks, crates, or baskets

Packaging protects produce and makes handling easier. Choose the right container for your crop. For grains like maize, beans, and rice, use clean jute or polypropylene sacks. A standard 50 kg sack is common. Ensure sacks are dry and free of holes. For vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and leafy greens, use plastic crates (20-25 kg capacity) or wooden crates. Crates allow airflow and prevent crushing. Avoid overfillingleave 2-3 cm at the top. For fruits like mangoes and oranges, use ventilated plastic crates or cardboard boxes with holes. For fragile produce like eggs, use egg trays or cartons. Baskets (woven from local materials) work for small quantities and local markets. Always line containers with clean paper or leaves to reduce bruising. Never use dirty containersthey spread disease. For transport, stack crates no more than 4 high to avoid crushing. Label each container with your farm name, produce type, grade, and weight. For sacks, use a permanent marker on a tag. A Nigerian farmer who switched from sacks to crates for tomatoes reduced losses from 30% to 10%. Packaging costs money, but it pays back through higher prices and less waste. Reuse crates and sacks when possible. Clean and dry them between uses. Proper packaging shows buyers you are serious.

4. Reducing post-harvest losses during transport

Post-harvest losses in Africa can reach 40% for fruits and vegetables. Most losses happen during transport. To reduce them, harvest at the right timeearly morning or late evening when it's cool. Never transport in the heat of the day. Use covered vehicles or tarpaulins to protect from sun and rain. But don't seal completelyleave vents for airflow. Pack produce tightly to prevent shifting, but not so tight that it crushes. Use cushioning materials like dry grass or paper between layers. For long distances, use refrigerated trucks if available, but they are expensive. A cheaper option: use evaporative cooling. Wrap produce in wet sacks or place in a ventilated box with wet sand. For example, a farmer in Senegal used wet jute sacks over vegetables and reduced losses by half. Avoid mixing different produceethylene from ripe fruits speeds ripening of others. Keep fruits and vegetables separate. Drive carefully on rough roads; speed causes bruising. Plan your route to avoid delays. If you sell at a market, arrive early to secure a shaded spot. For short trips, use a bicycle or motorcycle with a padded box. Always handle crates gentlydon't drop them. After transport, unpack immediately and store in a cool place. A Kenyan tomato farmer who used crates and early morning transport cut losses from 35% to 12%. Simple steps save money.

5. Labeling and branding your produce simply

Labeling and branding help buyers recognize and trust your produce. You don't need expensive printing. Start with a simple label: your farm name, location, produce type, grade, and weight. Use a permanent marker on a tag or sticker. For example, 'Green Valley Farm, Nakuru, Tomatoes Grade A, 10 kg'. If you sell in bulk, put a label on each sack or crate. For packaged units (like 1 kg bags of beans), create a simple sticker with your name and contact. Branding means creating a consistent identity. Choose a name and logo. A logo can be a simple drawinga sun, a leaf, a tractor. Use the same colours on all labels. This helps customers remember you. In local markets, a recognizable brand can get you 10-20% higher prices. For example, a Ghanaian pepper farmer used a red label with a chili logo and became known for quality. He now sells out before other vendors. You can also use social media (WhatsApp, Facebook) to show your brand. Take photos of your well-packaged produce. Tell buyers your storyhow you grow, harvest, and pack. Honest labeling builds trust. Never lie about grade or weightit ruins your reputation. Keep labels simple and clear. Use local language if needed. A label is your handshake with the buyer.

6. Meeting buyer quality requirements

Buyers have specific quality standards. Before you plant, ask your buyer what they want. For supermarkets, they may require: uniform size, specific colour, no blemishes, pesticide residue limits, and proper packaging. For exporters, standards are stricter: GlobalG.A.P. certification, traceability, and cold chain. For local hotels and restaurants, they may want fresh, clean produce delivered daily. To meet these, start with good seed and proper growing practices. Harvest at the right maturity. Clean, grade, and pack carefully. Keep records: planting date, inputs used, harvest date. This helps with traceability. For pesticide use, follow pre-harvest intervalsdon't spray close to harvest. Use only approved chemicals. If you can't meet a standard, don't promise it. It's better to sell to a buyer with lower standards than to fail a contract. For example, a Kenyan farmer lost a supermarket contract because his tomatoes had spots. He now grades strictly and checks each fruit. To find buyer requirements, visit the buyer, ask questions, and read contracts carefully. Ask for a sample of what they expect. Attend training from extension officers or NGOs. Many buyers provide feedbackuse it to improve. Consistent quality leads to repeat orders and better prices. Start with one buyer, meet their needs, then expand. Quality is a habit, not a one-time effort.

<h2>Practical Reference: Preparing Your Produce for Market</h2>
<p><strong>Module context:</strong> Farm Market Access &amp; Pricing Preparing Your Produce for Market. This practical reference is written for smallholder and emerging commercial farmers in Africa who sell into formal and semi-formal value chains: local traders, urban fresh markets, hotels, schools, processors, and export aggregators. The core message is simple: <strong>post-harvest handling is not a cost centre it is a price lever.</strong> Every hour, every bruise, and every mixed-size bag quietly discounts your crop before the buyer even sees it.</p>

<h3>Why preparation is a business decision, not a chore</h3>
<p>In most African markets, buyers apply an informal but consistent "quality discount." Mixed sizes, soil, stones, damaged units, and poor packaging translate into lower farmgate prices, rejected deliveries, or delayed payment. Conversely, cleaned, graded, and well-packaged produce can earn 1040% more per kilogram and open doors to institutional buyers who will not accept unsorted consignments. The six lessons in this module cleaning and sorting, grading, packaging, reducing transport losses, simple labelling, and meeting buyer specifications are the operational steps that convert raw harvest into a sellable, priced product.</p>

<h3>Cleaning and sorting produce for better prices</h3>
<p>Cleaning removes field heat, soil, stones, insects, and damaged units that accelerate spoilage. Sorting separates the sellable from the unsellable before it contaminates the rest of the consignment. For roots and tubers (cassava, sweet potato, Irish potato), dry brushing or gentle washing followed by shade drying is standard. For leafy vegetables (kale, amaranth, Ethiopian mustard), remove yellowed leaves and wash in clean water, then drain thoroughly wet leaves rot within hours in a sack. For fruits (tomato, mango, avocado, banana), wipe rather than soak, and never pile more than two layers deep before packing.</p>
<p>Sorting should be done at the field edge, not at the market. A simple three-pile system works: <em>Grade A</em> (premium, uniform, unblemished), <em>Grade B</em> (sound but slightly off-spec), and <em>reject/processing</em> (damaged, over-ripe, or undersized). The reject pile is not waste it is feedstock for local processors, animal feed, or home consumption, and it protects the price of your Grade A lot.</p>

<h3>Grading by size, colour, and quality</h3>
<p>Grading is the act of applying consistent, written or buyer-agreed standards so that every bag or crate from your farm looks the same. Buyers pay for predictability. Grade on three axes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Size:</strong> Use a simple sizing ring, wooden gauge, or calibrated crate. For tomato, mango, and avocado, size classes (e.g., small/medium/large or counts per box) are often specified by the buyer.</li>
<li><strong>Colour:</strong> Maturity stage drives shelf life and price. Green tomato travels further; red tomato sells faster locally. Banana and mango have defined colour stages (17 scales) used by exporters.</li>
<li><strong>Quality:</strong> Freedom from disease, insect damage, mechanical injury, foreign matter, and off-odours. Uniformity is the visible signal of quality.</li>
</ul>
<p>Write your grade standards down. Even a one-page sheet taped to the packing shed wall prevents arguments, reduces rework, and lets you train casual labourers quickly.</p>

<h3>Packaging in sacks, crates, or baskets</h3>
<p>Packaging does three jobs: protects the produce, communicates quality, and determines how easily the buyer can handle and resell it. The right choice depends on crop, distance, and buyer.</p>
<p><strong>Sacks (woven polypropylene, jute):</strong> Cheap and widely available, but poor for fresh perishables they crush, trap heat, and hide damage. Best for dry grains, pulses, and root crops sold to processors. Never overfill; a 50 kg sack that becomes a 70 kg sack bursts and bruises.</p>
<p><strong>Crates (plastic or wooden):</strong> The gold standard for fresh produce stackable, ventilated, reusable, and they protect shape. Plastic crates reduce bruising dramatically versus sacks for tomato, mango, and leafy greens. Their higher upfront cost is recovered over multiple seasons, and many buyers now require them.</p>
<p><strong>Baskets (woven, local):</strong> Good for short-distance local sales and traditional markets. Line with clean paper or leaves to reduce abrasion. Avoid overloading; baskets deform and crush bottom layers.</p>
<p>Whatever you use, follow three rules: <em>clean</em> (no previous crop residue or chemicals), <em>ventilated</em> (air must move), and <em>right-sized</em> (a weight a buyer can lift and resell).</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fff5f5;padding:14px 16px;border-radius:6px;margin:18px 0">
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;color:#c0392b;font-weight:bold"> SAFETY WARNING Chemical and contamination risks in post-harvest handling</p>
<p style="margin:0">Never store or transport produce in containers previously used for pesticides, herbicides, fuel, or motor oil. Residues contaminate food and can cause acute poisoning or rejection at border inspection. Do not use untreated wastewater for washing produce destined for sale. Do not apply pesticides closer to harvest than the label's pre-harvest interval (PHI) this is a legal and market-access issue, not just a health one. Wear gloves and wash hands when handling treated crops. Buyers and export inspectors test for pesticide residues; a single failure can end a supply contract and, in some countries, trigger regulatory action.</p>
</div>

<h3>Reducing post-harvest losses during transport</h3>
<p>Transport is where the largest avoidable losses occur. In much of Africa, produce travels on poor roads, in open trucks, under high heat, with no ventilation and no cushioning. Practical, low-cost fixes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Harvest in the cool of the day</strong> (early morning or evening) and move produce to shade immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-cool or shade-wilt</strong> where possible. Even evaporative cooling (wet sacks over crates in a ventilated shed) extends shelf life for leafy greens.</li>
<li><strong>Do not mix crops or grades</strong> in one load ethylene-producing fruits (banana, tomato) accelerate ripening of neighbours.</li>
<li><strong>Load carefully:</strong> stack crates squarely, avoid crushing bottom layers, and secure the load so it does not shift on corners.</li>
<li><strong>Cover with a breathable tarpaulin</strong> not plastic sheeting that traps heat to protect from sun, dust, and rain.</li>
<li><strong>Travel at night or early morning</strong> where security allows, to avoid the midday heat.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the shortest route</strong> and, where volumes justify it, consolidate loads with neighbouring farmers to reduce cost per kilogram.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Labelling and branding your produce simply</h3>
<p>Labelling is the cheapest marketing you will ever do. A simple label tells the buyer who you are, what is inside, and when it was packed. It builds trust, enables repeat orders, and makes you traceable increasingly a requirement for supermarkets and processors.</p>
<p>Minimum viable label: <strong>farm or business name, crop and grade, net weight, date of packing, and a phone number.</strong> Use a permanent marker on a card, a printed sticker, or a stencil on the crate. Keep it honest: if you label Grade A, the contents must be Grade A. Consistency in weight and grade is what converts a one-off sale into a supply relationship.</p>

<h3>Meeting buyer quality requirements</h3>
<p>Different buyers have different specifications. A hotel wants uniform, clean, ready-to-use produce in small crates. A processor wants a specific variety, maturity, and often a maximum defect tolerance. An exporter wants documented grades, traceability, and compliance with the destination market's standards (e.g., EU maximum residue limits, GlobalG.A.P. where required).</p>
<p>Before you plant for a buyer, ask for their written specification: variety, size, colour, packaging, labelling, delivery schedule, and payment terms. If they do not have one, propose one this positions you as a professional supplier, not a commodity seller. Then build your cleaning, grading, and packing routine around that specification.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Harvest cool and clean.</strong> Pick in the early morning or evening; keep produce in shade from the moment it leaves the plant.</li>
<li><strong>Sort at the field edge.</strong> Separate Grade A, Grade B, and reject before anything enters a bag or crate.</li>
<li><strong>Grade to a written standard.</strong> Size, colour, and quality consistent from bag to bag and week to week.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the right package.</strong> Crates for fresh perishables; clean sacks for dry and durable crops; never reuse chemical containers.</li>
<li><strong>Do not overfill.</strong> A bag or crate should be liftable and stackable without crushing the bottom layer.</li>
<li><strong>Protect the load in transit.</strong> Shade, breathable cover, secure stacking, and travel in the coolest hours.</li>
<li><strong>Label every lot.</strong> Farm name, crop, grade, net weight, pack date, and phone number.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm the buyer's spec in writing</strong> before you plant, pack, or deliver and keep a copy at the packing shed.</li>
</ol>
