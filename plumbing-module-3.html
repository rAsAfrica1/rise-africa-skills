1. Water Supply Systems in African Buildings

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the water supply systems you will encounter and install in African buildings, from single-storey homes in Kumasi to multi-storey apartments in Nairobi and Lagos. The goal is practical: by the end you should be able to size a system, choose materials, and explain the cost and reliability trade-offs to a client. Start with the source. In most African cities you have three possible sources: the municipal mains, a borehole with a submersible pump, and stored rainwater. Mains pressure is often unreliable, so the standard design is a break tank or storage tank plus a booster pump. Never design a system that depends on continuous mains pressure. Always include storage. A typical family of six needs about 150 to 200 litres per person per day for drinking, cooking, washing and sanitation. That is 900 to 1200 litres per day. Size your storage tank for at least one full day, preferably two. A 2000 litre tank is a common starting point for a small home. For a guesthouse with 20 rooms, plan 4000 to 6000 litres. Calculate demand first, then size the tank, then size the pump. The pump must refill the tank within a reasonable time, usually 2 to 4 hours, and must also provide pressure to the building. A common mistake is to buy a pump that is too small and then wonder why showers dribble when two taps are open. Use this simple rule: for a two-storey building with up to 10 outlets, a 0.75 kW to 1.1 kW booster pump with a 24 litre pressure vessel is usually adequate. For three or four storeys, go to 1.5 kW or 2.2 kW and consider a variable speed drive. Variable speed pumps cost more but save electricity and give steady pressure. Now materials. In Africa you will mostly use PPR, PVC, and sometimes copper or PEX. PPR is the workhorse for hot and cold water inside buildings. It handles temperatures up to 70 degrees Celsius, is cheap, and is easy to weld with a heat fusion tool. PVC is for cold water only and for drainage. Do not use PVC for hot water; it will soften and fail. Copper is excellent but expensive and often stolen for scrap, so it is less common in exposed areas. PEX is growing in popularity because it is flexible and resists scale, but you must use the correct fittings and protect it from sunlight. For buried mains from the tank to the building, use HDPE or heavy-duty PVC. Always bury pipes below 600 mm to avoid damage from vehicles and digging. In sandy or rocky soils, bed the pipe in sand and use a warning tape. For joints, use compression fittings on HDPE and solvent cement on PVC. Solvent cement must be applied to both surfaces and twisted a quarter turn as you push. Let it cure for at least 24 hours before pressure testing. Pressure testing is not optional. Fill the system, bleed the air, and pressurise to 1.5 times the working pressure for one hour. A typical domestic system works at 2 to 3 bar, so test at 4.5 bar. Watch for drops. If pressure falls, find the leak before you bury or plaster. In many African markets, labour is affordable but rework is expensive because it damages finishes. Test first. Water quality matters. Borehole water can be hard, high in iron, or contaminated. Always recommend a basic treatment train: sediment filter, then carbon filter, then a UV or chlorine dose if the water is for drinking. For hard water, a softener protects geysers and washing machines. Explain to the client that a 500 dollar treatment unit can save a 2000 dollar geyser. That is a business-first argument. Finally, think about power. In areas with load shedding, a booster pump without a tank is useless. A tank plus a small generator or solar pump keeps water flowing. Solar pumping is now very competitive in Africa. A 1 kW solar array can pump 5000 litres a day from a shallow borehole. For a rural clinic or school, this is often the best option. Your job is not just to install pipes. Your job is to design a system that works when the grid fails, when the mains pressure drops, and when the family grows. Document everything: pipe sizes, pump model, tank size, and test results. Give the client a simple drawing. That drawing is your marketing. Next lesson we cover drainage and sanitation, which is where most plumbing failures actually happen. Before you move on, complete this exercise: measure a real building, count the outlets, calculate daily demand, and write a one-page specification with a bill of quantities. Bring it to the next session.

2. Drainage and Sanitation Systems

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 3. Drainage is the part of plumbing that people only notice when it smells or backs up. In Africa, drainage failures cause more complaints than supply failures. This lesson gives you the practical rules for soil, waste, and vent systems, plus septic tanks and soakaways. Start with the golden rule: water must flow downhill, and air must flow freely. Every fixture that discharges into the drainage system needs a trap. The trap holds a water seal of 50 to 75 mm. That seal stops sewer gas from entering the building. If the seal is lost, you get smell. Seals are lost by siphonage, back pressure, or evaporation. That is why we install vents. A vent pipe runs from the drainage stack up through the roof. It equalises pressure so the trap seal stays intact. In a typical African home, use a 110 mm PVC stack for soil and a 50 mm pipe for waste from sinks and showers. The stack should rise at least 300 mm above the roof and be fitted with a cowl or mesh to keep out birds and rodents. Never terminate a vent inside the roof space. That is a common and dangerous mistake. Slope is critical. For 110 mm pipe, use a fall of 1 in 40 to 1 in 60. For 50 mm pipe, use 1 in 20 to 1 in 30. Too little slope and solids settle. Too much slope and water runs away from solids, leaving them behind. Use a spirit level and a string line. Cut pipes squarely and deburr them. Use solvent cement properly. Support horizontal pipes every 1 metre and vertical stacks every 2 metres. Now fixtures. A water closet needs a 110 mm connection. A wash basin needs 40 mm. A shower needs 50 mm. A kitchen sink needs 50 mm and a grease trap if it is a restaurant. A washing machine needs 50 mm and a standpipe. A floor drain needs 50 mm and a trap. In public buildings, add a floor drain in every wet room. In many African markets, cheap flexible traps are sold. Avoid them. They block easily and do not hold a proper seal. Use solid PVC traps with a cleaning eye. Cleaning eyes are essential. Put one at every change of direction and at the base of every stack. If you cannot rod the drain, you cannot maintain it. Now septic tanks. A septic tank is a settling and digestion chamber. It must be watertight, have two or three chambers, and have a T-shaped inlet and outlet. Size it by the number of users. A rule of thumb is 1 cubic metre of tank volume for every 5 users, with a minimum of 3 cubic metres. For a family of 10, use a 3 to 4 cubic metre tank. The tank must be at least 1.5 metres from the building and 15 metres from any well or borehole. If the water table is high, use a raised tank or a sealed biodigester. Empty the tank when sludge reaches one third of the depth. In many African cities, private emptiers charge by the trip. Advise the client to budget for emptying every 2 to 5 years. Soakaways handle the liquid effluent. A soakaway is a pit filled with stones or rubble, lined with geotextile, and covered with soil. Size it by the percolation rate of the soil. In sandy soil, a 1.5 metre cube can handle a small household. In clay soil, you need a much larger area or a raised mound. Test the soil with a simple percolation test: dig a hole, fill it with water, and see how fast it drains. If it drains slower than 25 mm per hour, do not use a soakaway. Use a mound system or connect to a sewer if available. In dense urban areas, connect to the public sewer. But check the sewer level and the connection point. Many African sewers are shallow and can back up during rain. Install a backflow valve on the sewer line. That valve is cheap insurance. Also, never connect storm water to the foul sewer. That is illegal in most cities and causes overflow. Keep storm water separate. For high-water-table areas, use a biodigester or a raised septic tank. Biodigesters are popular in East Africa. They are compact, sealed, and produce less sludge. They cost more but save space. For a school or clinic, a biodigester plus a soakaway or a small treatment plant is often the best solution. Maintenance is the key to sanitation. Give the client a simple maintenance schedule: inspect traps monthly, rod the drain every six months, empty the septic tank every 2 to 5 years, and check the soakaway for ponding after rain. Write this schedule on a laminated card and fix it near the tank. That card is a service contract waiting to happen. Finally, think about business. Drainage work is often quoted as a lump sum. Break it down: pipes, fittings, labour, excavation, tank, soakaway, testing. Show the client the cost of a proper vent versus the cost of a smelly house. That comparison wins jobs. Next lesson we cover hot water systems and solar water heating, which is a growing market across Africa.

3. Hot Water Systems and Solar Heating

Welcome to Lesson 3. Hot water is a comfort product, but in Africa it is also a business opportunity. Electricity is expensive and often unreliable, so solar water heating and heat pumps are growing fast. This lesson covers the main systems, sizing, installation, and safety. Start with demand. A person uses 20 to 50 litres of hot water per day for bathing and washing. A family of six needs 120 to 300 litres. Size the geyser or solar tank for one day of demand. A 150 litre electric geyser is standard for a small family. A 200 to 300 litre tank suits a larger family or a guesthouse. For a hotel, calculate per room: 50 to 80 litres per room per day. Now the systems. The most common in Africa are: electric resistance geysers, solar water heaters with electric backup, heat pumps, and instantaneous gas heaters. Electric geysers are cheap to buy but expensive to run. A 150 litre geyser with a 3 kW element uses about 3 to 5 units of electricity per day. In a country with high tariffs, that is a significant monthly cost. Solar water heaters cost more upfront but cut running costs by 60 to 80 percent. There are two main types: flat plate and evacuated tube. Flat plate collectors are cheaper and work well in sunny, warm areas. Evacuated tubes are more efficient in cooler or cloudy conditions and in highland areas. For most of Africa, flat plate is a good choice. A 200 litre solar system with a 2 square metre collector is a common domestic size. Heat pumps work like a reverse air conditioner. They use electricity but move heat instead of making it. A heat pump can cut water heating costs by 50 to 70 percent compared to a resistance geyser. They work best in warm climates and need good ventilation. Instantaneous gas heaters are popular where gas is available. They heat water only when you open the tap, so there is no standing loss. But they need a flue and a gas certificate. Never install a gas heater in a bathroom without a proper flue. Carbon monoxide kills. Safety first. Every electric geyser must have a thermostat and a thermal cutout. The temperature should be set to 60 degrees Celsius to kill bacteria, but mix it down to 50 degrees at the tap to prevent scalding. Install a pressure control valve and an expansion vessel on a closed system. Without them, pressure builds up and the geyser bursts. That is a common and expensive failure. Also install a vacuum breaker and a drip tray with a drain. The drip tray must discharge to a safe place, not onto the floor. For solar systems, the tank must be above the collector for a thermosiphon system, or you need a pump for a forced circulation system. Thermosiphon is simpler and cheaper, but the tank must be on the roof. That adds structural load. Check the roof before you install. A 200 litre tank full of water weighs 250 kg. Spread the load over at least two rafters. For frost or cold highland areas, use a closed loop with antifreeze. For hard water areas, use a heat exchanger or a softener. Scale kills solar collectors and geysers. Now installation practice. Mount the collector facing the equator: north in the southern hemisphere, south in the northern hemisphere. Tilt the collector at an angle equal to your latitude plus 10 degrees for year-round performance. In Nairobi, latitude is about 1 degree south, so tilt 10 to 15 degrees. In Johannesburg, latitude is 26 degrees south, so tilt 30 to 35 degrees. Use stainless steel or brass fittings on solar systems. Galvanised steel corrodes. Insulate all hot pipes with closed-cell insulation. In Africa, UV destroys cheap insulation. Use UV-resistant lagging or paint it. Support pipes every 1.5 metres. Install isolation valves so you can service the collector without draining the whole system. Install a tempering valve at the outlet to mix hot and cold water to a safe temperature. That valve protects children from scalding. For a guesthouse, consider a central solar system with a circulation loop. That gives instant hot water at every tap and reduces water waste. But it costs more and needs a pump. For a small home, a close-coupled thermosiphon system is the best value. Maintenance is simple but essential. Flush the collector every six months. Check the anode in the geyser every year. Replace it if it is more than half consumed. Check the pressure valve every year. Clean the collector glass. For solar systems, check the pump and the controller. Keep a spare element and thermostat on hand. Now the business case. A solar water heater costs 800 to 2000 dollars in most African markets. An electric geyser costs 150 to 400 dollars. The solar system pays back in 2 to 4 years at typical tariffs. Show the client the monthly saving. That is the sale. Also offer a maintenance contract. A yearly service visit is cheap for you and valuable for the client. Finally, remember that hot water is a health product. In clinics and hospitals, you need reliable hot water for sterilisation and hand washing. Size those systems with redundancy: two geysers or a solar system with a large electric backup. Next lesson we cover pumps, tanks, and pressure systems in more detail, including boreholes and solar pumping.

4. Pumps, Tanks and Pressure Systems

Welcome to Lesson 4. Pumps move water, but in Africa they also move money. A well-chosen pump lasts 10 years. A poorly chosen pump fails in 6 months and destroys your reputation. This lesson covers pump types, sizing, tanks, and controls. Start with the source. A borehole is a hole in the ground with a casing and a screen. The water level is the static level. When you pump, the level drops to the dynamic level. The difference is the drawdown. You must know the dynamic level to size the pump. If you do not, you will install a pump that runs dry and burns out. Always measure the water level and the yield. A simple test: pump at a known rate and measure how fast the level drops. If the level drops faster than the pump can recover, the borehole is weak. In that case, use a smaller pump or a storage tank with a float switch. Now pump types. The main types are: submersible, centrifugal, jet, and solar. Submersible pumps go inside the borehole. They are efficient and quiet. They come in 0.5 kW to 7.5 kW sizes for domestic and small commercial use. Centrifugal pumps sit above ground and are used for shallow wells and tanks. Jet pumps are for shallow wells and can self-prime. Solar pumps use DC motors and solar panels. They are ideal for rural areas with no grid. For a domestic borehole, a 0.75 kW submersible pump can lift water 40 metres and deliver 2000 litres per hour. That is enough for a family. For a farm or a small estate, use a 2.2 kW or 3.7 kW pump. For a large hotel, use a multistage pump or a booster set. Sizing is simple if you follow the steps. First, calculate the total head. Total head equals the vertical lift plus the friction loss plus the pressure required. Vertical lift is the distance from the water level to the highest outlet. Friction loss depends on pipe size and flow. Use a friction chart or a simple rule: add 10 percent for every 100 metres of pipe. Pressure required is usually 1 to 2 bar, which is 10 to 20 metres of head. Add them up. That is your total head. Then choose a pump that delivers your required flow at that head. Do not choose a pump that is too big. A big pump cycles on and off, which wears out the motor and the pressure switch. Use a pressure tank to reduce cycling. A 24 litre pressure tank is standard for a small system. For a larger system, use a 100 to 300 litre tank. The tank should be pre-charged with air to 0.2 bar below the cut-in pressure. For example, if the cut-in is 2 bar, charge the tank to 1.8 bar. Now tanks. Storage tanks come in plastic, steel, and concrete. Plastic is the most common in Africa. It is cheap, light, and does not rust. But it must be protected from sunlight, because UV makes it brittle. Paint it or shade it. Steel tanks are strong but rust. Concrete tanks are durable but expensive and must be sealed. For drinking water, use food-grade plastic or lined steel. Never use a tank that held chemicals. Size the tank for at least one day of demand. For a house, 1000 to 2000 litres. For a school, 10,000 litres. For a clinic, 5000 litres plus a reserve. Install the tank on a strong base. A full 2000 litre tank weighs 2 tonnes. Use a concrete slab or a steel stand. Do not put it on a wooden platform. Now controls. A float switch turns the pump on and off based on the tank level. A pressure switch turns the pump on and off based on pressure. A dry-run protection device stops the pump if there is no water. That device is essential for boreholes. Without it, the pump burns out in minutes. A timer can also help: run the pump during off-peak hours if tariffs are lower. For solar pumps, use a controller that tracks the maximum power point. That gives up to 30 percent more water. Now installation. Use a torque arrestor on submersible pumps to stop the pump from twisting. Use a safety rope to pull the pump if it fails. Use heat-shrink connectors on the cable. Do not use tape alone. Tape fails in water. Use a check valve on the pump outlet. Use a gate valve and a pressure gauge. Label every valve and every pipe. That labelling saves time on every service call. Finally, think about maintenance. A pump service every 12 to 24 months extends life. Check the pressure tank charge every 6 months. Check the float switch every 3 months. Clean the tank every year. For solar pumps, clean the panels every month. Dust reduces output by 10 to 20 percent. In many African markets, dust and birds are a real problem. Now the business. Pumps and tanks are high-value items. Offer a package: pump, tank, controls, installation, and a one-year service contract. That package is easier to sell than a pump alone. Also offer a solar pumping package for rural clients. It is a growing market and often funded by NGOs and government programmes. Learn to write a simple proposal with a diagram, a bill of quantities, and a payback calculation. That proposal wins contracts. Next lesson we cover plumbing business skills: costing, quoting, customer care, and managing a team.

5. Plumbing Business Skills and Project Management

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson of Module 3. You now know the technical side. This lesson turns that knowledge into money. We cover costing, quoting, customer care, team management, and the habits that grow a plumbing business in Africa. Start with costing. Every job has direct costs and indirect costs. Direct costs are materials, labour, transport, and equipment hire. Indirect costs are your office, your phone, your vehicle, your tools, and your time. Many plumbers forget indirect costs and then wonder why they are busy but broke. Add 15 to 25 percent overhead to every job. Then add profit. A healthy profit margin is 20 to 30 percent on small jobs and 10 to 20 percent on large contracts. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on reliability, cleanliness, and a written warranty. Now quoting. A quote must be clear. It must state the scope, the materials, the timeline, the price, the payment terms, and the warranty. Use a simple template. List each item with a quantity and a unit price. Show the total. State what is not included. For example, if you are not breaking concrete, say so. That avoids disputes. For large jobs, use a bill of quantities. That is a detailed list of every pipe, fitting, valve, and fixture. It shows the client you are professional. It also protects you if the client changes the design. Always get a written acceptance before you start. A simple signature or a WhatsApp message is enough in many African markets. But write it down. Now customer care. In Africa, word of mouth is the strongest marketing. One happy client can bring five more. One unhappy client can destroy your name. So manage expectations. Arrive on time. Call if you are late. Keep the site clean. Cover floors and furniture. Use dust sheets. Remove all rubbish. Test everything with the client present. Show them how to use the system. Give them a simple maintenance card. Follow up after one week. That follow-up call is rare and it builds loyalty. Now pricing for service calls. Many plumbers undercharge for call-outs. Charge a call-out fee that covers your travel and the first 30 minutes. Then charge by the hour or by the job. For emergency work at night or on weekends, charge a premium. That premium is fair and clients understand it. Now team management. As your business grows, you will hire helpers and apprentices. Train them properly. Teach them safety, cleanliness, and customer care. Pay them on time. Give them a bonus for good work. Use a simple checklist for every job: tools, materials, safety, test, clean, handover. That checklist reduces mistakes. For larger projects, use a daily log. Record the work done, the materials used, and any problems. That log is your evidence if there is a dispute. Now project management. A plumbing project has five stages: design, procurement, installation, testing, and handover. In the design stage, measure and draw. In procurement, order materials early. In Africa, materials can take weeks to arrive. Do not start work without the critical items. In installation, follow the drawings. In testing, pressure test and flow test. In handover, give the client a folder with drawings, warranties, and maintenance instructions. That folder is your professional signature. Now risk management. Common risks are non-payment, theft, damage, and injury. Reduce non-payment by taking a deposit of 30 to 50 percent. Take progress payments. Do not hand over the system until the final payment is made. Reduce theft by locking tools and materials. Reduce damage by using protection boards. Reduce injury by using gloves, goggles, and safe work practices. Insure your business if you can. In many African countries, small business insurance is affordable and worth it. Now growth. The best growth is repeat business and referrals. Ask every happy client for a referral. Offer a small discount for referrals. Build a simple website or a Facebook page. Show photos of your work. Use WhatsApp Business to send quotes and reminders. Keep a customer list. Send a message every six months to remind them about maintenance. That message brings in service work. Finally, think about specialisation. You can specialise in solar water heating, borehole pumps, or commercial kitchens. Specialists earn more and compete less. Choose a niche that matches your market. In a city with many hotels, specialise in commercial hot water and drainage. In a rural area, specialise in solar pumping and rainwater harvesting. Now your action plan. Write a one-page business plan. List your services, your prices, your target customers, and your marketing plan. Set a monthly revenue target. Track your jobs and your costs. Review every month. That discipline separates a handyman from a plumbing business. You have completed Module 3. You now have the technical and business skills to design, install, and maintain water supply, drainage, hot water, and pumping systems in African buildings. Go out and do the work. Measure twice, cut once, test everything, and treat every client like your best client. That is how you build a reputation that lasts.

<strong style="color:#b00020;font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING WATER QUALITY AND CHEMICALS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;font-size:13px;color:#222;">Never assume a clear, odourless supply is safe. Untreated borehole and tank water can carry <em>E. coli</em>, <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>Vibrio cholerae</em> and other pathogens that cause diarrhoeal disease a leading killer of children under five in Africa. Before commissioning any system:</p>
<ul style="font-size:13px;color:#222;margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li>Test for <strong>faecal coliforms / E. coli</strong> and, where relevant, <strong>nitrate, arsenic and fluoride</strong> (common in East African groundwater).</li>
<li>Chlorine dosing must be measured over-chlorination causes burns and disinfection by-products; under-chlorination leaves pathogens. Target 0.20.5 mg/L free residual at the tap (WHO).</li>
<li>Store chlorine and acids in labelled, locked containers away from food, feed and children. Wear gloves and eye protection.</li>
<li>Never cross-connect a treated supply with an untreated or greywater line. Use backflow prevention on all hose bibs.</li>
<li>If a client reports illness, stop work, advise them to seek medical care, and arrange an accredited laboratory test before re-commissioning.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist On-Site Pipe Sizing and Commissioning</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial;font-size:13.5px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Confirm demand first.</strong> Count fixtures and occupants, then size the riser for simultaneous peak use (Table 1), not average use.</li>
<li><strong>Check static and dynamic pressure.</strong> A gravity tank must sit high enough to give 1 bar per 10 m of head; booster pumps need a pressure switch and expansion vessel.</li>
<li><strong>Size the tank with one day of autonomy</strong> plus fire or irrigation reserve (Table 2). Undersized tanks mean nightly pump noise and client complaints.</li>
<li><strong>Slope all drainage at 1:60 to 1:40</strong> (2025 mm per metre) for 100 mm pipe; flatter runs block, steeper runs leave solids behind.</li>
<li><strong>Fit isolation valves</strong> on every branch and a strainer before every pump. This turns a two-hour repair into a ten-minute job and protects your warranty.</li>
<li><strong>Test water quality before handover.</strong> Record free chlorine residual, pH and bacteriological result; give the client a signed commissioning sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Label and document.</strong> Mark pipe runs, valve positions and tank capacity; hand over a simple schematic. This reduces callbacks and justifies your fee.</li>
<li><strong>Price the risk.</strong> Include testing, flushing and a 12-month workmanship warranty in your quote clients pay more for a plumber who documents compliance.</li>
</ol>
