1. How a Toilet Roll Rewinding Machine Works

A toilet roll rewinding machine takes a large jumbo roll of tissue paper (typically 2.5 to 3 meters wide) and converts it into smaller, finished rolls. The machine has several key parts: a jumbo roll stand, a web tension control system, a perforation unit, a rewinding mandrel, and a log saw. First, you load the jumbo roll onto the stand. The tissue paper is threaded through a series of rollers that maintain tension. Too much tension tears the paper; too little causes wrinkles. Next, the paper passes through the perforation unit, which has a rotating blade that cuts small slits at regular intervalsusually every 12 to 15 centimeters. This allows you to tear off sheets easily. Then the paper is wound onto a cardboard core (the inner tube) on the rewinding mandrel. The machine counts the number of sheets per roll (common counts: 200, 300, 500) and stops when the log reaches the desired diameter. The finished log is ejected, and a new core is inserted automatically or manually. Finally, the log moves to a log saw, which cuts it into individual rolls of standard widthtypically 10 to 12 centimeters. In small African workshops, manual or semi-automatic rewinders are common. They cost less but require an operator to feed cores and monitor tension. A basic manual rewinder can produce 100 to 200 rolls per hour, while a semi-automatic one can do 500 to 800. The key is to keep the jumbo roll centered, adjust the perforation blade sharpness, and maintain consistent winding speed. With practice, you can produce quality rolls that compete with imported brands.

2. Setting Up a Small-Scale Converting Line in Your Workshop

To start a small-scale tissue converting line, you need a space of at least 6 by 8 meters with a smooth concrete floor, good lighting, and ventilation. Your main equipment: a jumbo roll stand (can be locally welded), a rewinding machine (manual or semi-automatic), a log saw (circular saw with a jig), a core cutter, and a sealing machine for wrapping. You also need a supply of jumbo rolls (from a paper mill), cardboard cores (2.5 cm inner diameter), and plastic wrap. Arrange the line in a U-shape: jumbo roll at one end, rewinder in the middle, log saw and wrapping table at the other end. This reduces walking and increases flow. Power requirements: a semi-automatic rewinder needs 1.5 to 2.2 kW, a log saw 1.1 kW. If you have unstable electricity, invest in a 5 kVA generator or inverter. Water is not needed except for cleaning. For safety, install guards on all rotating parts and provide dust maskstissue dust is a respiratory irritant. Start with a manual rewinder (cost: $800$1,500) and upgrade as you grow. You can build a simple log saw using a circular saw mounted on a table with a sliding carriage. For cores, buy pre-cut or cut your own from long tubes using a core cutter. A good layout allows two workers to produce 300500 rolls per day. Keep raw materials drymoisture causes jumbo rolls to stick and tear. Label your finished products with your brand, roll count, and ply. Many successful African converters start in a garage or small room and expand gradually.

3. Adjusting Tension and Perforation for Quality Rolls

Tension and perforation are the two most critical adjustments for quality toilet rolls. Tension controls how tightly the paper is wound. If tension is too high, the roll becomes hard, the paper stretches and may tear during unwinding, and the perforations break prematurely. If tension is too low, the roll is loose, feels cheap, and may telescope (the inner layers slip out). To set tension, start with the jumbo roll brake or dancer roller. On a manual rewinder, you adjust a friction brake on the jumbo roll stand. Turn the knob until the paper feels taut but still flexibleyou should be able to press a finger into the roll and feel slight give. A simple test: pull a sheet; it should resist but not snap. For perforation, the blade must be sharp and set at the correct angle. Dull blades cause torn perforationsragged edges that make the roll hard to tear. Set the perforation length to 1215 cm (about 56 inches). You can adjust the perforation unit by rotating the blade holder or changing gears. The blade should cut through about 80% of the paper thickness, leaving a small uncut portion to hold the log together during winding. Test by tearing a sheet: it should separate cleanly with a straight edge. If it tears diagonally or leaves fuzz, adjust blade depth or replace the blade. Also check perforation alignmentthe cut should be perpendicular to the paper edge. In humid conditions, paper absorbs moisture and becomes weaker, so reduce tension slightly. In dry conditions, increase tension a bit. Regularly clean the perforation blade with a soft brush to remove paper dust. With practice, you'll learn to feel the right tension and hear the right perforation sounda crisp 'snick' means good cutting.

4. Cutting Logs into Rolls: Manual and Semi-Automatic Methods

After rewinding, you have a long log of tissueoften 1.5 to 2 meters long. You must cut it into individual rolls of 1012 cm width. Manual cutting uses a hand saw or a miter box. For small production, a simple jig: a wooden V-block that holds the log, and a fine-tooth hand saw (24 teeth per inch). Mark the log with a measuring tape and pencil every 10 cm. Cut with smooth, even strokesdon't force the saw, or you'll crush the roll. This method yields 5080 rolls per hour but is tiring and less accurate. Semi-automatic cutting uses a circular saw mounted on a table with a sliding carriage. You clamp the log in a holder, then push the carriage through the saw blade. A stop block sets the cut length. This is faster (200400 rolls per hour) and more consistent. Safety: wear gloves and safety glasses; keep hands away from the blade. For either method, use a sharp blade designed for papera dull blade tears the tissue and creates dust. After cutting, inspect each roll: edges should be clean, not fuzzy. If edges are torn, your blade is dull or the log was wound too loosely. You can also use a bandsaw for smoother cuts, but it's more expensive. In many African workshops, a converted woodworking circular saw works well. Set the blade height to just above the log diameter. Feed the log slowly to avoid burning. After cutting, tap the rolls gently to align the layers. Store cut rolls in a dry placehumidity makes them swell and jam in wrapping machines. For higher output, consider a fully automatic log saw, but it costs $5,000+. Start manual, then upgrade to semi-automatic when you reach 500 rolls per day.

5. Wrapping and Sealing Toilet Rolls by Hand or Machine

Wrapping protects the rolls from dust, moisture, and handling damage. It also carries your brand. For hand wrapping, you need a roll of plastic film (typically 2030 microns thick, either clear or printed), a heat sealer or a simple candle or hot wire, and a roll of tape. Cut a piece of film about 30 cm by 20 cm. Place the roll in the center, fold the film around it, and tuck the ends into the core. Seal the seam with a heat sealer or a hot wirepress for 12 seconds. For a more professional look, use a shrink wrap gun: wrap the roll loosely, then apply heat; the film shrinks tight. Hand wrapping produces 100200 rolls per hour. Machine wrapping uses a flow wrapper or a shrink tunnel. A semi-automatic wrapping machine (cost: $1,500$3,000) feeds rolls on a conveyor, wraps them in film, and seals with a heated bar. Output: 8001,200 rolls per hour. For sealing, ensure the temperature is correcttoo hot burns the film, too cold leaves weak seals. Test by pulling the seam; it should not open. Common mistakes: loose wraps (film not tight enough) and torn film (from sharp edges on the roll). To avoid loose wraps, stretch the film slightly as you wrap. For machine wrapping, adjust the film tension roller. Also, make sure the roll is centered. Use printed film to show your brand, roll count, and ply. In Africa, many small producers use clear film and a simple label sticker. That's finejust ensure the label is waterproof. Store wrapped rolls in a cool, dry place away from sunlight, which can degrade the plastic.

6. Quality Checks: Avoiding Loose Wraps and Torn Perforations

Quality control is what separates a professional operation from a hobby. Two common defects are loose wraps and torn perforations. Loose wraps: the plastic film is not tight, allowing the roll to shift and look sloppy. Causes: insufficient film tension, wrong film thickness (too thin), or sealing temperature too low. Fix: increase film tension on the machine or stretch by hand; use 2530 micron film; raise sealing temperature by 510C. Test: hold the wrapped roll and squeeze gentlythe film should not slide. Torn perforations: the perforation line is ragged or breaks when you tear a sheet. Causes: dull perforation blade, wrong blade depth, or too much tension. Fix: replace or sharpen the blade; adjust depth to cut 80% of paper; reduce tension. Test: tear a sheet; it should separate cleanly with a straight line. Other checks: roll diameter (should be consistent, e.g., 1012 cm), sheet count (verify by counting sheets on a sample roll), and core alignment (core should be centered). Also check for 'telescoping'inner layers sticking outwhich means tension was too low. And 'starring'wrinkles radiating from the corecaused by too much tension. Do these checks every 30 minutes: pull a random roll from the line, unwrap it, and inspect. Keep a log of defects and adjustments. Train your workers to spot problems early. In hot, humid African climates, paper absorbs moisture, so adjust tension and perforation daily. A simple checklist: 1) Tear test, 2) Squeeze test, 3) Measure diameter, 4) Count sheets. If you find defects, stop and fix before continuing. Consistent quality builds customer trust and repeat sales.

Practical Reference: Basic Tissue Converting From Jumbo Roll to Finished Product

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA module Basic Tissue Converting: From Jumbo Roll to Finished Product. It is written for entrepreneurs setting up or improving a small-scale tissue converting workshop in Africa. The focus is on practical decisions that affect output, quality, cost per roll, and customer satisfaction.

Toilet paper converting is a straightforward manufacturing activity: a large jumbo reel of tissue paper is unwound, embossed or calendered, perforated, rewound into logs, cut into individual rolls, and packaged. The business case depends on reliable raw material supply, consistent roll quality, low waste, and efficient labour. In many African markets, imported jumbo reels are available through ports such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Durban, Tema, and Lagos, while local tissue mills are expanding in Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt, South Africa, and Morocco. Your converting line can create value by serving regional brands, hotels, schools, clinics, and private-label contracts.

1. How a Toilet Roll Rewinding Machine Works

A toilet roll rewinding machine, often called a log saw rewinder or perforating rewinder, performs four core functions:

  1. Unwinding: The jumbo roll is mounted on a shaft or stand. The web is pulled through a tension control system.
  2. Embossing or calendering: Steel or rubber rollers emboss the sheet for softness, bulk, and ply bonding. Some lines use a calender for smoother paper.
  3. Perforating: A perforation roller with a fixed blade or serrated edge creates the tear line between sheets.
  4. Rewinding: The paper is wound onto a cardboard core at high speed until the log reaches the required diameter. The log is then transferred to the log saw.

In small-scale workshops, semi-automatic rewinders are common. They may require manual core loading, manual tail sealing, and manual log removal. Fully automatic lines integrate core loading, tail sealing, and log transfer, but they cost significantly more and require stable electricity and compressed air.

2. Setting Up a Small-Scale Converting Line in Your Workshop

Before buying equipment, define your target market. A workshop producing 1,000 to 3,000 rolls per day can be viable with a semi-automatic line. Key setup requirements:

  • Space: Minimum 60100 m for raw material storage, converting, cutting, packing, and finished goods.
  • Power: Three-phase power is preferred. A 515 kW motor is typical for small rewinders. Install a voltage stabiliser and backup generator if grid supply is unstable.
  • Air: Many semi-automatic machines require compressed air at 68 bar. A small compressor with dryer is essential.
  • Raw material: Jumbo reels are typically 2.02.8 m wide, 1.01.5 m diameter, and 300800 kg. Store on pallets in a dry, clean area away from direct sunlight.
  • Labour: 36 operators for a semi-automatic line, depending on automation level.
  • Core supply: Cardboard cores must match your finished roll inner diameter, commonly 3850 mm.

Business tip: negotiate jumbo reel pricing in USD or local currency with clear Incoterms. A 10% reduction in raw material cost often improves net margin more than a 10% increase in production speed.

3. Adjusting Tension and Perforation for Quality Rolls

Tension control is the most common source of quality problems. Too much tension causes web breaks, stretched paper, and tight rolls that are hard to tear. Too little tension causes loose logs, wrinkled paper, and poor perforation registration.

Perforation quality depends on blade condition, roller pressure, and paper grade. Dull perforation blades create torn sheets and excessive dust. In humid African coastal markets, paper absorbs moisture and becomes weaker; adjust tension and perforation pressure accordingly.

4. Cutting Logs into Rolls: Manual and Semi-Automatic Methods

After rewinding, logs are cut to finished roll width. Common widths are 10 cm for standard toilet rolls and 2023 cm for kitchen towels. Manual log saws use a circular blade and a manual clamp. Semi-automatic log saws feed logs automatically and cut at high speed.

Blade sharpness is critical. A dull blade compresses the roll edge, causing deformed rolls and paper dust. Replace or sharpen blades according to manufacturer recommendations, typically every 820 hours of operation depending on paper grade and speed.

5. Wrapping and Sealing Toilet Rolls by Hand or Machine

Packaging protects the product and communicates brand value. Hand wrapping uses pre-printed polypropylene or polyethylene film, a heat sealer, and manual folding. Machine wrapping uses a flow packer or shrink wrapper. For small-scale operations, hand wrapping with a impulse sealer is the lowest-cost entry point. As volume grows, a semi-automatic flow wrapper improves speed and consistency.

Key packaging decisions:

  • Film thickness: 2540 microns for individual rolls; 4060 microns for multi-packs.
  • Print: Flexographic printing on film gives a professional look. Minimum order quantities can be high; consider plain film with labels initially.
  • Sealing: Heat sealing temperature must match film type. Too hot burns the film; too cold gives weak seals.
  • Multi-packs: 4, 6, 10, or 12 rolls per pack. Multi-packs increase basket size and reduce handling.

6. Quality Checks: Avoiding Loose Wraps and Torn Perforations

Quality control should be simple, visual, and consistent. Train operators to check every 30 minutes and record results. Common defects and remedies:

  • Loose wrap: Increase sealing temperature, check film tension, or adjust wrapping speed.
  • Torn perforations: Reduce perforation blade pressure, replace dull blade, or reduce web tension.
  • Loose logs: Increase winding tension, check core diameter, or reduce winding speed.
  • Rolls not tearing cleanly: Inspect perforation blade alignment and paper moisture content.
  • Dirty or stained paper: Clean rollers, check for oil leaks, and improve workshop hygiene.
SAFETY WARNING

Rewinders and log saws have rotating and sharp components. Never operate with guards removed. Isolate power before clearing jams or changing blades. Wear cut-resistant gloves, safety glasses, and hearing protection. In workshops without emergency stop buttons, install a master isolator within reach of every operator. Ensure all staff are trained on lockout/tagout procedures before any maintenance.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify jumbo reel width, diameter, and paper grade match your machine specification.
  2. Check core diameter and condition before loading into the rewinder.
  3. Set unwind tension according to paper grade and ambient humidity.
  4. Inspect perforation blade sharpness and alignment at the start of each shift.
  5. Measure finished log diameter every 30 minutes and record.
  6. Check log saw blade sharpness and cut quality every 2 hours.
  7. Test seal strength on wrapped packs every hour using a simple peel test.
  8. Record waste percentage per shift and investigate any increase above 3%.
