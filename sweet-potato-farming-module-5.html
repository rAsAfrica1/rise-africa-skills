1. Determining Optimal Planting Time

Planting sweet potatoes at the right time is critical for a good harvest. In Africa, sweet potatoes can be planted in two main seasons: the rainy season and the dry season if you have irrigation. The best time is when the soil is moist and warm, usually at the start of the rains. In West Africa, plant from April to June; in East Africa, from March to May and again from September to November. In Southern Africa, plant from October to December. Avoid planting in the middle of the dry season unless you can water regularly.

Sweet potatoes need at least 3 to 4 months of warm weather to produce tubers. If you plant too late, the vines may not have enough time to form good-sized roots before the cold or dry season. Also, avoid planting when heavy rains are expected because waterlogged soil can rot the slips. A good rule: plant when the soil is moist but not soaked, and when the forecast shows regular rainfall for the next few weeks.

In areas with a short rainy season, choose early-maturing varieties like 'Kabode' or 'Naspot 1' that mature in 3 months. If you have irrigation, you can plant year-round, but the best time is still when temperatures are between 20C and 30C. Too cold (below 15C) and growth stops; too hot (above 35C) and the plant stresses.

Prepare your land before the rains start. Make ridges or flat beds two weeks before planting so the soil settles. Collect your vines from a healthy mother garden. Cut slips 2030 cm long, with at least 46 nodes. Plant them within 12 days of cutting for best results. If you must wait, keep them in a cool, shaded place and sprinkle water.

Many farmers in Africa plant sweet potatoes as a relay crop after maize or beans. That is fine, but ensure the previous crop is harvested at least 2 weeks before planting to reduce pests. Also, check the moon? No, that's not science. Use soil moisture and temperature as your guide. A simple test: squeeze a handful of soil; if it holds together but breaks easily, it's perfect for planting.

2. Spacing and Plant Density Recommendations

Spacing determines how many plants you get per hectare and how well they grow. For sweet potatoes, the recommended spacing is 30 cm between plants and 75100 cm between ridges. If you plant on flat land, use 30 cm within rows and 80100 cm between rows. This gives you about 33,000 to 44,000 plants per hectare. If you want bigger tubers, give more space; if you want more tubers but smaller, plant closer. But don't go closer than 20 cm because plants will compete for light, water, and nutrients.

In Africa, many farmers use 1 m x 0.3 m spacing on ridges. That means ridges are 1 meter apart and plants are 30 cm apart along the ridge. This is easy to remember and works well for most varieties. For vine production (to sell slips), you can plant closer: 15 cm x 60 cm, because you are harvesting vines, not roots.

Plant density also affects weed control. Closer spacing shades the ground faster, reducing weeds. But too close and you get more small roots. Aim for a balance. If you are growing for market and want uniform medium-sized tubers, stick to 30 cm x 80 cm. If you are growing for home consumption and want many tubers, 25 cm x 75 cm is fine.

When planting on ridges, make the ridges 3040 cm high and 6080 cm wide at the base. Plant slips on the sides of the ridge, not the top, so water doesn't sit on them. On flat land, plant on the flat and earth up later. Always plant with the slip at an angle, about 45 degrees, with 23 nodes buried and the rest above ground. This encourages root formation at nodes.

Use a string and stick to mark spacing. It saves time and ensures uniformity. A simple measuring stick 30 cm long can be used to space plants along the row. For row spacing, use a rope marked at 80 cm. After planting, press the soil around the slip to remove air pockets. Water immediately if no rain. Proper spacing is one of the cheapest ways to increase yielddon't guess, measure.

3. Ridge vs. Flat Planting Methods

Sweet potatoes can be planted on ridges or on flat land. Each method has advantages. Ridges are best in areas with heavy rainfall or poor drainage because they keep the roots from getting waterlogged, which causes rot. Ridges also make harvesting easieryou can dig along the ridge and pull out tubers without damaging them. In Africa, ridge planting is common in the highlands and wet regions. Make ridges 3040 cm high and 6080 cm wide, spaced 75100 cm apart. You can make ridges with a hoe or a tractor-drawn ridger. On slopes, ridges should follow the contour to reduce soil erosion.

Flat planting is simpler and cheaper. You just plant slips directly into the soil without making ridges. This works well in dry areas with sandy soils that drain quickly. Flat planting also conserves moisture better because the soil surface is not exposed as much. However, in heavy clay or waterlogged soils, flat planting can lead to root rot. Also, harvesting is harder because you have to dig around the plants. If you use flat planting, you can earth up the soil around the plants 46 weeks after planting to form small ridges. This combines the benefits of both methods.

Which should you choose? If your area gets more than 800 mm of rain per year, or if your soil is clay, use ridges. If you are in a dry area with sandy soil, flat planting is fine. In many parts of Africa, farmers use ridges because it also makes it easier to apply fertilizer and control weeds. But if you have limited labor, flat planting saves time. You can also plant on raised beds, which are like wide ridges, 20 cm high and 1 m wide. This is good for commercial production.

No matter the method, ensure the soil is loose and well-drained. Sweet potatoes need deep, friable soil for good root development. If you plant on ridges, orient them east-west so both sides get sunlight. If you plant on flat, mark your rows. After planting, keep the field weed-free for the first 6 weeks. Weeds compete for nutrients and can reduce yield by up to 50%. So choose the method that fits your soil, climate, and labor, and do it well.

4. Mechanical vs. Manual Planting

Planting sweet potatoes can be done by hand or with machines. In Africa, most smallholder farmers plant manually using a hoe or a dibber (a pointed stick). Manual planting is labor-intensive but allows you to carefully place each slip. You can plant 12 hectares per day with a team of 510 people. The steps: make a hole with a dibber or hoe, insert the slip at an angle, cover the roots with soil, and press firmly. Manual planting is best for small farms, uneven land, or when you want to intercropping. It also gives you a chance to inspect each slip for pests or disease.

Mechanical planting is used on large farms. A tractor-drawn transplanter can plant several hectares per day. It makes a furrow, drops the slips, and covers them with soil. But mechanical planters require uniform slips, well-prepared land, and a tractor. They are expensive and not common in most African countries. However, some commercial farms in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria use them. If you are a smallholder, you likely won't use a machine, but you can use simple tools like a hand-pushed planter or a modified hoe.

One intermediate option is to use a ridge maker and then plant manually on the ridges. This saves time on land preparation. Another is to use a jab planter, which is a hand tool that makes a hole and drops the slip in one motion. It costs about $20$50 and can speed up planting by 30%. But it works best with uniform slips and moist soil.

For manual planting, organize your labor. Have one person cut slips, another carry them, and others plant. Cut slips in the morning, plant in the afternoon or next morning. Keep slips shaded and moist. Plant them with 23 nodes underground. If using a hoe, make a hole at a 45-degree angle, place the slip, and cover. Don't plant too deepif you bury more than 4 nodes, the plant may rot. After planting, water if no rain. Mechanical planting can be faster, but manual planting gives better establishment if done carefully. For most African farmers, manual is the way to go, but invest in good tools and train your workers.

5. Transplanting Slips: Best Practices

Transplanting is moving sweet potato slips from the nursery to the main field. The best slips are 2030 cm long, with 46 nodes, and are cut from healthy, disease-free vines. Cut slips in the early morning or late afternoon when it's cool. Use a sharp knife or secateurs to make a clean cut. Remove the leaves from the lower 23 nodesthese nodes will be buried and will produce roots. Leave the top leaves on. If the sun is hot, you can trim the top leaves in half to reduce water loss.

Plant slips within 24 hours of cutting. If you must store them, keep them in a cool, shaded place, and sprinkle water on them. You can wrap them in banana leaves or a wet sack. Never leave them in the sun. Before planting, dip the cut ends in water or a weak fertilizer solution (1 tablespoon of NPK in 10 liters of water) to give them a start. Some farmers use a rooting hormone, but it's not necessary if the slips are fresh.

When planting, make a hole at a 45-degree angle. Place the slip so that 23 nodes are underground and the rest is above. Cover with soil and press firmly to remove air pockets. The soil should be moist. If it's dry, pour a little water into the hole before planting. Space plants 30 cm apart on ridges 80100 cm apart. After planting, water each plant if there is no rain. In the first week, check for wilting. If slips wilt, they may still recover; water them. If they turn brown and dry, replace them.

A common mistake is planting slips too deep. If you bury too many nodes, the plant may rot, especially in wet soil. Another mistake is planting in dry soil without watering. Sweet potato slips need moisture to root. If you plant in the dry season, irrigate before and after planting. Also, avoid planting on a hot, sunny daychoose a cloudy day or evening. After transplanting, keep the field weed-free. Weeds compete for water and nutrients. Within 2 weeks, you should see new growth. That means the slips have rooted. Then you can relax a bit, but keep watering if dry. With good transplanting, you can get over 90% survival.

6. Ensuring Proper Soil Moisture at Planting

Soil moisture is the most important factor for sweet potato slip survival. If the soil is too dry, slips will wither and die. If it's too wet, they may rot. The ideal soil moisture at planting is when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. A simple test: take a handful of soil and squeeze it. If it forms a ball that holds together but breaks when you poke it, it's perfect. If it drips water, it's too wet. If it crumbles, it's too dry.

Before planting, check the soil moisture at 1015 cm depth. If it's dry, irrigate the field 12 days before planting. You can use a watering can, hose, or drip irrigation. In Africa, many farmers rely on rain, so plant at the onset of rains when the soil is well-moistened. But if the rains are erratic, you may need to supplement. For small plots, you can fill a jerrycan with water and pour a cup into each planting hole. That's 200300 ml per plant. This ensures the slip has immediate moisture.

After planting, water again if there is no rain within 24 hours. In the first week, keep the soil moist but not soggy. If you have mulch, apply it around the plants to conserve moisture. Use dry grass, straw, or leaves. Mulch also suppresses weeds and keeps soil temperature stable. But don't pile mulch against the stemit can cause rot. If you plant on ridges, water along the furrows so water seeps into the ridge. On flat land, water gently so you don't wash away the soil.

If you are planting in the dry season with irrigation, water every 23 days for the first two weeks. Then reduce to once a week. Sweet potatoes are drought-tolerant once established, but during the first 34 weeks, they need consistent moisture. A lack of water at planting can reduce yield by 3050%. So don't skip this step. Also, avoid planting in waterlogged areas. If your field floods, make ridges or plant on raised beds. Good moisture management at planting sets the stage for a healthy crop. Remember, sweet potatoes are not ricethey don't like standing water.

<h2>Practical Reference: Planting Techniques and Crop Establishment for Sweet Potato in Africa</h2>

<p>Sweet potato (<em>Ipomoea batatas</em>) is one of Africa's most strategic food and cash crops. It matures in 90150 days, tolerates marginal soils, and delivers reliable yields where maize and cassava struggle. But yields on African smallholder farms typically range between 4 and 8 t/ha, while well-managed commercial plots reach 2035 t/ha. The gap is rarely about variety. It is almost always about <strong>planting technique and crop establishment</strong>.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into a single decision-support document for farmers, extension officers, and agribusiness managers. Treat it as a field manual: use the tables to plan, the diagram to visualise ridge geometry, the checklist to verify before you plant, and the safety box to protect your team.</p>

<h2>1. Determining Optimal Planting Time</h2>

<p>Sweet potato is planted at the onset of rains in rainfed systems, or year-round under irrigation. The critical rule: <strong>plant when soil moisture is adequate to a depth of 1520 cm</strong>. Planting into dry soil causes slip desiccation and up to 60% establishment failure.</p>

<p>In West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Benin), the main planting window is AprilJune with the first rains; a second window opens SeptemberOctober. In East Africa (Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania), the long rains (MarchMay) and short rains (OctoberNovember) define two planting seasons. In Southern Africa (Malawi, Zambia, Mozambique), NovemberDecember is optimal. Irrigated commercial production can plant any month provided soil temperature exceeds 15C.</p>

<h2>2. Spacing and Plant Density Recommendations</h2>

<p>Plant density drives yield. Too sparse and you waste land; too dense and you get small, unmarketable roots. The standard recommendation for African conditions is <strong>30,00040,000 plants per hectare</strong>, achieved with ridges spaced 75100 cm apart and slips spaced 2530 cm within the row.</p>

<p>For commercial table-root production targeting premium markets, use the higher density (40,000 plants/ha) with 75 cm ridge spacing and 30 cm in-row spacing. For fodder or dual-purpose production, reduce to 25,000 plants/ha.</p>

<p>Flat planting is used in very sandy soils, on steep slopes where ridges erode, or in low-input systems. Yields are typically 2040% lower than ridged planting because of poorer drainage and smaller rooting volume. For commercial production, always ridge.</p>

<h2>4. Mechanical vs. Manual Planting</h2>

<p>Manual planting dominates African sweet potato production. A farmer using a hand hoe can ridge and plant 0.20.4 ha/day. Mechanical ridging with a tractor-mounted ridger or two-wheel tractor can prepare 25 ha/day and plant 12 ha/day with a semi-automatic transplanter.</p>

<p>For agribusiness operations above 5 ha, mechanisation pays back within one to two seasons through labour savings and more uniform spacing. For smallholders, improved manual tools the <strong>Chaka hoe</strong> (Kenya) and the <strong>mandam</strong> ridger increase output by 3050% over traditional hoes.</p>

<h2>5. Transplanting Slips: Best Practices</h2>

<p>Slip quality determines establishment success. Use vine cuttings 2530 cm long with 57 nodes, taken from healthy mother plants 812 weeks old. Cut slips in the morning, keep them shaded, and plant within 2448 hours. For commercial operations, treat slips with a fungicide dip (e.g., benomyl at label rate) to prevent <em>Fusarium</em> and <em>Macrophomina</em> infections.</p>

<p>Plant slips at a 45 angle with 23 nodes below the soil surface and the growing tip above. Firm the soil around the slip to eliminate air pockets. Water immediately a minimum of 250 ml per plant.</p>

<h2>6. Ensuring Proper Soil Moisture at Planting</h2>

<p>Sweet potato slips are highly sensitive to moisture stress during the first 710 days. Soil moisture should be at field capacity at planting. In rainfed systems, plant within 23 days of a soaking rain (20 mm). In irrigated systems, pre-irrigate to 1520 cm depth, then plant and irrigate again lightly.</p>
