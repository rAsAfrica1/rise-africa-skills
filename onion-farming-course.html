Onion Farming

A complete 6-month program — day-length varieties that actually bulb, nursery to lifting, the weed and thrips war, and the curing and storage discipline that lets you sell on the price curve instead of into the glut.

⏱️ 6 Months (crop + storage)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? An onion crop takes 4–5 months from nursery to lifting, and then the REAL business begins: curing and storing bulbs while the market price climbs. Six months walks you from seed to a stored, graded crop sitting in your store while everyone else's has already been sold cheap. Onions are the classic African price-curve crop — the farmer who can store is the farmer who profits.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the nursery, the field and the curing shed.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Day-length decides everything. Onions bulb according to hours of daylight — plant a long-day (European/temperate) variety in tropical Africa and you will harvest leaves and nothing else. Buy SHORT-DAY or intermediate varieties suited to your latitude, confirmed with your seed dealer. This single mistake has ruined more onion crops than any pest.
  • Storage rot starts in the field. Bulbs bruised at lifting, cured badly, or stored with thick necks will rot in the store and take their neighbours with them. Months 5–6 are the money.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check: onion seed is expensive and quickly loses viability — buy fresh, dated seed from 3-quote-checked registered dealers only.
MONTH 1 — Onion Economics, Varieties & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why onions are the storage crop

  • Every household, restaurant and street vendor uses onions daily — demand never stops, and unlike tomatoes the crop does not perish in days.
  • The price curve: onions are cheapest at the harvest glut and typically 2–4× higher in the months before the next crop. Properly cured and stored onions turn time into money.
  • Yields: neglected crops give 10–15 t/ha; well-managed irrigated crops give 30–50 t/ha. Bulb size and uniformity — both management outcomes — decide your price per kg on top of that.

Lesson 1.2 — Day-length: the make-or-break lesson

  • Onion plants only start forming bulbs when daylight passes a variety-specific threshold. Categories: short-day (bulbing at about 10–12 hours — correct for most tropical/subtropical Africa), intermediate (12–14 h), long-day (14–16 h — temperate Europe/North America).
  • Plant a long-day variety near the equator and the threshold is never reached: you grow beautiful leaves, no bulbs, and lose the season.
  • Always confirm with your dealer/extension officer: "Is this variety short-day and recommended for our area?" Common short-day types in Africa include Texas Grano-family, Red Creole, Bombay Red and locally bred selections — availability varies by country, so ask locally rather than ordering blind.

Lesson 1.3 — Colour, pungency and the market

TypeTraitsMarket
Red onionsPungent, colourful, generally good storageDominant across much of Africa; households and vendors
Yellow/brownFirm, strong storage, uniformSupermarkets, processors, export packs
WhiteMild, softer, shorter storageNiche/fresh use, restaurants
  • Storage rule of thumb: the more pungent and firm the bulb, the longer it keeps. Mild sweet onions are for eating soon, not for the price curve.

Lesson 1.4 — Site, soil and rotation

  • Onions want deep, loose, well-drained loam or sandy loam with high organic matter; pH 6.0–7.0. Heavy clay produces small misshapen bulbs and rots them in wet spells.
  • Shallow roots (mostly in the top 20–25 cm) mean onions are poor competitors for water and nutrients and are HELPLESS against weeds — a fact that dictates Month 4.
  • Rotate 3+ years away from onions, garlic, leeks (allium family) to escape purple blotch, downy mildew, white rot and nematodes. White rot in particular contaminates soil for a decade or more.
  • Full sun, no shade, and a reliable irrigation source for 4–5 months.

MONTH 2 — Nursery & Land Preparation ▾

Lesson 2.1 — The onion nursery

  • Seed rate: 3–5 kg/ha of seed raised in nursery beds produces the 400,000+ seedlings a hectare needs. For 500 m², that's roughly 150–250 g.
  • Nursery beds 1 m wide, raised 15 cm, with fine tilth and well-rotted compost; sow in shallow drills 10–15 cm apart, thinly, and cover lightly with fine soil then a thin grass mulch until emergence (remove it as seedlings appear).
  • Water gently twice daily with a fine rose; shade lightly in the hottest weeks; onion seed germinates in 7–12 days and seedlings are slow and delicate for the first month.
  • Onion seed loses viability fast — never use seed more than a year old unless you have tested germination.

Lesson 2.2 — Nursery care to transplant

  • Keep the nursery meticulously weed-free — competing weeds at seedling stage cripple the crop before it starts.
  • Damping-off is the main nursery killer: thin sowing, no waterlogging, good airflow, and clean media prevent it.
  • Transplant at 6–8 weeks when seedlings are pencil-lead thick and 15–20 cm tall (about 3–4 leaves). Older, thicker seedlings are more likely to bolt (run to flower) later.
  • Harden off 5–7 days: reduce watering and remove shade so seedlings toughen before the field.

Lesson 2.3 — Preparing the field

  • Plough/dig deep and work to a FINE, level tilth — bulbs cannot swell properly in cloddy or compacted ground.
  • Incorporate 20–30 t/ha of well-rotted compost or manure (never fresh manure — it causes thick necks, forked bulbs and rots in storage).
  • Form raised beds 1–1.2 m wide where drainage is at all doubtful; onions hate wet feet and rot fast in standing water.
  • Basal fertiliser: a phosphorus-rich compound at roughly 300–500 kg/ha worked into the bed before transplanting.

Lesson 2.4 — Alternative planting methods

  • Direct seeding: cheaper on labour, needs precise sowing and excellent weed control; common on larger mechanised farms.
  • Sets (small bulbs): fastest to harvest and easy, but expensive per hectare and prone to bolting — useful for small plots and early market.
  • Transplanting (this course's track): best bulb uniformity and land-use efficiency for smallholders, since the field is only occupied for 3–3.5 months.

MONTH 3 — Transplanting, Spacing & Irrigation ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Transplanting technique

  • Lift seedlings after watering the nursery; trim excessively long roots slightly and (optionally) tip the leaves by a third to reduce wilting.
  • Plant SHALLOW — 2–3 cm deep only. Deep planting is a classic beginner error: it produces thick necks, small bulbs and poor storage.
  • Firm the soil around each seedling and irrigate immediately; transplant in the cool of late afternoon.
  • Gap-fill within 7–10 days from your spare seedlings.

Lesson 3.2 — Spacing and plant population

SystemSpacingPopulation
Standard rows30 cm rows × 10 cm in-row≈330,000/ha
Intensive beds4–5 rows per 1 m bed, 10 cm apart in row400,000–500,000/ha
Larger bulbs wantedWider: 12–15 cm in-row250,000–300,000/ha
  • The trade-off to understand: closer spacing = more total tonnage but smaller bulbs; wider spacing = fewer, larger bulbs. Let your MARKET decide — supermarkets and export want big uniform bulbs; local markets often prefer medium.

Lesson 3.3 — Irrigation: consistent, then decisive

  • Onions need steady moisture in the top 20–25 cm — shallow roots mean frequent lighter irrigation (every 3–5 days depending on soil and weather), not occasional floods.
  • Critical demand periods: establishment and the bulbing phase. Water stress during bulbing directly shrinks bulb size — this is where yield is won.
  • Avoid overhead irrigation late in the day; wet foliage overnight invites purple blotch and downy mildew. Drip or furrow is far better for onions.
  • STOP irrigation 2–3 weeks before harvest — this is a deliberate signal that hardens the bulb skins, tightens the necks and dramatically improves storage life. Onions irrigated up to lifting rot in the store.

Lesson 3.4 — Top dressing

  • Nitrogen top dressing in 2 splits: about 3–4 weeks after transplanting and again at 6–8 weeks (roughly 100–150 kg/ha of AN/urea equivalent in total, adjusted to soil fertility).
  • Stop all nitrogen once bulbing starts — late nitrogen produces soft, thick-necked bulbs that rot in storage and delays maturity.
  • Potassium and sulphur matter for onions: sulphur drives pungency and storage quality; a sulphur-containing fertiliser is a real advantage where soils are deficient.

(3) Apply top dressing split 1 and mark split 2 on the calendar with the "no N after bulbing" line drawn.

MONTH 4 — Weeds, Pests & Diseases ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The weed war (onions' greatest weakness)

  • Onions' thin upright leaves never shade the ground, so weeds grow unchecked and can cut yields by 50–80% — more than any pest or disease in this course.
  • Keep the crop weed-free especially for the first 8–10 weeks: shallow hand-weeding every 2–3 weeks, never deep (onion roots sit in the top 20 cm and are easily cut).
  • Weed labour is the biggest cost item in smallholder onion production — budget it honestly, or use mulch between rows and consider registered pre-emergence herbicides on larger areas with extension guidance.

Lesson 4.2 — Thrips: the main pest

  • Onion thrips are tiny slender insects hiding in the leaf axils; damage shows as silvery-white streaks and stippling, then distorted, drying leaves — and heavy infestations cut bulb size sharply.
  • They explode in hot dry weather. Scout weekly by pulling leaves apart and looking into the neck of 20 plants across the field.
  • Management: overhead sprinkling (which physically knocks thrips down) where disease risk allows, blue sticky traps, registered insecticides ROTATED between chemical groups (thrips develop resistance notoriously fast), and neem-based products in low-input systems. Spray with high pressure into the leaf axils, or the chemical never reaches them.

Lesson 4.4 — Bolting and doubling (quality problems)

  • Bolting (flower stalk shooting up): caused by over-large seedlings, cold spells after transplanting, or stress. Bolted bulbs have a woody core and will not store — remove and sell them fresh immediately.
  • Doubles/splits (two bulbs in one skin): caused by irregular watering and over-thick seedlings — even irrigation prevents most of it.
  • Both problems are decided at nursery and transplant stage, not at harvest — which is why Month 2's seedling spec matters.

MONTH 5 — Harvest & Curing: Where Storage Life Is Made ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Knowing when to lift

  • The signal: tops fall over (neck softens and leaves lodge) across the field. Harvest when about 50–80% of tops have fallen — not before, and not weeks after.
  • Don't artificially bend tops over early: it's an old habit that reduces yield and storage quality; let the crop tell you.
  • Lift in dry weather after the 2–3 week irrigation cut-off. Wet-lifted onions are storage failures waiting to happen.

Lesson 5.2 — Lifting technique

  • Loosen with a fork and pull by hand; NEVER throw bulbs into piles or drop them into containers. Every bruise is a rot entry point that shows up six weeks later in the store.
  • Leave bulbs in the field a day or two to dry if weather is dry and hot sun isn't scorching them — covering rows with the leaves of the next row protects bulbs from sunscald while they dry.
  • Discard immediately: thick-necked bulbs, bolters, doubles, damaged or diseased bulbs. These are the SELL-NOW pile; they must never enter the store.

Lesson 5.3 — Curing (the single most important post-harvest step)

  • Curing means drying the neck and outer skins until the neck is tight and papery — typically 2–3 weeks in shade with good airflow (or field-curing plus shed time in dry climates).
  • Methods: spread bulbs in a single layer on slatted racks or dry ground under a ventilated shelter; or tie tops and hang bunches; or windrow in the field where weather is reliably dry.
  • The test: a properly cured onion has a completely dry, tight neck that doesn't slide when twisted, papery outer skins, and a rustling sound when handled.
  • Then top and trim: cut tops leaving 2–3 cm of dry neck (never cut into the bulb), trim roots close, and gently brush off loose soil — no washing, ever.

Lesson 5.4 — Grading

  • Grade by size: small (under ~40 mm), medium (40–70 mm), large (70 mm+) — and separate any bulb with a soft neck, split skin or blemish.
  • Uniformity is what buyers pay for: a graded 20 kg net of even medium reds beats a mixed sack every time, in every market.
  • Record percentages by grade — this is your quality KPI and it improves measurably every season you apply this course.

MODULE 6 — Storage, Marketing, Economics & Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Storage: turning time into money

  • Ideal smallholder store: DRY, well-ventilated, shaded, off the ground. Onions store best cool and dry — in tropical conditions a raised slatted platform in a ventilated shed with cross-breeze is the practical standard.
  • Never store in sealed plastic sacks — use net bags, slatted crates, or traditional hanging plaits/tied bunches under a roof. Air movement is everything.
  • Stack shallow (no deep piles crushing lower bulbs) and inspect every 2 weeks: remove any softening bulb immediately — one rotting onion spoils the bag around it.
  • Realistic storage life: 2–5 months for well-cured pungent varieties in good ventilated conditions; less in humid climates. Sprouting and softening signal it's time to sell.

Lesson 6.2 — Marketing on the curve

  • Sell in tranches, not all at once: some at harvest (cash flow and reject clearing), some mid-curve, some at the peak. Trying to hit the exact top is gambling; laddering out is business.
  • Channels: farm-gate traders (lowest, fastest), municipal markets, supermarkets and processors (graded, contracted), restaurants/vendors (regular medium volumes), and lean-season retail by the bucket (highest per kg).
  • Keep a market notebook of weekly prices — after two seasons you'll know your local curve better than the traders quoting you.

  • Dried onion flakes and powder (see the rAs Solar Drying course) convert small, split and surplus bulbs into a shelf-stable, high-value product with no cold chain.
  • Peeled/prepped onions for restaurants and pickled onions are further options where hygiene standards can be met.

  • Assemble: variety choice with day-length justification, rotation history, nursery-to-harvest calendar, irrigation plan including the cut-off, weeding labour budget, thrips/disease programme, curing and storage design, grade percentages achieved, the price curve chart with your selling tranches, risk plan (bolting, thrips resistance, storage rot, price collapse), and a 3-season growth path.

📖 Glossary

  • Short-day / long-day — the daylight hours a variety needs to bulb.
  • Bulbing — the phase when the bulb swells; the water-critical period.
  • Bolting — premature flower stalk; ruins storage quality.
  • Doubles — two bulbs in one skin from irregular water.
  • Curing — drying necks and skins for storage.
  • Neck rot — Botrytis storage rot from poor curing.
  • White rot — incurable soil fungus; decade-long contamination.
  • Thrips — the main onion pest, hiding in leaf axils.
  • Topping — cutting tops to 2–3 cm of dry neck.
  • Price curve — the seasonal rise from harvest glut to lean season.
