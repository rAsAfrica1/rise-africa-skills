1. Starter diets for newly received calves

When calves arrive at your farm after transport, they are stressed, often dehydrated, and may have been off feed for hours. The first 24 hours are critical. Provide free-choice hay and fresh water with electrolytes. Do not offer grain immediately. After 12-24 hours, introduce a starter diet. A good starter for a 200 kg calf should contain 14-16% crude protein, 2-3% fat, and 15-20% fiber. Use locally available ingredients: maize meal (energy), soybean meal or cottonseed cake (protein), wheat bran (fiber), and molasses (palatability). A simple mix: 40% maize, 25% soybean meal, 20% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 5% mineral-vitamin premix. Mix well and offer small amounts (0.5 kg per calf) several times a day. Increase gradually over 7-10 days to 2-3 kg per calf per day. Always provide clean water. In Africa, drought-stressed calves may need extra protein; consider adding 1% urea to the concentrate if natural protein is scarce, but never feed urea to weak calves. Monitor calves for scours; if it occurs, reduce grain and add fiber. Weigh calves weekly to track growth. A well-managed starter phase can achieve 0.5-0.7 kg daily gain. Use a feed trough with dividers to prevent bullying. If calves are very young or light, consider a creep feed with 18% protein. Always consult a local extension officer for ingredient availability and price. Remember, starter diets are not just about nutrients; they are about getting calves eating and healthy. Clean feed troughs daily to prevent mold. This phase sets the foundation for the entire feeding period.

2. Growing and finishing diets for steers and heifers

Growing and finishing diets are designed to put on weight efficiently. Growing phase (200-350 kg) targets 0.7-1.0 kg daily gain with 12-14% protein. Finishing phase (350-500 kg) aims for 1.0-1.5 kg daily gain with 10-12% protein and high energy. In Africa, common energy sources are maize, sorghum, millet, and cassava. Protein sources include soybean meal, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and fishmeal. A typical finishing diet: 60% maize, 20% cottonseed cake, 10% wheat bran, 5% molasses, 5% mineral premix. This gives about 11% protein and 70% TDN. For heifers, keep protein at 12% to support pregnancy if breeding. Feed twice daily, and provide roughage like hay or silage at 10-15% of diet. Use a feed mixer or mix on a clean concrete floor. Weigh ingredients accurately; a simple scale is essential. Adjust diet based on body condition score (BCS). Aim for BCS 3.5-4 at finish. In dry seasons, add 1% urea to replace part of protein. Monitor manure; loose manure means too much grain, firm means good. Provide free-choice minerals and clean water. In feedlot systems, use growth implants if legal and available. For smallholders, group cattle by weight and feed accordingly. Record feed intake and weight gain to calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR). A good FCR is 6:1 to 8:1. If FCR is poor, check for parasites and adjust diet. Always ensure consistent feed quality. Buy ingredients in bulk to reduce cost. Consider using by-products like brewers grains or molasses. In Africa, climate affects feed intake; during hot months, feed early morning and evening. This phase is where profit is made, so attention to detail pays.

3. Diets for breeding bulls and cows

Breeding bulls and cows have different nutritional needs. Bulls need energy for mating and condition; cows need energy for pregnancy and lactation. A mature bull (600-800 kg) should get 10-12% protein and 60-65% TDN. A simple bull diet: 50% maize, 20% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 5% mineral premix. Feed 1-2% of body weight daily plus good quality hay. During breeding season, increase energy by adding 10% more maize. Avoid overfeeding, as fat bulls have poor fertility. For cows, nutrient needs depend on stage. Dry cows: 8-10% protein, 50-55% TDN. Pregnant cows: 10-12% protein, 55-60% TDN. Lactating cows: 12-14% protein, 60-65% TDN. A lactating cow diet: 45% maize, 20% cottonseed cake, 15% wheat bran, 10% molasses, 5% mineral premix, 5% limestone. Provide 2-3 kg concentrate per 5 kg milk. Always offer free-choice mineral blocks, especially with selenium and copper. In Africa, many cows graze natural pasture; supplement during dry season with crop residues like maize stover treated with urea. For breeding, flush cows 3 weeks before mating by increasing energy by 10-20%. This improves conception. Keep bulls and cows in good body condition (BCS 3-3.5). Overconditioning leads to calving problems. Test feed ingredients for aflatoxin, especially maize and cottonseed. Store feed in dry, ventilated area. Provide clean water at all times. For smallholder farmers, group cows by production stage and feed accordingly. Record breeding dates and calving intervals. A good breeding diet ensures healthy calves and high fertility. Consult a veterinarian for specific mineral deficiencies in your area.

4. Transition and adaptation diets

Switching cattle from one diet to another must be gradual to avoid digestive upsets like acidosis. Transition diets are used when moving from high-forage to high-grain, or when changing ingredients. The rule: take 7-14 days to transition. Start with 70% forage and 30% concentrate, then increase concentrate by 10% every 2 days until desired level. For example, if final diet is 60% grain, start with 20% grain and 80% hay. Mix thoroughly. Always provide long-stem hay during transition to stimulate rumination. Add buffers like sodium bicarbonate (0.5-1% of diet) to prevent acidosis. In Africa, where feed availability fluctuates, adaptation is common. When introducing new ingredients like molasses or urea, do it slowly. Urea should never exceed 1% of total diet and must be mixed well. For newly received calves, transition from hay to starter over 10 days. Monitor manure consistency; if loose, slow down. Use a feed bunk score: 1 (empty) to 4 (full). Aim for 2-3. If cattle leave feed, they may be sick. During transition, weigh cattle weekly to ensure they are gaining. A sudden drop in intake indicates problems. For feedlot cattle, use a step-up program: step 1 (55% forage), step 2 (45% forage), step 3 (35% forage), step 4 (25% forage), step 5 (final). Each step lasts 3 days. In smallholder systems, mix concentrate with chopped hay or silage. If you see bloating, give vegetable oil orally. Always have fresh water available. Transition is also a time to observe cattle for health issues. Work with your extension officer to design a transition schedule based on your available feeds. Remember, a smooth transition saves money and keeps cattle healthy.

5. Creep feeding and backgrounding diets

Creep feeding provides supplemental feed to nursing calves while they are still with their mothers. It improves weaning weights and prepares calves for feedlot. A creep feeder is a small enclosure with a gate that only calves can enter. In Africa, you can build one with local materials. The creep diet should be highly palatable and nutrient-dense: 16-18% protein, 70-75% TDN. A simple mix: 50% maize, 25% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 5% molasses, 5% mineral premix. Offer creep feed from 2 months of age until weaning. Calves will eat 0.5-1.5 kg per day. Ensure fresh feed and clean water. Creep feeding is especially useful in dry seasons when pasture is poor. Backgrounding is the period after weaning and before finishing, where cattle are fed to grow frame and muscle. Backgrounding diets are high in forage (60-70%) with moderate energy (55-60% TDN) and 12-14% protein. A typical backgrounding diet: 40% maize, 30% hay, 20% cottonseed cake, 5% molasses, 5% mineral premix. Feed 2-3% of body weight daily. The goal is 0.5-0.8 kg daily gain. Backgrounding can be done on pasture with supplement or in a drylot. In Africa, use crop residues like maize stover, sorghum stover, and legume haulms. Treat stover with urea (4 kg urea in 100 L water per 100 kg stover) to improve digestibility. Provide mineral blocks. Weigh cattle every 2 weeks to monitor gain. Backgrounding prepares cattle for finishing and reduces health issues. For smallholders, backgrounding can be a profitable enterprise. Keep records of feed intake and weight. Adjust diet based on body condition. Always ensure adequate water and shade. This phase is about steady growth, not fattening.

6. Diet formulation for drought or emergency feeding

Drought is a common challenge in Africa. When pastures dry up, cattle lose weight and may die. Emergency feeding aims to keep animals alive and maintain minimal production. The key is to use available low-quality roughage and supplement with energy and protein. Start with what you have: crop residues (maize stover, sorghum stover, millet straw), agro-industrial by-products (molasses, brewers grains, cottonseed cake), and unconventional feeds (cactus, acacia pods). For a 250 kg cow, a survival diet might be 70% roughage, 20% molasses, 10% protein supplement. Molasses is energy-rich but must be limited to 20% of diet to avoid toxicity. Mix molasses with urea (3% urea in molasses) to provide non-protein nitrogen. A simple emergency mix: 50% chopped stover, 30% molasses-urea mix, 15% cottonseed cake, 5% mineral premix. Feed 1-2% of body weight daily. Always provide clean water. In severe drought, consider culling unproductive animals. For breeding herds, prioritize pregnant and lactating cows. Use urea treatment of straw: mix 4 kg urea in 100 L water, sprinkle on 100 kg straw, cover and ferment for 2 weeks. This increases protein content. Another option is feeding cactus (Opuntia) which is high in water and energy; chop and mix with roughage. In emergencies, feed frequency may increase to 3 times daily. Monitor body condition; if BCS drops below 2, increase supplement. Work with local extension services for available feed resources. Avoid sudden diet changes. Keep records of feed inventory. Emergency feeding is expensive, so plan ahead by conserving hay and silage in good seasons. For smallholders, community feed banks can help. Remember, the goal is survival, not weight gain. Once rains return, gradually transition back to normal grazing.

Practical Reference: Formulating Diets for Different Production Stages


Feed is the single largest cost in any cattle enterprise â routinely 60â75% of total operating expenses in African beef and dairy operations. Getting the diet right at each production stage is not an academic exercise; it is the difference between a business that returns a profit per animal and one that bleeds money through poor growth, disease, and wasted feed. This practical reference gives you the numbers, the sources, and the decision rules you need to formulate diets for six production stages under African conditions.



Throughout, remember the core business principle: match nutrient supply to animal requirement at the lowest cost per unit of gain or milk. Overfeeding protein to a finishing steer wastes money. Underfeeding a newly received calf costs you mortality and treatment bills. Precision is profit.





Stage 1: Starter Diets for Newly Received Calves


Newly received calves â whether weaners bought at auction, transported from a different agro-ecological zone, or introduced to a feedlot â are the highest-risk group on any operation. They face stress, dehydration, and exposure to unfamiliar pathogens. The starter diet must maximize dry matter intake (DMI) while supplying enough protein and energy to support immune function.


Targets: 14â16% crude protein (CP), 65â70% TDN, 0.6â0.8% calcium, 0.4â0.5% phosphorus. Include 10â15% roughage (good-quality hay or silage) to stimulate rumen development and prevent acidosis. Use highly palatable ingredients: maize meal, molasses, cottonseed cake, and lucerne hay.


Business note: Starter diets are the most expensive per kg. Do not extend them longer than necessary. Calves should transition to grower diets within 14â21 days of arrival, once DMI exceeds 2% of body weight and calves are consistently eating from the trough.



Stage 2: Growing and Finishing Diets for Steers and Heifers


The growing phase (200â350 kg live weight) builds frame and muscle. The finishing phase (350â500 kg) deposits intramuscular and subcutaneous fat. In African feedlots, the economic target is usually 1.2â1.8 kg average daily gain (ADG) with a feed conversion ratio (FCR) below 7:1.


Growing: 12â14% CP, 70â75% TDN. Finishing: 10â12% CP, 75â82% TDN. Energy density drives gain; protein above requirement is simply excreted â an unnecessary cost.


Common African finishing rations use maize or sorghum as the energy base, cottonseed or sunflower cake as protein, and urea or poultry litter as non-protein nitrogen (NPN) where cost-effective. Stage 3: Diets for Breeding Bulls and Cows


Breeding animals are an investment, not a finished product. Their diets must maintain body condition score (BCS) of 2.5â3.5 on a 5-point scale, support gestation and lactation, and sustain libido and semen quality in bulls.


Dry cows: 8â10% CP, 50â55% TDN â maintenance only. Lactating cows: 11â13% CP, 60â65% TDN. Bulls (breeding season): 11â12% CP, 60â65% TDN, with adequate zinc, selenium, and vitamin E for fertility.


In African communal and smallholder systems, breeding cows are often the most underfed group because they are not "producing" visibly. This is a false economy: a thin cow rebreeds late or not at all, costing you an entire year's calf crop.



Stage 4: Transition and Adaptation Diets


Rumen microbes need 7â21 days to adapt to new feed. Sudden changes cause acidosis, bloat, and production loss. The transition protocol is simple but must be followed strictly:



  • Days 1â3: 70% roughage, 30% concentrate

  • Days 4â7: 50% roughage, 50% concentrate

  • Days 8â14: 30% roughage, 70% concentrate

  • Days 15+: target finishing ratio (often 10â20% roughage)


Always provide clean water and free-choice mineral. Add yeast culture (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) at 5â10 g/head/day to stabilize rumen pH during transition.



Stage 5: Creep Feeding and Backgrounding Diets


Creep feeding gives suckling calves access to a supplemental diet while still on the cow. It increases weaning weight by 15â30 kg and reduces weaning stress â a direct revenue gain at sale. Creep diet: 16â18% CP, 70â75% TDN, highly palatable (maize, soybean meal, molasses, good hay). Limit intake to 1â2 kg/calf/day.


Backgrounding follows weaning and precedes finishing. It grows frame and prepares the animal for high-concentrate feeding. Backgrounding diet: 12â14% CP, 60â68% TDN, with 40â60% roughage. In African conditions, backgrounding on crop residues (maize stover, groundnut haulms) plus a protein supplement is highly cost-effective.

Drought is a recurring reality across the Sahel, Horn of Africa, and southern Africa. When pasture fails, you must feed to keep breeding stock alive and productive â not to finish animals. The goal shifts from gain to maintenance and survival.


Emergency diet principles:



  • Use locally available by-products: maize stover, sorghum stover, sugarcane tops, molasses, poultry litter, urea-treated straw.

  • Urea treatment of straw (4 kg urea in 100 L water per 100 kg straw, sealed 3 weeks) raises CP from 3% to 8â10% and digestibility by 10â15 percentage points.

  • Feed 1â2 kg concentrate per head per day to breeding cows; prioritize pregnant and lactating animals.

  • Do not attempt to finish animals during drought â sell surplus stock early while they still have condition.

Urea is toxic if misused. Never feed urea to non-ruminants or to calves under 3 months. Never exceed 1% of total diet or 0.3 g/kg body weight per day. Always introduce urea gradually over 3â4 weeks. Never feed urea with high-urea forages or in water. Signs of urea toxicity include bloat, staggering, and death within 30â60 minutes. Keep a bottle of vinegar (acetic acid) on hand â 2â4 L orally can save an affected animal. If you are not experienced with NPN, use oilseed cakes instead. The cost saving is not worth the risk of losing your herd.




Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Know your stage: Confirm whether the animal is starter, growing, finishing, breeding, or emergency. Wrong stage = wrong diet = lost profit.

  2. Test your feed: Send representative samples of maize, hay, and by-products for CP and TDN analysis before formulating. Book values can be off by 20%.

  3. Balance energy first: Energy drives gain. Protein, minerals, and vitamins correct the balance â they do not replace energy.

  4. Transition slowly: 7â21 days, stepwise. Never change more than 20% of the diet per day.

  5. Water and minerals free-choice: A cow drinks 30â60 L/day. Water restriction kills intake and gain faster than any feed error.

  6. Watch body condition: Score monthly. BCS is your cheapest diagnostic tool â adjust feed before animals lose weight.

  7. Calculate cost per kg gain: Not cost per bag. A cheaper feed that produces less gain is more expensive.

  8. Keep records: Feed in, weight out, cost per animal. Without records you are guessing, and guessing is not agribusiness.
