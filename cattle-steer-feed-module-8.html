Lesson 8.1~13 min

Feed Efficiency and Performance Tracking

In this lesson
  • Run a monthly weigh day that produces usable average daily gain figures
  • Calculate feed conversion from feed delivered and weight gained over a defined period
  • Compare your own conversion against published benchmarks and act on the difference

You cannot manage what you do not weigh. Most cattle operations in Africa assess performance by eye, which detects a disaster and nothing smaller. A monthly weigh day converts a vague impression into two numbers you can act on: average daily gain, and kilograms of feed per kilogram of gain.

Start with the weighing itself. A crush scale is best. A weigh band, a tape measured around the heart girth, is the practical alternative and costs a few dollars; it is less accurate for any single animal but perfectly adequate for tracking the same animal over time, which is what you need. The rules that make weights comparable matter more than the instrument:

  1. Weigh on the same day of the month, every month.
  2. Weigh at the same time of day, preferably in the morning before feeding and watering, because gut fill can swing a reading by several percent.
  3. Weigh the same animals, identified by ear tag or brand, and record the identity next to the weight.
  4. Use the same operator and the same technique with a weigh band.
  5. Write the weight down at the crush, not afterwards.

Average daily gain is then simple subtraction. Worked example. A steer weighs 268 kg on 3 March and 301 kg on 2 April, 30 days later.

  1. Gain: 301 - 268 = 33 kg.
  2. Days: 30.
  3. Average daily gain: 33 / 30 = 1.10 kg per day, which is 2.4 lb per day.

Feed conversion needs the feed side, which is why the feed records from Module 7 Lesson 6 are the other half of this exercise. Take a group rather than an individual, because feed is delivered to groups.

Worked example. A pen of 12 steers over 30 days.

  1. Total feed delivered: 30 days x 12 head x 9.0 kg of dry matter = 3,240 kg.
  2. Group weight at start: 3,216 kg. Group weight at end: 3,612 kg.
  3. Total gain: 3,612 - 3,216 = 396 kg.
  4. Feed conversion: 3,240 / 396 = 8.2 kg of dry matter per kg of gain.
  5. Average daily gain per head: 396 / 12 / 30 = 1.10 kg.

Now benchmark it. Published figures put grain-finished feedlot cattle at a feed to gain ratio of 5.5 to 6.5 to 1 with daily gains of 3.0 to 4.0 lb, and forage-based systems at 8 to 1 to 12 to 1 with daily gains of 1.0 to 2.0 lb. Our pen at 8.2 to 1 and 2.4 lb per day is performing as a good forage-based system, not as a feedlot. If it is being fed and priced as a feedlot, something is wrong; if it is a forage system, it is doing well. The benchmark tells you which conversation to have.

Cost of gain follows in one line. If the ration costs 0.26 USD per kg of dry matter, the feed cost of gain is 8.2 x 0.26 = 2.13 USD per kg. Add non-feed costs per head per day, say 0.35 USD, which at 1.10 kg of gain per day is 0.32 USD per kg, giving a full cost of gain of 2.45 USD per kg. If finished cattle sell at 2.60 USD per kg live weight, every kilogram you add earns 0.15 USD. If they sell at 2.30, every kilogram you add loses money and the correct decision is to sell now. The system-level economics of that decision, including whether the feedlot model suits your situation at all, are worked through in Module 12 Lessons 1 and 2; the job here is to produce the numbers those decisions need.

Three practical cautions about the arithmetic. First, always work feed in dry matter, not as fed, or a change in silage moisture will look like a change in efficiency. Second, a single month is noisy; three consecutive months is a trend. Third, gut fill, a wet coat and a hot afternoon can each move a weight by more than the month's real gain, which is exactly why the weighing rules above are not fussiness.

What to do when conversion is poor. Work down this list in order, because it runs from cheapest to dearest.

  1. Check water. Restricted or dirty water suppresses intake more than any feed change.
  2. Check intake against capacity using DMI as a percentage of body weight = 120 / NDF%. If the forage is too fibrous, the animals physically cannot eat enough.
  3. Check the protein floor. Below 7.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter, fibre digestion and intake both fall.
  4. Check parasites and health. A wormy animal converts badly on a perfect ration.
  5. Check the mix, using the calibration and batch records.
  6. Only then change ingredients.

Concrete African context. A 45-head fattening unit near Dodoma, Tanzania, weighed cattle only on arrival and at sale. Introducing a monthly weigh band day, taking about ninety minutes for the whole group, revealed that gains collapsed in the third month of every batch, which traced back to a forage change that had never been recorded. The fix cost nothing; finding it required weighing.

Weigh monthly, record at the crush, work in dry matter, and compare against the benchmark rather than against last year's feeling.

Lesson 8.2~13 min

Cost Analysis and Profit Margins

In this lesson
  • Separate feed cost, non-feed cost and opening animal value in a margin calculation
  • Calculate margin per head and margin per head per day for a finished or milking animal
  • Identify which single cost line is actually driving your result before changing anything

Margin per head is the only figure that tells you whether the last six months were worth doing. It has four components and most producers can name only one of them. Building it properly takes twenty minutes with the records from Lesson 1 and Module 7 Lesson 6.

The four components are opening value, feed cost, non-feed cost and closing value. Opening value is what the animal was worth when the period started, whether you bought it or bred it; if you bred it, use what you could have sold it for on that day, because that is the money you chose not to take. Feed cost is total dry matter delivered multiplied by cost per kg of dry matter. Non-feed cost is everything else on a per head per day basis: labour, water, health, dipping, transport, mortality allowance and interest. Closing value is the sale price achieved or the current market value.

Worked example: a fattening animal.

  1. Opening value: a 250 kg steer bought at 2.20 USD per kg live weight = 550.00 USD.
  2. Feeding period: 120 days at 9.0 kg of dry matter per day = 1,080 kg.
  3. Feed cost at 0.26 USD per kg of dry matter = 280.80 USD.
  4. Non-feed cost at 0.35 USD per head per day x 120 = 42.00 USD.
  5. Total cost: 550.00 + 280.80 + 42.00 = 872.80 USD.
  6. Gain at 1.10 kg/day x 120 = 132 kg, so closing weight is 382 kg.
  7. Sale at 2.60 USD per kg = 993.20 USD.
  8. Margin per head: 993.20 - 872.80 = 120.40 USD.
  9. Margin per head per day: 120.40 / 120 = 1.00 USD.

The per-day figure is the one to keep, because it lets you compare a 120-day fattening animal against a 300-day one and against a dairy cow, all on the same basis.

Worked example: a milking cow, using income over feed cost.

  1. Yield: 12 litres per day at 0.40 USD per litre = 4.80 USD.
  2. Feed: 6.0 kg of silage dry matter at 0.09 USD per kg of dry matter = 0.54 USD, plus 2.5 kg of concentrate at 0.35 USD = 0.875 USD, plus 0.115 kg of mineral at 0.45 USD = 0.05 USD. Total feed 1.47 USD.
  3. Income over feed cost: 4.80 - 1.47 = 3.33 USD per cow per day.
  4. Non-feed cost at 0.60 USD per head per day gives a margin of 2.73 USD per cow per day.
  5. Across 300 days of lactation that is 819 USD per cow, before the cost of the dry period.

Now the sensitivity work, which is where the value is. Change one variable at a time and watch the margin.

  1. Fattening steer, purchase price rises from 2.20 to 2.45 USD per kg: opening value rises to 612.50 USD and the margin falls from 120.40 to 57.90 USD. A 0.25 USD move in the feeder price took more than half the profit.
  2. Fattening steer, feed conversion worsens so daily gain falls from 1.10 to 0.95 kg: closing weight becomes 364 kg, sale value 946.40 USD and margin 73.60 USD, with the same feed bill.
  3. Dairy cow, milk price falls from 0.40 to 0.34 USD per litre: income drops to 4.08 USD and income over feed cost to 2.61 USD, a 22 percent cut in margin from a 15 percent price move.
  4. Dairy cow, concentrate price rises from 0.35 to 0.50 USD per kg: feed cost rises to 1.84 USD and income over feed cost falls to 2.96 USD.

Two lessons come out of those four lines. First, the largest lever on a fattening enterprise is the price you pay for the animal, not the price you pay for feed, which is why disciplined buying beats clever formulation. Second, on a dairy enterprise a small change in milk price moves the margin more than a large change in feed price, because feed is only a share of income. Know which of those two businesses you are running before you decide where to spend management attention. The wider structural questions, such as whether to be in fattening at all and how systems compare over years, belong to Module 12.

Break-even is the last calculation and the one to keep on the wall. For the fattening steer: total cost of 872.80 USD divided by a closing weight of 382 kg gives a break-even sale price of 2.28 USD per kg. Any offer above that makes money, any offer below it does not, and you no longer have to negotiate on feeling. For the dairy cow, feed cost of 1.47 USD divided by 12 litres gives a feed cost of 0.1225 USD per litre; add non-feed cost of 0.60 USD over 12 litres, or 0.05 USD, for a break-even of about 0.17 USD per litre before overheads and the dry period.

Concrete African context. A fattening operation in Kaduna State, Nigeria, updates three figures on a chalkboard by the office door every week: feeder price per kg, ration cost per kg of dry matter, and finished price per kg. From those three the break-even is recalculated in a minute, and the buying decision follows automatically. In a season when the feeder price ran ahead of the finished price, they simply stopped buying for six weeks, which is a decision almost no operation without those three numbers can make in time.

Build the margin, run the sensitivities, post the break-even where the buying decision is made.

Lesson 8.3~13 min

Marketing Strategy for Cattle Products

In this lesson
  • Compare the baseline commodity channels available for live cattle and raw milk in your area
  • Sell on weight and specification rather than by eye, and know your break-even before negotiating
  • Time sales around the seasonal price pattern instead of around cash flow emergencies

Most African cattle producers sell into one of a small number of baseline commodity channels, and the choice between them is worth more per animal than most feeding decisions. This lesson is about those channels and the mechanics of selling well within them. Value-added processing and integration strategies are a separate question and are covered in Module 12 Lesson 5.

For live cattle the baseline channels are these.

  1. Farm-gate sale to a trader. Convenient, immediate cash, no transport cost, and normally the lowest price because the trader carries the risk and the margin.
  2. Livestock market or auction. Better price discovery, competition between buyers, but transport, market fees, shrink in transit and the risk of returning home unsold.
  3. Direct sale to a butchery or abattoir. Usually the best price for cattle that meet a specification, but requires consistent supply, transport and often deferred payment.
  4. Contract or scheme supply. A negotiated price for a defined animal delivered on a defined date, which reduces price risk but requires you to be able to hit the specification reliably.

The mechanic that changes the outcome in every one of them is weighing. An animal sold by eye is priced by the buyer's estimate, and the buyer's estimate is never generous. If you have a weigh band and a break-even price from Lesson 2, you enter the negotiation knowing two things the buyer does not know you know: what the animal weighs, and the price below which you will walk away.

Worked example. A steer weighs 382 kg on your weigh band and your break-even from Lesson 2 is 2.28 USD per kg.

  1. A trader offers 900 USD for the animal, "as it stands".
  2. That is 900 / 382 = 2.36 USD per kg. Above break-even, so it is a real offer.
  3. The market price you have recorded over the past month is 2.60 USD per kg, which would be 993 USD.
  4. The difference is 93 USD. Transport to market plus fees is 35 USD, and shrink in transit might cost another 8 kg of saleable weight, about 21 USD.
  5. Net advantage of going to market: 93 - 35 - 21 = 37 USD, before the risk of not selling.

Now you have a decision rather than an argument. Thirty-seven dollars may or may not be worth a day's work and the risk, but you know what you are choosing.

For milk the baseline channels are equally short.

  1. Direct sale to neighbours and local consumers. Highest price per litre, smallest volume, daily collection effort, and payment risk.
  2. Sale to a cooperative or collection centre. Lower price per litre, reliable offtake, often monthly payment, and usually the route to inputs on credit.
  3. Sale to a processor or trader collecting at the gate. Intermediate price, volume-dependent, and often a quality-based payment.

The critical question with milk is the payment basis, because it should drive your feeding. If the buyer pays a flat price per litre regardless of composition, you are being paid for volume and there is no return on producing richer milk; recall that NEL per kg of milk is 0.360 + (0.0969 x fat percentage), so 4.5 percent milk costs about 14 percent more energy per litre to produce than 3.5 percent milk. Under a flat price that extra energy is a pure cost. If the buyer pays on butterfat, the same milk is worth more and the feeding decision reverses. Ask your buyer, in writing, exactly how the price is calculated.

Quality standards are the other side of the milk contract. Aflatoxin transfers from feed to milk at 2.0 to 6.2 percent of intake, and the milk limit is 0.5 micrograms per kg against 20 micrograms per kg in feed, so a feed problem becomes a rejection problem quickly. Rejections are also caused by bacterial load from poor cooling and by antibiotic residues from treated cows whose withdrawal period was not observed. Each of those is a record-keeping failure more than a technical one; see Lesson 5.

Timing is the last lever. Cattle prices in most African markets follow a predictable seasonal shape, rising ahead of major festivals and falling when everyone destocks at the end of a hard dry season. Selling because you have run out of feed is selling at the bottom, which is why the seasonal feed budget in Module 6 Lesson 6 is also a marketing tool. If you know in October that you cannot feed twenty animals through to March, you can sell four in November at a good price instead of twenty in February at a poor one.

Concrete African context. Members of a livestock marketing group in Kajiado County, Kenya, began recording the actual price per kilogram achieved on every animal sold, along with the channel and the date, in one shared book. Within a year the book showed clearly which traders consistently paid below market and which months returned the best prices. No new infrastructure was built; the group simply stopped selling blind.

Weigh before you negotiate, know your break-even, ask how the price is calculated, and let the feed budget rather than the cash crisis choose the selling date.

Herd Health and Preventive Feeding

In this lesson
  • Build a twelve-month preventive feeding calendar keyed to your own calving and rainfall pattern
  • Place each supplementation window before the risk period rather than during it
  • Cost the calendar per head per year and compare it against the losses it prevents

Most nutrition-related disease in cattle is seasonal and predictable, which means it can be prevented by a calendar rather than diagnosed by a veterinarian. This lesson builds that calendar. The clinical detail behind each condition, including how to recognise and treat grass tetany, milk fever, ketosis, displaced abomasum, mastitis and bloat, is in Module 11; what follows is the operational scheduling.

The calendar has five recurring windows.

  1. The magnesium window, before and through the flush of green growth after rain. Lactating cows need 18 to 21 g of magnesium per day against 7 to 9 g in late pregnancy, and prevention means 0.20 to 0.25 percent dietary magnesium, or about 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head per day, or a free-choice high-magnesium mineral at 8 to 10 percent Mg. The window opens two to three weeks before the first grazing of lush growth, not on the day the first case appears. Delaying grazing until forage is at least 6 inches tall reduces the risk at no cost.
  2. The periparturient window, five weeks before through three weeks after calving. This is the published window for vitamin E and selenium supplementation, at a ratio of 1 mg of selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E, with lactating dairy cattle needing 25 to 30 IU of vitamin E per lb of dry matter. It is also the window in which calcium must not be over-supplied before calving, with post-calving calcium at 0.30 to 0.45 percent and periparturient magnesium at 0.40 to 0.50 percent.
  3. The transition window, the three weeks after calving. A three-week step-up onto the milking diet with high-quality forage and gradual concentrate increase is the published approach, keeping any change between feeding groups within 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter.
  4. The stored-forage vitamin window, whenever cattle have been on stored or dormant forage for more than a few weeks. Beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage; gestating cows at 1,200 lb need 34,000 IU of vitamin A per day and lactating cows 54,000 IU, with vitamin D at 125 IU per lb of diet dry matter.
  5. The concentrate step-up window, whenever cattle move onto a higher-grain diet. Maximum concentrate is 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day and forage NDF must stay above 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter.

Worked example: costing the calendar for a 20-cow herd.

  1. Magnesium, 60 days across the flush. A high-magnesium mineral at an assumed 0.07 USD per head per day: 20 x 60 x 0.07 = 84.00 USD.
  2. Vitamin E and selenium, 56 days per cow around calving at 0.06 USD per head per day: 20 x 56 x 0.06 = 67.20 USD.
  3. Vitamin A supplement, 120 dry-season days at 0.03 USD per head per day: 20 x 120 x 0.03 = 72.00 USD.
  4. Base year-round mineral at 0.05 USD per head per day for the remaining 129 days: 20 x 129 x 0.05 = 129.00 USD.
  5. Total: 352.20 USD per year, or 17.61 USD per cow per year, or under 5 cents per cow per day averaged across the year.

Now weigh that against what it prevents. One down cow that does not get up, one aborted calf, one cow that fails to conceive and eats for another year with nothing to sell, or one course of veterinary treatment will each cost more than the whole herd's annual programme. That is the entire economic argument for preventive feeding and it does not require any assumption about response rates.

Building your own calendar takes one sheet of paper and three inputs: your rainfall pattern, your calving pattern and your forage inventory.

  1. Draw twelve columns for the months.
  2. Mark the expected start of the rains and the flush of green growth.
  3. Mark your calving period or periods.
  4. Mark the months in which cattle will be on stored or dormant forage.
  5. Place the five windows above onto that chart, each starting before its risk period.
  6. Write the product, the daily allowance and the cost per head per day into each window.
  7. Pin it in the feed store next to the ration card.

Two disciplines make the calendar work. First, buy the product before the window opens; a mineral ordered on the day the first case appears arrives two weeks late. Second, record what was actually fed, using the batch and stock records from Module 7 Lesson 6, because a calendar that is not executed is indistinguishable from one that never existed.

Feed is not the whole of preventive health. Parasite control, dipping and vaccination sit alongside it, and a heavy worm burden will defeat any ration. But the feeding calendar is the part you control completely and the part that costs least.

Concrete African context. A smallholder dairy group in Nyandarua County, Kenya, drew a single twelve-month wall chart for the whole group, marking the long rains, the short rains and the group's two calving peaks, and agreed a bulk order of high-magnesium mineral timed to arrive three weeks before the long rains. Buying as a group cut the unit price, and buying in advance meant the mineral was in the store before the grass came, which was the actual change that mattered.

Put every window before its risk period, cost the whole year in one figure, and buy ahead.

Regulatory Compliance and Record Keeping

In this lesson
  • Keep the four record sets that satisfy both a buyer's audit and your own management needs
  • State the published feed and milk limits for the mycotoxins that cause rejections
  • Observe withdrawal periods and market restrictions on regulated feed additives

Compliance for a small cattle enterprise is not complicated, but it is unforgiving: the paperwork either exists on the day it is asked for or it does not. Four record sets cover almost everything a buyer, a lender or a regulator will ask, and each of them is also a management tool you should want anyway.

The four record sets are:

  1. Animal and weight records. Identity, date of birth or purchase, source, monthly weights, movements on and off the farm.
  2. Feed records. What was purchased, from whom, in what quantity, at what price, on what date, with the supplier's lot or batch number; and what was mixed and fed to which group on which day.
  3. Health and treatment records. Animal identity, date, product name, dose, who administered it, the reason, and the withdrawal period end date for both milk and meat.
  4. Sales records. Date, animal or volume, channel, buyer, weight, price per kg or per litre, and payment received.

The mycotoxin limits are the numbers most likely to appear in a rejection notice, and they belong on the wall of the feed store. Aflatoxin is limited to 20 micrograms per kg in cattle feed, with 2.0 to 6.2 percent of intake transferring to milk and a milk limit of 0.5 micrograms per kg set to protect human infants. Fumonisin has an FDA guidance level of 15 mg per kg for lactating dairy and breeding cattle, with minimal milk transfer of 0 to 0.05 percent. Deoxynivalenol, commonly called vomitoxin, is advised at 5 mg per kg in grains where grain is no more than 40 percent of the diet, with cattle relatively resistant because rumen microbes convert it to the less toxic DOM-1 and milk transfer at or below 0.02 percent. Zearalenone causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers above 10 mg per kg and affects lactating cow fertility above 20 mg per kg. Ergot alkaloids cause clinical signs at around 2 mg per kg of ergotine in the diet.

The practical risk-management steps that go with those numbers are short. Inspect visually for discoloured grain, mould growth and off odours. Send suspect hay or silage for a mycotoxin screen. Verify that your grain supplier has an aflatoxin testing protocol, and keep the delivery note that identifies the lot. Store feed dry, below 12 to 14 percent moisture, and rotate stock so the oldest is used first.

Worked example: what a traceable record actually does. A processor reports an aflatoxin result above the milk limit from a bulk collection.

  1. Your feed records show three maize bran deliveries in the relevant period, with supplier names and lot numbers.
  2. Your batch sheets show which of those deliveries went into which mix on which days.
  3. Your health records show no treatments that could confound the result.
  4. You can therefore isolate one supplier and one lot within an hour, stop feeding it, retain a sample, and demonstrate to the processor exactly what action was taken.
  5. Without those records, the only available response is to stop feeding everything and hope, and the processor has no reason to believe the problem is under control.

Regulated feed additives are the other compliance area, and the key point for an African producer is that the rules differ by destination market. Ionophores such as monensin are used in an estimated 90 to 97 percent of US feedlots at typical inclusions of 20 to 30 g per ton of diet, improving feed efficiency by 6.4 percent, and are approved in some jurisdictions and not others. Beta-agonists such as ractopamine, fed for the last 28 to 42 days before slaughter, are approved in the USA but banned in the EU and Canada, and some international markets restrict imports carrying residues. Growth implants using estradiol or trenbolone are approved in the USA, Canada and Australia but generally banned in the EU. All of these are regulated products whose approvals, dosages and withdrawal periods change, so verify current status with your national regulator and a veterinarian before use, and check what your buyer's market requires. If you sell into an export chain, the buyer's specification, not your national minimum, is the standard you must meet.

Withdrawal periods deserve their own discipline. A treated cow's milk must be discarded until the withdrawal period expires, and a treated animal must not be slaughtered before its meat withdrawal ends. The failure mode is always the same: a cow is treated, the person who treated her is not the person who milks her, and nobody wrote it down. The fix is a treatment board in the milking parlour with the cow's identity and the date on which she returns to the bulk tank, plus the same information in the health record book.

Concrete African context. A dairy cooperative in the Kabale district of Uganda began requiring members to present a simple treatment record before milk was accepted after any veterinary visit. The requirement cost nothing and removed almost all antibiotic residue rejections at the collection centre within a season, because the record forced the withdrawal date to be written down at the moment of treatment rather than remembered afterwards.

Keep four books, write in them at the time, staple the delivery notes in, and post the withdrawal dates where the milker can see them.

Scaling and Growth Strategy

In this lesson
  • Identify which single resource actually limits the size of your operation
  • Calculate the working capital required per additional head before committing to expansion
  • Stage growth so that forage supply, cash and management capacity expand together

Most cattle operations that fail while growing do not fail on price. They fail because they added animals faster than they added feed, cash or supervision. Growth is an operational problem before it is a financial one, and it can be planned with the same arithmetic used everywhere else in this module.

Start by finding the binding constraint. There are only four candidates and only one of them is binding at any moment.

  1. Forage. Convert your land into tonnes of dry matter per year and divide by annual herd requirement, using the feed budget method from Module 6 Lesson 6. Pasture-based systems need roughly 2 to 5 acres per head depending on productivity, against 150 to 500 square feet per head in a confined feeding system.
  2. Cash. Every extra animal ties up its purchase price plus its feed for the whole feeding period before it returns anything.
  3. Management. One person can only observe, weigh, treat and record a limited number of animals properly. Beyond that, feed conversion quietly falls and nobody knows why.
  4. Market. Volume you cannot sell at your break-even is not growth.

Worked example: working capital per additional head, on a fattening enterprise.

  1. Purchase price of a 250 kg feeder at 2.20 USD per kg: 550.00 USD.
  2. Feed for 120 days at 9.0 kg of dry matter and 0.26 USD per kg: 280.80 USD.
  3. Non-feed cost at 0.35 USD per head per day for 120 days: 42.00 USD.
  4. Working capital per head: 872.80 USD, tied up for 120 days before any return.
  5. Adding 10 head therefore requires 8,728 USD of working capital, not the 5,500 USD that the purchase price alone suggests.
  6. Expected return at the Lesson 2 margin of 120.40 USD per head: 1,204 USD across 120 days on 8,728 USD deployed.

That last line is the one that decides whether to borrow. If credit costs more than that return over the same period, the expansion destroys value even though each animal is profitable. Work it out before you sign, not after.

Now stage the growth. The rule that keeps operations solvent is that forage must lead, cattle must follow, and each step must be proved before the next is taken.

  1. Year one, fix the current herd. Get feed conversion measured, cost of gain calculated, and the four record sets running. An operation that cannot measure itself must not grow.
  2. Year two, expand forage first. Establish or renovate pasture, build silage capacity, secure a residue supply. From Module 6, home-made hay came out at about 68 USD per tonne of dry matter and silage at about 78 USD, against a bought price of 110 USD, so every tonne you produce yourself also raises the margin on the animals you already have.
  3. Year three, add animals up to the new forage capacity, in one increment, and re-measure everything.
  4. Only then consider adding a second production group, extra labour or a second site.

Two specific traps deserve naming. The first is expanding into the dry season. An operation that is comfortable in the rains and tight in the dry season has no spare capacity at all, because carrying capacity is set by the worst month, not the average. Adding cattle in a good rainy season and discovering the deficit in February is the single commonest failure in African cattle expansion. The second is expanding the herd while feed conversion is unmeasured. If your pen is converting at 9 to 1 when the forage benchmark is 8 to 1 to 12 to 1 and the concentrate benchmark is 5.5 to 6.5 to 1, doubling the herd doubles the inefficiency and doubles the loss.

Labour and supervision scale in steps, not smoothly. One extra animal costs nothing in management; the animal that forces you to hire a second worker costs a whole salary. Identify where those steps fall in your own operation and plan the increments to land just after a step rather than just before it.

Finally, decide what kind of business you are building. A larger version of the same commodity operation is a legitimate goal and is what this module has costed. Moving into processing, integration or premium markets is a different strategy with different capital and different risks, and is examined in Module 12 Lesson 5. Do not confuse the two: scaling a commodity operation is about cost per unit, and integration is about capturing margin from another stage of the chain.

Concrete African context. A dairy unit in Kiambu County, Kenya, grew from 8 to 25 cows over four years by a strict sequence: each year the owner added silage capacity first, filled it, and only then bought cows up to the new capacity. Neighbours who bought cows first and planned to buy feed later were repeatedly forced to sell animals in the dry season at poor prices. Same market, same genetics, opposite outcomes, and the difference was the order of the two decisions.

Find the binding constraint, expand it, prove the new level, and only then add animals.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A steer goes from 268 kg to 301 kg in 30 days. What is his average daily gain?

33 kg of gain divided by 30 days = 1.10 kg per day.

2. A pen eats 3,240 kg of dry matter and gains 396 kg. What is the feed conversion?

3,240 / 396 = 8.2 kg of dry matter per kg of gain.

3. What feed to gain ratio should a grain-finished feedlot achieve?

Published feedlot performance is 5.5 to 6.5 to 1 on a high-concentrate diet; forage-only systems run 8 to 1 to 12 to 1.

4. Why must feed be recorded as dry matter rather than as fed?

Working in dry matter removes moisture variation, so wetter silage does not appear as worse feed conversion.

5. Conversion is poor. What is the cheapest first thing to check?

Restricted or dirty water suppresses intake more than any feed change and costs nothing to investigate.

6. Total cost is 872.80 USD and closing weight is 382 kg. What is the break-even price?

872.80 / 382 = 2.28 USD per kg live weight.

7. Which figure allows a 120-day fattening animal to be compared with a dairy cow?

Margin per head per day puts enterprises of different lengths on the same basis.

8. A cow gives 12 litres at 0.40 USD and eats 1.47 USD of feed. What is income over feed cost?

4.80 - 1.47 = 3.33 USD per cow per day.

9. On a fattening unit, which cost line usually has the biggest effect on margin?

Opening animal value is the largest single component, so a small move in feeder price outweighs most feed changes.

10. What should be included in non-feed cost per head per day?

Non-feed cost covers labour, water, health, dipping, transport, a mortality allowance and interest, expressed per head per day.

11. A trader offers 900 USD for a 382 kg steer. What price per kg is that?

900 / 382 = 2.36 USD per kg live weight.

12. Your buyer pays a flat price per litre regardless of composition. What follows?

Higher fat milk costs more energy per litre to produce, so under a flat per-litre price that extra energy earns nothing.

13. What is the milk aflatoxin safety limit?

The milk limit is 0.5 micrograms per kg, far stricter than the 20 micrograms per kg allowed in feed, because it protects human infants.

14. Why is selling because you have run out of feed usually the worst timing?

End-of-dry-season destocking floods the market, which is why the feed budget should set the selling date months in advance.

15. Which channel typically pays the lowest price for live cattle?

Farm-gate sale is the most convenient and normally the lowest priced, because the trader carries the transport, risk and margin.

16. When should the magnesium window open?

The window must open before the risk period, two to three weeks ahead of the first grazing of lush green growth.

17. What is the periparturient supplementation window for vitamin E and selenium?

The published critical window is five weeks before through three weeks after calving.

18. Why must vitamin A be supplemented on stored forage?

Stored forage loses 33 to 50% of its beta-carotene per year, so cattle on old hay or dormant pasture need supplemental vitamin A.

19. What is the daily magnesium oxide guideline for cattle at grass tetany risk?

Published guidance is about 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head daily during the high-risk spring or flush period.

20. What makes a preventive feeding calendar fail most often?

A mineral ordered on the day the first case appears arrives weeks late; the calendar only works if product is bought ahead of the window.

21. What is the aflatoxin limit in cattle feed?

Feed is limited to 20 micrograms per kg; the milk limit is far stricter at 0.5 micrograms per kg.

22. Which record set lets you trace a mycotoxin problem to a single delivery?

Feed purchase records showing supplier, date and lot number, combined with batch sheets, isolate which delivery went into which mix.

23. Ractopamine is fed during which period, and where is it banned?

Beta-agonists such as ractopamine are fed for the last 28 to 42 days before slaughter and are banned in the EU and Canada.

24. Why do antibiotic residue rejections usually happen?

The person treating is often not the person milking, so the failure is a record-keeping one; writing the withdrawal end date at the moment of treatment prevents it.

25. At what dietary level does zearalenone affect lactating cow fertility?

Zearalenone above 10 mg/kg causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers and above 20 mg/kg affects lactating cow fertility.

26. What is the true working capital per additional fattening head in the worked example?

Purchase price 550.00 plus feed 280.80 plus non-feed 42.00 equals 872.80 USD tied up for the full feeding period.

27. Carrying capacity should be set against which period?

Carrying capacity is limited by the driest month; expanding on wet-season abundance guarantees a deficit later.

28. In what order should a cattle operation expand?

Forage must lead and cattle follow, because feed bought in a deficit is always dearer than feed produced in advance.

29. Why should an operation measure feed conversion before growing?

If conversion is already poor, doubling the herd doubles the loss; an operation that cannot measure itself should not grow.

30. How does management capacity scale?

Supervision scales in steps; the animal that forces a second salary costs far more than the one before it.

Module 8 capstone

Ninety-Day Operating System. Step 1: fix a weigh day. Choose one day a month, borrow or buy a weigh band or a crush scale, and weigh every animal in one production group on that day for three consecutive months, recording animal identity, date and weight in a single book. Step 2: over the same three months record every kilogram of feed delivered to that group and its cost in USD, so that at the end you can calculate average daily gain per head, feed conversion as kg of dry matter per kg of gain, and feed cost per kg of gain. Compare your feed conversion against the published benchmarks of 5.5 to 6.5 to 1 for grain-finished cattle and 8 to 1 to 12 to 1 for forage-only systems. Step 3: build the margin per head. Record purchase or opening value, total feed cost, non-feed costs per head per day, and either the sale price achieved or a current market valuation, and calculate margin per head and margin per head per day. Step 4: set up the four record books - animal and weight, feed purchased and fed, health and treatment with withdrawal dates, and sales and prices - and use them for the full ninety days. Step 5: write a twelve-month preventive feeding calendar marking the magnesium window before the flush, the vitamin E and selenium window from five weeks before to three weeks after each calving group, the vitamin A supplementation period on stored or dormant forage, and every planned ration step-up. Step 6: assemble a compliance file with feed purchase records and lot numbers, treatment records with withdrawal periods, and any milk or meat buyer requirements in writing. Step 7: at day ninety, write one page stating your actual cost of gain or cost per litre, the single largest cost line, and the one operational change you will make in the next quarter.
