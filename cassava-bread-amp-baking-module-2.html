Lessons

1. Grating cassava using manual and motorized graters

Grating is the first step in turning fresh cassava roots into flour. You must grate the roots within 24 hours of harvest, or they will spoil. Start by peeling the roots with a sharp knife, removing both the outer bark and the inner rind. Wash the peeled roots in clean water to remove dirt and sand. Cut them into chunks that fit your grater.

For manual grating, you can use a traditional wooden grater with a perforated metal sheet, or a simple hand grater. Hold the root firmly and push it against the rough surface, moving it back and forth. This is hard work: one person can grate about 10-15 kg per hour. For larger batches, a motorized grater is better. A small diesel or electric grater can process 100-200 kg per hour. You can buy one or build one using a engine and a drum with sharp teeth.

After grating, the cassava mash should be a fine, wet pulp. If the pieces are too coarse, the flour will be gritty. Check by rubbing a little mash between your fingers; it should feel like wet sawdust. If it's too coarse, pass it through the grater again.

Work in a clean area. Use a plastic sheet or clean concrete floor to catch the mash. Wear gloves if your hands are sensitive, because cassava juice can irritate skin. Also, the juice contains cyanide, so never taste raw mash. After grating, move quickly to pressing to remove excess water and toxins.

In many African countries, women grate cassava in groups, singing and working together. This makes the job faster and more social. If you are alone, take breaks to avoid back strain. Remember, a sharp grater is safer than a dull one because it requires less force.

2. Pressing and fermenting cassava mash for flour

After grating, the cassava mash contains about 60-70% water. You must press it to remove excess liquid and reduce cyanide. The traditional method is to put the mash in a woven bag (like a jute sack) and press it under heavy stones or a lever press for 1-2 days. A simple screw press or hydraulic jack press can shorten this to 3-4 hours. Press until the mash feels firm and crumbly, with less than 40% moisture.

Fermentation happens during pressing. The natural bacteria and enzymes in the mash break down starches and detoxify cyanide. For flour, ferment for 12-24 hours at room temperature (25-30C). In cooler weather, it may take 36-48 hours. You can tell it's ready when the mash smells slightly sour, like fermented dough, and feels dry. If it smells rotten or has mold, throw it away.

If you want a milder taste, ferment for a shorter time (6-12 hours). For a stronger sour taste, ferment up to 3 days. In West Africa, many people prefer a sour note in their cassava flour, while in East Africa, a milder flour is common. Adjust to your market.

After pressing, break up the mash with your hands or a wooden paddle. It should be crumbly, not sticky. If it's still wet, press more. You can also use a hydraulic jack to press the bag between two boards. Place a bucket under the press to catch the liquid, which can be discarded or used as pesticide (it contains cyanide).

Always press on a raised platform or clean concrete, not on bare ground, to avoid contamination. Wash your hands and tools before and after. The pressed mash is now ready for drying. If you are not drying immediately, keep it in a cool, ventilated place and use within 24 hours to prevent spoilage.

3. Drying cassava mash on racks or solar dryers

Drying is critical: if the flour is not dry enough, it will mold within weeks. The target moisture content is 10-12%. You can test by squeezing a handful of dried mash; it should crumble and not stick together. A more accurate way is to use a moisture meter, but if you don't have one, dry until the pieces snap when bent.

Sun drying on racks is the cheapest method. Build wooden racks 1 meter high, covered with clean mesh or woven mats. Spread the pressed mash in a thin layer, no more than 2-3 cm thick. Stir it every 2 hours with a rake or clean stick. Under strong sun (30-35C), it takes 2-3 days to dry. Bring the mash indoors at night or cover it with a tarpaulin to prevent dew and rain.

Solar dryers are better because they protect from dust, rain, and animals. A simple cabinet solar dryer has a black-painted interior and a clear plastic or glass top. The temperature inside can reach 50-60C, drying the mash in 1-2 days. You can build one with local materials: wood frame, plastic sheet, and a black metal tray. Place the dryer in full sun, facing the equator. Open vents to let moist air escape.

In humid regions, drying may take longer. Use a combination of solar drying and a fire-heated dryer if available. For fire drying, use a low fire and a mesh tray, stirring constantly to avoid scorching. The mash should be white or cream-colored, not brown or black.

After drying, the mash is called 'dry cake' or 'gari' if fermented. It should be hard and brittle. Break any lumps before milling. Store the dry mash in clean sacks in a dry place until you mill it. If it gets wet, dry it again immediately.

4. Milling dried cassava into fine flour

Milling turns the dried cassava mash into fine flour. You can use a manual mill (like a corn mill) or a motorized hammer mill or disc mill. For small-scale baking, a motorized mill is faster and gives a finer texture. A hammer mill with a 1-2 mm screen will produce flour that passes through a 0.5 mm sieve after sifting.

Before milling, ensure the dried mash is completely dry. If it's even slightly damp, it will clog the mill and produce lumpy flour. Break large pieces with a pestle or hammer. Feed the mash into the mill hopper gradually. Do not overfeed, or the mill will jam. For a motorized mill, run it at the recommended speed (usually 2,800-3,600 rpm). For a manual mill, turn the handle steadily.

After the first milling, you will get a mix of fine flour and coarse particles. That's normal. You will sift it later. The flour should be warm from friction; let it cool before packaging. If the mill is too hot, the flour may become sticky. In that case, mill in smaller batches and let the machine cool between batches.

In many African villages, you can take your dried cassava to a local mill for a fee (e.g., 200-500 Naira per 10 kg in Nigeria, or 500-1000 shillings in Kenya). If you mill your own, clean the mill thoroughly after use to prevent contamination. Also, wear a dust mask to avoid inhaling flour dust, which can harm your lungs.

Milling is noisy and dusty. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated room. After milling, store the flour in clean, dry sacks or plastic containers. Use within 3 months for best quality. If you want to fortify the flour, you can mix in 5-10% soybean or groundnut flour after milling, but that is optional.

5. Sieving cassava flour for consistent texture

Sieving removes coarse particles and ensures your flour is fine and uniform. This is important for baking because coarse flour makes dense, gritty bread. Use a sieve with a mesh size of 0.5 mm to 1 mm. A standard kitchen flour sieve works, or you can make one with a wooden frame and wire mesh. For larger batches, use a rotary sifter or a mechanical shaker.

Place the sieve over a clean bucket or tray. Pour the milled flour into the sieve in small amounts. Shake the sieve side to side or tap it gently. The fine flour falls through, while coarse particles remain on top. Do not press the flour through with your hands, as this can push coarse bits through. If the flour is damp, it will clog the sieve. In that case, dry it further in the sun for a few hours.

The coarse particles you remove can be re-milled. Put them back into the mill and grind again. You can repeat this until all flour passes through the sieve. The final yield of fine flour is usually 70-80% of the original dried mash. The remaining 20-30% is coarse bits that can be used for animal feed or re-milled.

After sieving, you should have a soft, powdery flour. Test by rubbing a pinch between your fingers; it should feel like talcum powder, not sand. If it's still gritty, sieve again with a finer mesh. You can also sift twice: first with a 1 mm sieve, then with a 0.5 mm sieve for very fine flour.

Work in a dust-free area. Sieving produces a lot of dust, so wear a mask. Clean your sieves and buckets after use to prevent mold. Store the sieved flour in airtight containers. In humid climates, add a moisture absorber sachet (like silica gel) to keep it dry. Properly sieved flour will give you consistent baking results every time.

6. Packaging and storing cassava flour for baking

Packaging protects your flour from moisture, insects, and contamination. Use clean, dry containers: plastic buckets with tight lids, heavy-duty plastic bags, or woven polypropylene sacks with inner plastic liners. For retail, small 1 kg or 2 kg bags are popular. You can seal plastic bags with a heat sealer or tie them tightly. Label each package with the date and type of flour (e.g., 'Cassava Flour, Fermented, Best Before: [date]').

Before packaging, ensure the flour is completely cool and dry. Any warmth will cause condensation inside the package, leading to mold. Fill containers to the top to reduce air space. If using sacks, double-bag them: an inner plastic bag and an outer woven sack. Store in a cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight and strong odors. The ideal storage temperature is 20-25C with less than 60% humidity.

Shelf life: properly dried and packaged cassava flour can last 6-12 months. If you add a moisture absorber, it can last up to 18 months. Check periodically for signs of spoilage: lumps, musty smell, or insects. If you see weevils, freeze the flour for 48 hours to kill eggs, then sift and repackage. Never sell moldy flour.

For long-term storage, keep flour in a dark room or cupboard. Avoid storing near chemicals, fuel, or strong-smelling foods like onions, as flour absorbs odors. In humid regions, use airtight plastic containers and keep them off the floor on wooden pallets. If you live in a very humid area, consider using vacuum sealing if available.

When you sell, package in attractive, clean bags with your brand name. Customers trust flour that looks and smells fresh. Always rotate your stock: sell older flour first. Write the production date on each package. With good packaging and storage, your cassava flour will be ready for baking bread, cakes, and pastries anytime.

<h2>Practical Reference: From Cassava to Flour</h2>

<p>Cassava (<em>Manihot esculenta</em>) is one of Africa's most important food security and income-generating crops. Nigeria alone produces over 60 million tonnes annually, making it the world's largest producer (FAOSTAT, 2023). Yet post-harvest losses and inefficient processing continue to limit the crop's full commercial potential. This practical reference module covers the complete transformation chain from fresh cassava roots to high-quality cassava flour (HQCF) suitable for baking a value-addition pathway that can increase farmer and processor margins by 3060% compared to selling raw roots.</p>

<p>High-Quality Cassava Flour (HQCF) is a white, fine flour with low cyanide content, suitable for partial or full substitution of wheat flour in bread, biscuits, cakes, and snacks. The market for HQCF in Africa is growing rapidly, driven by import substitution policies (notably Nigeria's backward integration policy) and rising wheat costs. Processors who master the six-step chain below can supply bakeries, supermarkets, and institutional buyers with a premium product.</p>

<h3>Step 1: Grating Cassava Manual and Motorized Graters</h3>
<p>Grating breaks the root's cell walls, releasing starch granules and facilitating cyanide reduction during pressing and fermentation. Manual graters (rasp-type) process 2050 kg/hour and suit micro-processors. Motorized graters (diesel or electric) handle 2001,000 kg/hour and are essential for commercial operations. The grating surface must be sharp and clean; dull graters produce uneven pulp and reduce yields. For HQCF, grate within 24 hours of harvest to prevent post-harvest deterioration (PPD).</p>

<h3>Step 2: Pressing and Fermenting Cassava Mash</h3>
<p>After grating, the mash is fermented (typically 1224 hours, or 35 days for traditional <em>lafun</em>/<em>fufu</em>) to reduce cyanogenic glycosides. Pressing removes excess water and dissolved cyanide. A hydraulic press or screw press is used. Target moisture after pressing: 4555%. Fermentation time affects flavour, colour, and acidity shorter fermentation (1224h) yields milder flour for baking.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Drying Cassava Mash on Racks or Solar Dryers</h3>
<p>Drying reduces moisture to 10% for safe milling and storage. Sun drying on raised racks is the cheapest method but depends on weather and risks contamination. Solar dryers (cabinet or tunnel type) offer faster drying (23 days vs. 57 days), better hygiene, and consistent quality. Temperature should not exceed 60C to avoid gelatinization.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Milling Dried Cassava into Fine Flour</h3>
<p>Hammer mills, disc mills, or pin mills are used. A hammer mill with a 12 mm screen produces flour suitable for baking. For premium HQCF, a pin mill or turbo mill achieves finer particle size. Throughput: 100500 kg/hour for small mills. Ensure the mill is cleaned between batches to prevent cross-contamination.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Sieving Cassava Flour for Consistent Texture</h3>
<p>Sieving removes coarse particles and fibre. Use a vibratory or rotary sifter with mesh sizes 60100 (250150 m). Re-mill oversize particles. Consistent particle size is critical for baking performance it affects water absorption and crumb structure.</p>

<h3>Step 6: Packaging and Storing Cassava Flour for Baking</h3>
<p>Pack in food-grade, moisture-proof bags (e.g., laminated PET/PE or polypropylene). Vacuum sealing extends shelf life. Label with production date, batch number, and nutritional information. Store in a cool, dry, ventilated warehouse away from direct sunlight. Use FIFO (First In, First Out) stock rotation.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING: Cyanide Poisoning Risk</strong>
<p style="margin-top:8px; color:#5a1010;">Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when hydrolysed. Inadequate grating, pressing, fermentation, or drying can leave dangerous HCN levels. <strong>Never skip fermentation and pressing steps.</strong> Always test final flour with picrate paper or laboratory analysis before sale or consumption. HCN levels above 10 ppm can cause acute poisoning symptoms include headache, dizziness, vomiting, and in severe cases, respiratory failure. Workers handling large volumes of raw cassava should wear gloves and ensure good ventilation. Process in well-ventilated areas and never allow children or untrained persons to handle raw mash.</p>
