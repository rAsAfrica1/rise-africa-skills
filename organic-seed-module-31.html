🧠 Brain Opener

Certification is not one thing. It is a family of standards. EU, USDA, JAS, and PGS are four different standards. Which one you need depends on which market you are selling to.

  • What is the difference between EU, USDA, JAS, and PGS?
  • Which standard does your buyer require?
  • What does each standard require you to prove?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

EU 2018/848 covers the European Union. USDA covers the United States. JAS covers Japan. PGS is a community-based standard accepted by many buyers. Your buyer tells you which one they require. All four require records, no banned inputs, and 2 to 3 years of conversion.

📖 Nyari Picks Her Standard

Nyari had been selling organic seed to local buyers for 3 years. She wanted to start exporting.

She contacted three potential buyers: one in Germany, one in the United States, one in Japan.

Each one sent her a document.

The German buyer: "We require EU 2018/848 certification. Must be from an approved certification body. Annual inspection."

The US buyer: "We require USDA Organic certification. Must be from a USDA-accredited certifier. Annual inspection."

The Japanese buyer: "We require JAS Organic. Must be from a JAS-registered certifier. Annual inspection."

Nyari looked at the three certifications. Each one cost 6,000 to 15,000 dollars per year. She could not afford all three.

She called the German buyer and asked one question: "Do you accept PGS?"

The buyer said no. Then Nyari asked if the buyer knew any EU certifiers that operated in Africa.

The buyer sent her a list of four. Two of them had offices in Kenya. Nyari contacted them. One quoted 8,000 dollars for the first year, then 5,000 per year.

Nyari calculated. Her export contract would earn 20,000 dollars per year. Certification costs 5,000. Net gain: 15,000. Worth it.

She worked with the certifier. The process took 6 months. She passed.

Her first export was 200 kilograms of organic tomato seed to Germany at 95 dollars per kilogram - 19,000 dollars.

She had paid 8,000 for the first-year certification. She had netted 11,000 dollars more than she would have earned on the local market.

The certification was expensive, but it opened a market that was 5 times more valuable.

💡 The Big Idea

Match the certification to the buyer

You do not need every certification. You need the one your specific buyer requires. Ask the buyer. Get a list of approved certifiers. Compare quotes. Do not waste money on certifications you do not need.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

EU 2018/848

The European Union regulation. In force since January 2022. Covers all organic products sold in the EU. Requires an approved certifier. Annual inspection. Product must be certified for the entire supply chain.

What you must prove: no banned inputs for 2 to 3 years, traceability from seed to sale, records for 5 years, annual inspection, and separation from conventional production.

Cost: 6,000 to 15,000 dollars year one. 4,000 to 8,000 dollars subsequent years.

2

USDA Organic

The United States standard. Covers all organic products sold in the US. Requires a USDA-accredited certifier. Annual inspection. Similar requirements to EU - no banned inputs, traceability, records.

What you must prove: land has been organic for 3 years, no prohibited substances used, records of every input, no GMO, and no sewage sludge.

Cost: 5,000 to 12,000 dollars year one. Similar in subsequent years.

3

JAS Organic (Japan)

The Japanese standard. Very strict. Requires a JAS-registered certifier. Annual inspection. Requires Japanese-language documentation for the buyer.

Similar to EU and USDA but with stricter requirements on processing and packaging. Often the most expensive certification to maintain.

Cost: 8,000 to 20,000 dollars year one. 6,000 to 12,000 dollars subsequent years.

4

PGS - the practical alternative

Participatory Guarantee System. Community-based certification. Farmers inspect each other. Local PGS council approves. Accepted by many national markets and some international buyers.

What you must prove: same organic standards as third-party (no banned inputs, traceability), plus participation in the local PGS group.

Cost: 100 to 500 dollars per year. Fast - usually 2 to 4 months to certificate.

🛠 Hands-On - Match Your Buyer to a Standard

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • The certification requirements from your buyers
  1. List every buyer you have or want to have.
  2. For each, find out which certification they require.
  3. If they accept PGS, note that. If they require third-party, note which one.
  4. Make a table: buyer, standard required, annual cost, time to certify.
  5. Add up the cost of all required certifications.
  6. Compare with the revenue each buyer would bring.
  7. Prioritize the buyers with the best cost-to-revenue ratio.
  8. Start with one certification. Add more only when the revenue justifies it.
Why it works: One well-chosen certification beats three expensive ones.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Kenya organic pineapple

Kenyan organic pineapple exporters to the EU must maintain EU 2018/848 certification. Cost per year: 12,000 dollars. Revenue premium from EU market: 3 to 4 times the local price. The certification is expensive but the premium pays for it many times over.

Uganda organic coffee

Ugandan coffee cooperatives export under USDA and EU certifications. Their certification is paid for by the buyer through a premium of 40 cents per kilogram above commodity price. The certification is essentially free - the buyer pays.
