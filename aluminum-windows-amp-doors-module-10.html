1. Calculating Material Costs per Window or Door

To price any window or door, you first need to know exactly what it costs you in materials. Start by measuring the frame. For a standard 1.2m x 1.5m window, you need aluminum profiles: top, bottom, left, right, and a middle horizontal bar. Each profile length is cut from standard 6-meter bars. Calculate total linear meters: for this window, the perimeter is (1.2+1.5)*2 = 5.4m, plus the middle bar 1.2m = 6.6m. But you must add 10% for cutting waste, so buy 7.3m. Aluminum bars cost about $12 per meter in many African cities, so 7.3m x $12 = $87.60. Next, glass: for a 1.2x1.5 window, you need two panes of 0.6x0.7m (since the middle bar splits it). Glass costs $25 per square meter, so area = 0.6*0.7*2 = 0.84 sqm, cost = $21. Add rubber seals: 6.6m at $0.50/m = $3.30. Add screws, handles, and hinges: about $8. So total material cost = $87.60 + $21 + $3.30 + $8 = $119.90. For a door, say 0.9m x 2.1m, perimeter = 6m, plus a lock stile and maybe a mid-rail. Aluminum: 6m + 2m = 8m, with waste 8.8m, cost $105.60. Glass or panel: often 0.8m x 1.8m = 1.44 sqm, at $25 = $36. Hardware: lock, handle, hinges = $25. Total material = $105.60 + $36 + $25 = $166.60. Always use a notebook or phone app to record these costs for each job. Keep receipts. Update prices every month because aluminum and glass prices change. This gives you the true material cost before labour and profit.

2. Pricing Your Products for Profit

Once you know material cost, you must add labour, overhead, and profit. Labour: if you pay a worker $10 per day and he makes two windows per day, labour per window is $5. But you also work yourself. Include your time. For a standard window, set labour at 30% of material cost. For the $119.90 window, labour = $36. Overhead includes rent, electricity, transport, and tools depreciation. If your monthly overhead is $300 and you make 30 windows per month, overhead per window = $10. So total cost = material $119.90 + labour $36 + overhead $10 = $165.90. Now add profit. A common mistake is to add only 10%. In aluminum fabrication, aim for 30-50% profit margin on total cost. For 40% profit, selling price = cost / (1 - 0.40) = $165.90 / 0.6 = $276.50. That may seem high, but customers expect quality. Check competitors: if they sell similar windows at $250, you can price at $260 and still profit. For doors, material $166.60, labour 30% = $50, overhead $10, total cost = $226.60. At 40% profit, price = $226.60 / 0.6 = $377.70. Always quote a price that includes installation if you offer it. Installation adds transport and labour: add $20 per window. Never negotiate below your total cost. If a customer pushes, offer a smaller size or simpler design. Use a simple formula: Selling Price = (Material + Labour + Overhead) / (1 - Profit Margin). Keep a price list for common sizes. Review prices every three months.

3. Managing Cash Flow and Buying Materials

Cash flow is the money moving in and out of your business. Many aluminum workshops fail not because they lack profit, but because they run out of cash to buy materials. For example, you get a big order for 10 windows. You need $1,200 for materials upfront. If you have only $300, you cannot start. So you must manage cash carefully. First, always ask for a deposit. A 50% deposit on a $2,500 order gives you $1,250, enough to buy materials. Use that deposit only for that job's materials. Keep the rest for labour and overhead. Second, buy materials in bulk when prices are low. Aluminum prices often rise, so if you have extra cash, buy a few extra bars. But don't tie up all your cash in stock. Keep at least $500 as emergency cash for small repairs or transport. Third, track every job's cash: money in from customer, money out for materials, labour, transport. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. At the end of each week, calculate how much cash you have. If you have less than $200, you cannot take new orders until you collect payments. Fourth, collect payments on time. For large jobs, split payments: 50% deposit, 40% on delivery, 10% after installation. If a customer delays, follow up politely but firmly. Fifth, separate business cash from personal cash. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary, say $100 per week, instead of dipping into job deposits. This discipline keeps your business alive.

4. Finding Reliable Suppliers in Your Area

Your business depends on reliable suppliers of aluminum profiles, glass, and hardware. Start by asking other fabricators in your area. They know who delivers on time and who sells fake profiles. Visit local hardware stores and aluminum distributors. In many African cities, you can find suppliers in industrial areas or large markets. For example, in Nairobi, go to Industrial Area; in Lagos, try Ladipo or Alaba markets. When you meet a supplier, ask three questions: 1) Do you have a price list? 2) How long does delivery take? 3) Can I return defective materials? Test them with a small order first. Buy one or two bars and check quality. Look for the grade of aluminum: 6063 T5 is common for windows and doors. Avoid thin profiles that bend easily. For glass, find a supplier who cuts to size accurately. Ask if they temper glass for doors. For hardware, buy from shops that specialize in aluminum accessories, not general hardware. Build relationships. Visit your supplier regularly, pay on time, and negotiate discounts for bulk orders. If a supplier is unreliable, find a backup. Always have at least two suppliers for each material. Keep a record of prices, phone numbers, and delivery times. Join a local fabricators' WhatsApp group to share supplier recommendations. When you find a good supplier, ask for a credit account after six months of prompt payments. That helps your cash flow.

5. Hiring and Training Apprentices

As your business grows, you cannot do everything alone. Hiring an apprentice is a low-cost way to increase production. In many African countries, apprentices learn on the job and receive a small stipend, not a full salary. Start by looking for young people aged 18-25 who are eager to learn. Ask family, friends, or local vocational schools for recommendations. When you hire, set clear expectations: working hours, tasks, and pay. For example, pay $3 per day for the first three months, then $5 per day after they can cut and assemble a simple window. Train them step by step. First, teach safety: always wear gloves and safety glasses when cutting aluminum. Second, teach measuring and cutting: how to use a tape measure, square, and hacksaw or cut-off saw. Third, teach assembly: how to fit corners, install glass, and attach handles. Use real jobs, not practice pieces. Show them once, then let them do it while you watch. Correct mistakes immediately. After one month, let them work on simple windows alone, but check their work. After three months, they should be able to make a standard window from start to finish. Pay them fairly and on time. Treat them with respect. A good apprentice can become your best worker. Consider sending them for a short course in welding or glass cutting if available. Keep a training log for each apprentice. After one year, you can hire them as a full employee or help them start their own workshop. This builds your reputation and creates a network of skilled workers.

6. Marketing Your Business Locally

Marketing does not require a big budget. Start with your immediate area. Tell everyone you meet: neighbors, shopkeepers, taxi drivers. Word of mouth is the strongest marketing in Africa. When you finish a job, ask the customer for a referral. Offer a small discount, say 5%, for every new customer they send. Put a signboard at your workshop with your phone number and what you do: 'Aluminum Windows & Doors â Made to Measure'. Use your phone to take photos of every finished job. Post them on WhatsApp status, Facebook Marketplace, and local buy-and-sell groups. Always include your phone number. Create a simple business card with your name, number, and services. Give it to hardware stores, building contractors, and real estate agents. Visit construction sites and talk to foremen. Offer to quote on their window and door needs. Partner with local builders: give them a 10% commission for any job they bring. Attend community events and church gatherings; wear a branded T-shirt. When you get a call, respond quickly. Go to the site, measure, and give a written quote within 24 hours. Follow up after two days. Good service leads to repeat business. Ask satisfied customers to post a review on Google Maps if they can. Keep a list of all customers and their phone numbers. Send them a message every six months to ask if they need repairs or new windows. This simple system can keep you busy all year.

3. Managing Cash Flow and Buying Materials

Cash flow kills more African fabrication businesses than lack of orders. The rule is simple: never buy material for a job until you have received a deposit â typically 50â60% of the quoted price. Use the deposit to purchase profiles and glass; use the balance on delivery to pay labour and yourself.

Buy in bulk where storage is secure. A 10â15% discount on a full profile bundle is common when you buy from an importer rather than a retailer. But do not tie up all your cash in stock â keep at least one month's operating expenses liquid. Track every job on a simple cash-flow sheet: deposit in, materials out, labour out, balance in.

4. Finding Reliable Suppliers in Your Area

Your supplier is your business partner. A late delivery of profiles means a late installation and an angry customer. Build relationships with at least two suppliers per material category: aluminium profiles, glass, and hardware. Ask for trade terms (30-day credit once you have a track record), confirm whether prices include VAT, and always request a written quotation with a validity period.

In most African markets, the reliable route is through the national importers and distributors who supply the major fabricators. Ask other fabricators in your city who they trust. Visit the warehouse, inspect the profile wall thickness with a caliper, and check that the alloy grade and temper (typically 6063-T5) match the invoice.

5. Hiring and Training Apprentices

Apprentices are the cheapest and most effective way to grow capacity â but only if you train them properly. Hire for attitude and reliability, not just skill. A structured 12â24 month apprenticeship should cover: measuring and cutting, drilling and assembly, glazing, hardware installation, and site installation. Pay a stipend that grows with competence, and always keep a signed apprenticeship agreement.

In many African countries, formal apprenticeship frameworks exist through the national vocational training authority. Register your apprentices where possible â it improves their credibility and yours, and may unlock tax incentives or grant funding.

6. Marketing Your Business Locally

Your best marketing is a finished job that neighbours can see. Beyond word-of-mouth, the highest-return activities for an aluminium fabricator are:

  • A WhatsApp Business profile with photos of completed windows, doors, and shopfronts.
  • A simple signboard at your workshop with your phone number and a sample window on display.
  • Relationships with building contractors, architects, and hardware shops who can refer you.
  • A Facebook page or local marketplace listing with before/after photos.

Price transparently, deliver on time, and follow up on every quote within 48 hours. In African construction markets, reputation travels faster than any advertisement.

Aluminium cutting produces fine metal dust and sharp swarf. Always wear safety goggles, gloves, and a dust mask when cutting, drilling, or grinding. Ensure adequate ventilation â aluminium dust is combustible and can cause respiratory irritation. Use a residual current device (RCD) on all power tools. Never allow apprentices to operate mitre saws or grinders without supervision and training. Keep a first-aid kit and fire extinguisher in the workshop at all times.
