Lessons

1. Gates, Frames and Doors

Aluminium gates, frames, and doors are in high demand across Africa because they resist rust, look modern, and last for years. To build a standard pedestrian gate, you need 40x40mm square tube for the frame, 25x25mm for the infill, and 2mm thick aluminium sheet for panels. Start by measuring the opening: a typical gate is 1m wide and 1.8m high. Cut your tubes with a mitre saw or a hacksaw with a mitre box, ensuring 45-degree corners for a clean join. Clean the cut ends with a stainless steel wire brush, then clamp the pieces on a flat welding table. Use a TIG welder with 2% thoriated tungsten electrode and 4043 filler rod (1.6mm diameter). Set the machine to 80-100 amps for 2mm thickness. Weld tack welds at each corner first, check for square by measuring diagonals, then weld the full joints. For doors, use 50x50mm frame with 3mm thickness; increase amps to 120-140. Add hinges by drilling and tapping, or weld pre-made steel hinges if you use a bi-metal insert. For frames around windows, use 40x40mm and weld at 90 degrees; a corner bracket helps alignment. Always wear a welding helmet with shade 10-12, leather gloves, and a respiratoraluminium fumes are harmful. After welding, grind smooth with a flap disc, then finish with a wire brush and apply a clear lacquer or powder coat. A simple gate takes 2-3 hours and can sell for $150-$300 depending on design. Start with a small gate for a neighbour, then scale up.

2. Aluminium Boats and Canoes

Aluminium boats are ideal for African lakes, rivers, and coastal waters because they are lightweight, don't rot, and need less maintenance than wood. A typical 4-meter canoe or small fishing boat uses 3mm thick marine-grade aluminium sheets (5052 or 5083 alloy) for the hull and 40x40mm square tube for the gunwales and ribs. You will need a TIG welder capable of 200 amps, 5356 filler rod (1.6mm or 2.4mm), and a spool gun for MIG if you prefer faster welding on thicker sections. Start by building a jig from timber or steel to hold the hull shape. Cut the bottom sheet to size, then bend the sides using a brake or by clamping between two strong boards and hammering gently. Tack weld the hull seams every 100mm, then weld continuously with a slight back-step technique to control distortion. Use a heat sink (a copper bar) behind the seam to prevent burn-through. For ribs, space them every 400mm along the length. Weld the gunwales on top, then add a transom for an outboard motor using 6mm plate. Test for leaks by filling with water before painting. A 4m boat takes about 40-60 hours to build and can sell for $1,500-$3,000. Safety: always work in a well-ventilated area, and wear a P100 respirator when welding aluminium. Start with a small 2.5m dinghy to learn the techniques.

3. Water Tanks and Containers

Aluminium water tanks are popular in rural Africa because they are lighter than concrete, don't rust like steel, and can be repaired easily. A typical 1000-litre tank is made from 3mm aluminium sheet rolled into a cylinder, with a 4mm bottom and top. You need a sheet roller or a local metal fabricator to roll the body. Cut the sheet to size: for a 1m diameter and 1.3m height, the circumference is 3.14m, so cut 3.2m to allow overlap. Weld the vertical seam using TIG with 4043 filler at 120-140 amps, using a back-step to reduce distortion. Then weld the bottom plate to the cylinder with a fillet weld, and add a top with a manhole cover. Reinforce the tank with two 25x25mm angle rings around the circumference, welded at 200mm intervals. For containers like jerry cans or small drums, use 2mm sheet and a MIG welder with 1.2mm wire for speed. Always pressure-test the tank by filling with water and checking for leaks; mark leaks and re-weld after draining. A 1000L tank takes about 8-10 hours to build and sells for $200-$400. For food-grade containers, use 5052 alloy and clean welds with a stainless brush, then passivate with nitric acid paste. Safety: aluminium dust is explosive, so keep the work area clean and use a fume extractor.

4. Truck Bodies and Trailers

Aluminium truck bodies and trailers are big business in Africa because they are lighter than steel, which saves fuel and increases payload. A typical truck body for a 7-tonne truck uses 2mm corrugated aluminium sheet for the sides and 3mm for the floor, with a frame made from 60x60mm square tube. You will need a MIG welder with a spool gun (200-250 amps) because TIG is too slow for long seams. Use 5356 filler wire (1.2mm). Start by building the base frame on a flat concrete slab, ensuring it is square by measuring diagonals. Weld the crossmembers every 500mm. Then attach the floor sheet with plug welds every 150mm, followed by seam welds. For the sides, weld the corrugated panels to the vertical posts, using a stitch weld (50mm weld, 100mm gap) to control distortion. Add a front bulkhead and a tailgate with hinges and latches. For trailers, build a chassis from 100x50mm rectangular tube, then add a deck and sides. A 6-meter trailer takes 60-80 hours and sells for $3,000-$6,000. Always use a jig or clamps to keep everything aligned. Safety: use a welding curtain to protect bystanders, and wear a leather apron. Start with a small utility trailer to learn the skills.

5. Signs and Structures

Aluminium signs and structural frames are everywhere in African citiesfrom shop signs to billboard supports and bus shelters. For a typical 2m x 1m sign, use a 40x40mm frame with 2mm aluminium composite panel (ACP) or 3mm sheet. Weld the frame with TIG at 90-110 amps using 4043 filler. For the face, you can weld on a 1mm aluminium sheet or use self-tapping screws if the sign is removable. For large billboards, build a truss structure from 50x50mm tube with diagonal bracing, welded at 120 amps. For bus shelters, use 80x80mm posts and a roof frame from 40x40mm, with polycarbonate sheets attached. Always check for wind loads: in coastal areas, use thicker material and more bracing. Weld all joints fully to prevent water entry. After welding, grind smooth and paint with zinc-rich primer and topcoat. A simple shop sign takes 2-3 hours and sells for $50-$150; a bus shelter takes 20-30 hours and sells for $800-$1,500. Safety: when welding overhead, wear a leather cape and use a fire blanket. Start with small sign frames for local shops, then move to larger structures.

6. Repair Work: The Steady Income

Repair work is the bread and butter of an aluminium welder in Africa because everything from pots to truck bodies gets damaged and needs fixing. Common repairs include cracked water tanks, broken gate hinges, leaking boat seams, and torn truck sides. For a cracked tank, drain it, clean the area with a stainless brush, and grind a V-groove along the crack. Weld with TIG using 4043 filler at 100-120 amps, starting from the crack ends and working inward. For a broken hinge on a gate, cut off the old hinge, clean the area, and weld a new one with 4043 filler at 90 amps. For boat seams, re-weld with 5356 filler after cleaning with acetone. For truck body tears, use a MIG spool gun with 5356 wire at 180-200 amps, and add a backing plate if the tear is large. Always test your repair: fill tanks with water, open and close gates, and check boats in water. Charge by the hour or by the job: a small repair $10-$30, a large one $50-$200. Keep a portable welder and a generator for on-site repairsmany customers will pay extra for you to come to them. Safety: always disconnect power before repairing electrical components, and wear a respirator when welding dirty aluminium. Build a reputation for quick, reliable repairs, and you will never lack work.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Real Products, Real Money</h2>

<p>In aluminium welding, the difference between a workshop that survives and one that thrives is rarely the welding machine. It is the product mix. A welder who can only repair a cracked chair earns repair money. A welder who can build a gate, a boat, a water tank, a truck body, a sign frame, and then repair all of them earns product money, project money, and repeat maintenance money.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference module pulls together the six product families in this course gates, frames and doors; boats and canoes; water tanks and containers; truck bodies and trailers; signs and structures; and repair work into one working document. Use it as a bench reference, a pricing prompt, and a sales tool. The numbers are indicative for a small to medium aluminium fabrication workshop in sub-Saharan Africa, and they must be adjusted for your local metal prices, labour rates, transport, and taxes.</p>

<h3>Why Product Mix Beats Single-Product Shops</h3>

<p>Aluminium is expensive relative to mild steel, so the business case for aluminium rests on three advantages: it does not rust, it is light, and it can be extruded into profiles that assemble quickly. That means aluminium wins where corrosion, weight, or assembly speed matter coastal towns, truck fleets, water storage, signage, and marine work. A workshop that understands this can charge a premium for lifespan and low maintenance, not just for labour.</p>

<p>In practice, a healthy African aluminium workshop runs a portfolio: fast-turnaround repair jobs to cover daily costs, medium-ticket products (gates, frames, signs, tanks) to build margin, and larger project work (boats, truck bodies, trailers) to build reputation and cash reserves. The six lessons in this module are the six revenue streams. The skill is in sequencing them so that cash from repairs funds materials for products, and product profits fund the tooling and certification needed for bigger projects.</p>

<h3>Line 1: Gates, Frames and Doors</h3>
<p>This is the entry product line for most aluminium workshops because it needs only a mitre saw, a TIG or MIG set, and a flat assembly table. Aluminium gates and window frames sell on corrosion resistance and low weight. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, and Accra, salt air destroys steel gates within a few years, while aluminium frames can last decades with minimal maintenance.</p>

<p>Key commercial points: standardise your profiles so you buy in bulk; keep a small catalogue of gate and frame designs with fixed prices; and offer installation as a separate line item. Frames are often sold to builders in batches, so a relationship with two or three contractors can keep a workshop busy for months.</p>

<h3>Line 2: Aluminium Boats and Canoes</h3>
<p>Aluminium boats are a natural fit for Africa's lakes, rivers, and coastlines. They are lighter than steel or timber, do not rot, and need less maintenance. The market includes fishing canoes, passenger boats, patrol and rescue craft, and small cargo boats. This is higher-risk work because a failed hull weld is a safety failure, so only welders with solid TIG/MIG experience and proper jigs should take on hull fabrication.</p>

<p>Business notes: boats are usually sold on specification, not on a shelf, so you need a written design, a deposit, and a clear delivery date. Partner with a naval architect or an experienced boat builder for your first few hulls. Marine-grade 5083 or 6061 alloys are preferred; never substitute a general-purpose alloy in a hull without engineering advice.</p>

<h3>Line 3: Water Tanks and Containers</h3>
<p>Aluminium water tanks are popular for rooftops, farms, and construction sites because they are lighter than concrete or steel and resist corrosion. Typical products are rectangular or cylindrical tanks, transport containers, and food-grade bins. Welding quality is critical: any pinhole leak wastes water and destroys your reputation. Pressure-test every tank before delivery.</p>

<p>Business notes: tanks are a volume product, so jigs and templates pay for themselves quickly. Offer a standard range of sizes with published prices, plus custom sizes at a premium. In dry regions, a tank is an essential purchase, not a luxury, which makes demand more resilient than gate or signage demand.</p>

<h3>Line 4: Truck Bodies and Trailers</h3>
<p>Aluminium truck bodies and trailers are a high-value line. The weight saving over steel improves fuel economy and payload, which fleet owners understand. Typical work includes flatbed bodies, box bodies, tipper bodies, fuel tankers, and livestock trailers. This is project work: it requires drawings, jigs, lifting equipment, and often a second welder.</p>

<p>Business notes: fleet contracts are the prize. A single transport company can order ten bodies a year, plus repairs. Price by weight of metal plus fabrication hours plus a project margin. Always agree on axle ratings, load capacity, and inspection requirements in writing before cutting metal.</p>

<h3>Line 5: Signs and Structures</h3>
<p>Aluminium signage and light structures billboard frames, shopfronts, canopies, stair rails, and display stands are steady, visible work. Signage is often repeat business because businesses rebrand, expand, and repair. Aluminium is preferred for outdoor signs because it does not rust and takes paint or vinyl well.</p>

<p>Business notes: signage has short lead times and high margin per kilogram. Structures such as canopies and railings are more engineering-heavy and may need load calculations. Keep a portfolio of photographs; in this line, a visible finished sign is your best advertisement.</p>

<h3>Line 6: Repair Work The Steady Income</h3>
<p>Repair work is the base of the pyramid. It pays daily wages, uses small amounts of material, and generates customer relationships that lead to product sales. Typical repairs include cracked frames, broken gate hinges, leaking tanks, damaged truck bodies, bent sign frames, and boat hull patches. Repair customers often become product customers.</p>

<p>Business notes: price repairs by a minimum call-out fee plus material plus labour, not by guesswork. Keep a repair log with customer names, phone numbers, and the work done. That log is a ready-made marketing list for your next product promotion.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828;background:#ffebee;border-radius:8px;padding:14px 18px;margin:20px 0">
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;font-size:1.05em"> SAFETY WARNING Aluminium Welding Hazards</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#7f0000">Aluminium welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation, ozone, and fine metal fume. Always wear a properly rated auto-darkening welding helmet, flame-resistant clothing, leather gloves, and safety boots. Weld in a well-ventilated area or use fume extraction never weld in a closed room. Aluminium dust from grinding and cutting is combustible; keep it away from sparks and clean it up daily. Never weld a sealed tank, drum, or container that has held fuel, chemicals, or unknown liquids residual vapour can explode. Purge, fill with water, or have the container professionally cleaned and certified before any hot work. Cylinders must be secured upright, and acetylene must never be used at pressures above 15 psi. In boats and tanks, ensure adequate ventilation and a fire watch during and after welding.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Quote in writing.</strong> Every job repair or product gets a written spec, price, deposit, and delivery date before any metal is cut.</li>
<li><strong>Take a 5070% deposit</strong> on product and project work to buy materials; never fund a customer's project from your own cash.</li>
<li><strong>Match the alloy to the job.</strong> Use marine-grade alloy for boats, food-grade or appropriate alloy for tanks, and standard structural alloy for gates and frames.</li>
<li><strong>Use jigs and templates.</strong> They cut labour time, improve repeatability, and let you train helpers faster.</li>
<li><strong>Test before delivery.</strong> Pressure-test tanks, leak-test hulls, and load-check trailers and truck bodies.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a repair log.</strong> Names, numbers, and jobs done become your product sales list.</li>
<li><strong>Control fume and fire.</strong> Ventilate, extract, and never weld sealed or fuel-contaminated containers.</li>
<li><strong>Reinvest in one capability at a time.</strong> Add a bender, a jig, or a second welder only when a product line is consistently profitable.</li>
</ol>
