Lessons

1. Handle Materials: Wood, Bone, Antler, and Synthetics

Choosing the right handle material depends on what's available locally and the knife's purpose. In Africa, you have excellent options: hardwoods like ebony, mahogany, and African blackwood (Dalbergia melanoxylon) are dense and take a high polish. Bone from cattle or camel shin bones is commonboil and degrease thoroughly, then cut into scales. Antler from kudu, impala, or warthog makes beautiful handles; use the solid outer part, not the spongy core. Synthetics like Micarta (fabric or canvas soaked in resin) and G10 (fiberglass laminate) are durable, waterproof, and available from knife supply shops or online. For a beginner, start with hardwood or Micarta. Avoid softwoods like pinethey dent easily. Stabilized wood (vacuum-impregnated with resin) is best but costs more. If using bone or antler, wear a respirator when sanding; dust is harmful. Always cut scales slightly oversized: 10 mm thick, 40 mm wide, 120 mm long for a typical knife. For hidden tang, you need a block, not scales. Test material by dropping it on concretegood handle material shouldn't crack. In rural areas, look for dry, seasoned wood from fallen trees; avoid green wood that will shrink and crack. Store materials in a dry place. Remember, the handle is what you hold, so comfort and grip matter more than looks.

2. Designing Handle Shape and Ergonomics

A good handle fits your hand like a handshake. Start by tracing your hand on paper: mark the palm, fingers, and thumb. The handle should be 100120 mm long for an average adult, with a circumference of 90110 mm. For a working knife, avoid symmetrical shapesadd a palm swell (a slight bulge in the middle) and a finger guard or choil. The front of the handle (near the blade) should be narrower than the back to prevent your hand sliding forward. A slight taper toward the butt helps with control. Test the shape by carving a prototype from softwood or even soap. Hold it in multiple grips: hammer grip (for chopping), pinch grip (for detail work), and reverse grip. Mark high spots and sand them down. For African bushcraft knives, a drop-point blade with a straight handle works well. For skinning, a curved handle with a deep finger groove helps. Keep the handle thickness between 2025 mm at the thickest point. If you have large hands, add 5 mm. For small hands, subtract 5 mm. The butt can be rounded or flat; a flat butt with a lanyard hole (6 mm diameter) is practical. Don't forget a thumb ramp or jimping (small notches) near the blade for grip. Draw your design full-size, then transfer to your material. Remember: ergonomics is personalwhat works for you may not work for others, so test before final shaping.

3. Attaching Scales: Epoxy and Pins

For full-tang knives, scales are attached with epoxy and pins. Start by preparing the tang: clean it with acetone or alcohol, then rough it with 80-grit sandpaper for better adhesion. Drill pin holes in the tangtypically two or three 4 mm holes, spaced evenly. Use a drill press or hand drill with a carbide bit; clamp the tang securely. For the scales, cut two pieces slightly larger than the tang. Clamp one scale to the tang, drill through the existing holes into the scale, then repeat for the other scale. Insert pins (brass, copper, or stainless steel rods) through the holes to align. Mix two-part epoxy (like Devcon 2-Ton or Loctite Hysol) thoroughly for 2 minutes. Apply a thin layer to both the tang and the inside of each scale. Don't use too muchexcess squeezes out. Place the scales on the tang, insert the pins, and clamp with spring clamps or a vise. Wipe off excess epoxy with acetone before it cures. Let cure for 24 hours at room temperature (or 1 hour at 60C). For a stronger bond, peen the pins: cut them slightly longer than the handle, then hammer the ends to mushroom them. This mechanically locks the scales. After curing, sand the pins flush with the scales. If using Corby bolts (screwed rivets), follow the same process but tighten with a screwdriver. For hidden tang, see Lesson 5. Always wear gloves and work in a ventilated areaepoxy fumes are toxic.

4. Shaping and Finishing Handles

After the epoxy cures, shape the handle to its final form. Use a belt grinder with a 60-grit belt to remove bulk, then 120-grit to refine. If you don't have a grinder, use a farrier's rasp (wood rasp) and files. Work slowlycheck symmetry by eye and by feel. For a palm swell, mark the center and remove material from the top and bottom. Keep the handle cool by dipping it in water frequently; overheating can weaken epoxy. Once the shape is right, move to hand sanding: start at 120-grit, then 220, 400, 800, and up to 1500 for a polished finish. Sand with the grain, not across it. For bone or antler, sand up to 2000 and buff with white compound. For wood, raise the grain by wiping with a damp cloth between grits, then sand again. Apply a finish: boiled linseed oil (BLO) for woodapply thin coats, let dry 24 hours, repeat 3 times. For a more durable finish, use tung oil or polyurethane. For Micarta or G10, no finish is needed; just sand to 600-grit for grip. For bone, seal with cyanoacrylate (super glue) to prevent cracking. After finishing, wax the handle with beeswax or carnauba wax for water resistance. Test the handle for comfort: it should feel smooth but not slippery. If it's too smooth, leave it at 400-grit. Finally, clean the blade and apply a protective oil. A well-finished handle makes the knife look professional and last for years.

5. Hidden Tang Construction: Drilling and Assembly

Hidden tang knives have a tang that goes into a hole drilled in the handle block. This is common for stick tang knives like Puukko or Bowie. Start by drilling a hole in the handle block that matches the tang's width and thickness. Use a drill press with a brad-point bit or Forstner bit. For a 4 mm thick tang, drill a 5 mm hole. Measure the tang length and drill to a depth 5 mm longer than the tang. If the tang is wider than the drill bit, drill multiple holes and clean out with a chisel or broach. For a curved tang, you may need to burn the tang in: heat the tang with a torch until red-hot, then push it into the handle block (wood only) to char a perfect fit. This is traditional and strong. For assembly, mix epoxy and coat the tang and the inside of the hole. Insert the tang, then tap the butt of the handle on a hard surface to settle it. Wipe excess epoxy. For extra security, cross-drill a 3 mm hole through the handle and tang near the front and back, then insert brass pins. Let cure 24 hours. After curing, shape the handle as in Lesson 4. If the handle block splits, you can repair with epoxy and sawdust. For a metal ferrule or bolster, slide it over the tang before inserting into the handle. Always test fit before gluingonce epoxy sets, it's permanent. In Africa, many traditional knives use hidden tang with bone or hardwood handles, often with a leather washer at the front. That works well too.

6. Securing and Finishing the Handle

Once the handle is shaped and finished, secure it to prevent loosening. For full tang, peen the pins: cut pins 2 mm longer than the handle, then hammer the ends with a ball-peen hammer to mushroom them. File flush. For hidden tang, you can add a butt cap or pommel. A simple method: drill a hole in a piece of brass or antler, epoxy it onto the tang end, then peen the tang over it. Another way is to thread the tang end and screw on a nut. For a traditional African look, wrap the handle with leather or cord. Leather washers: cut circles from 3 mm thick leather, stack them on the tang, compress with a vise, and soak in epoxy. This gives a superb grip. For cord wrap, use paracord or leather cord, wrap tightly, and seal with epoxy or shellac. After securing, do a final sanding and finishing. Check for any gaps between handle and bladefill with epoxy mixed with sawdust. For a waterproof seal, dip the handle in boiled linseed oil for 10 minutes, then wipe dry. For bone or antler, apply a coat of clear lacquer. Test the knife by chopping into a piece of hardwoodthe handle should not move. If it does, re-epoxy. Finally, sharpen the blade and make a sheath. A well-secured handle is safe and durable. Remember, the handle is the interface between you and the blade; it must be strong. Keep your tools clean and your workspace organized. Practice on scrap materials first.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Handle Construction for African Bladesmiths

In African bladesmithing, the handle is not an afterthought it is the point of contact between the maker's craft and the user's livelihood. Whether you are fitting a machete for a farmer in Kisumu, a hunting knife for a professional hunter in Namibia, or a chef's knife for a hotel kitchen in Kigali, the handle determines control, safety, repeat sales, and word-of-mouth. This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into a single working document: materials selection, ergonomic design, scale attachment, shaping and finishing, hidden-tang drilling and assembly, and final securing. Treat it as a bench-side standard operating procedure, not a theory chapter.

1. Material Selection: Matching the Handle to the Market

Material choice is a business decision before it is an aesthetic one. A handle that swells, cracks, or delaminates in the customer's hand costs you the sale, the return, and the referral. In Africa, the dominant stressors are heat, humidity, dust, and unpredictable impact not the controlled conditions of a European display cabinet.

Wood. Indigenous hardwoods such as mukwa (Pterocarpus angolensis), mopane (Colophospermum mopane), ebony (Diospyros spp.), and olive (Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata) offer excellent density, natural oils, and local availability. Mukwa and mopane are particularly valued in southern Africa for stability and figure. Always dry to 810% moisture content before shaping; green wood will move after assembly.

Bone. Cattle bone from abattoirs in Botswana, Kenya, and South Africa is cheap, abundant, and takes a high polish. It is brittle under lateral impact and must be stabilised or at least thoroughly degreased. It suits display knives and traditional ceremonial pieces more than heavy-use field blades.

Antler. Kudu, impala, and warthog antler/horn are iconic African materials. They are dense, grippy, and culturally resonant a strong premium signal. The challenge is the porous core: it must be filled with epoxy or stabilised resin, and the solid outer layer must be oriented to the user's grip.

Synthetics. G10, Micarta, and stabilised wood composites are the workhorses of professional African makers serving export and tactical markets. They are dimensionally stable, waterproof, and consistent critical when you are producing batches for lodges, safari operators, or export buyers who need identical units.

2. Designing Handle Shape and Ergonomics

Ergonomics is not a luxury it is a repeat-sales strategy. A handle that fits the hand reduces fatigue, improves control, and lowers the risk of the user's hand slipping onto the edge. Design for the actual user: a farmer gripping a machete for six hours, a butcher working wet and bloody, a chef doing precision cuts, a tourist holding a knife for a photograph.

Key principles:

  • Palm swell: The handle should fill the palm without forcing the fingers open. A subtle swell at the centre and a taper toward the rear prevents the hand sliding forward.
  • Finger guard or index groove: For working knives, a front finger groove or a slight guard protects the hand during thrusting and chopping.
  • Contour, not cylinder: A perfectly round handle rotates in the hand. An oval or egg-shaped cross-section gives directional control.
  • Length: For a full-hand grip, 110125 mm is a practical working range for most adult hands; adjust for the target market.
  • Texture: Checkering, grooves, or a matte finish improve grip when wet or bloody. High-gloss finishes look good in photographs and fail in the field.

3. Attaching Scales: Epoxy and Pins

Scale attachment is where most handle failures originate. The two-part system epoxy plus mechanical pins is standard because epoxy alone is not a structural fastener.

  • Surface preparation: Degrease the tang and scale mating faces with acetone or alcohol. Rough the tang with 80120 grit to give the epoxy a mechanical key.
  • Epoxy selection: Use a slow-cure (24-hour) structural epoxy such as a quality two-part marine or knife-maker grade. Fast-cure five-minute epoxies are weaker and shrink more.
  • Pin selection: Brass, stainless steel, copper, or mosaic pins. Pin diameter should be roughly one-third the scale thickness. Two pins minimum for a fixed handle; three for larger knives.
  • Pin placement: Place pins in the widest, flattest areas of the handle front and rear avoiding the thin edges where the scale tapers.
  • Clamping: Apply even pressure with clamps or a vise; avoid over-clamping, which starves the joint of epoxy.

SAFETY WARNING Dust and Fumes

Sanding bone, antler, G10, Micarta, and stabilised wood produces fine dust that is hazardous to lungs and eyes. G10 and Micarta contain glass fibre and resin; bone and antler dust can carry biological contaminants. Always wear a fitted P2/N95 respirator, safety glasses, and nitrile gloves. Work with dust extraction or in a well-ventilated area never in a closed room. Epoxy hardeners are sensitizers; avoid skin contact and work with gloves. In many African workshops, open-sided sheds with cross-ventilation are the practical minimum. If you feel dizziness, skin irritation, or breathing discomfort, stop work immediately and move to fresh air.

4. Shaping and Finishing Handles

Shaping is where the handle becomes a product. Work from coarse to fine, and check the fit in the hand at every stage not just at the end.

  1. Rough shaping: Use a belt grinder, rasp, or file to bring the scales close to the tang profile. Keep the tang visible as a centreline reference.
  2. Contour shaping: Create the palm swell, index groove, and rear taper. Remove material evenly from both sides to avoid twisting the handle.
  3. Blending: Blend the scale to the tang and bolster so there are no sharp steps or gaps where moisture and dirt can collect.
  4. Sanding: Progress through 120, 220, 400, 600, and up to 1200 grit for a polished finish. For working knives, stop at 400600 for grip.
  5. Sealing: Apply a penetrating oil (linseed, tung, or commercial gunstock oil) for wood; use a sanding slurry to fill pores. For synthetics, a light buff is sufficient.
  6. Final inspection: Check for gaps at the tang, pin flushness, symmetry, and comfort in a wet-hand grip test.

5. Hidden Tang Construction: Drilling and Assembly

Hidden tang (stick tang) construction is common in African traditional knives, daggers, and many hunting knives. It reduces weight, allows sculpted handles, and is strong when executed correctly. The weakness is always the transition between tang and handle so the drilling and assembly must be precise.

  • Tang preparation: Taper the tang slightly toward the rear and cut shallow notches or a threaded section for mechanical keying.
  • Block drilling: Drill a hole slightly larger than the tang through the handle block. Use a drill press or a guide block for alignment. For curved handles, drill in stages or use a broach.
  • Dry fit: Insert the tang and check alignment, depth, and handle-to-guard fit before any epoxy.
  • Epoxy assembly: Fill the hole with slow-cure epoxy, coat the tang, and insert with a twisting motion to distribute glue and avoid air pockets.
  • Alignment: Check the blade-to-handle angle immediately and clamp or prop in position until cured.

6. Securing and Finishing the Handle

The final stage determines whether the knife survives its first season of real use.

  • Pin peening or flush finishing: For through-pins, peen the ends or file flush and polish. For hidden tangs, a rear pin or threaded pommel nut adds mechanical security.
  • Gap filling: Any gap at the guard, bolster, or tang is a failure point. Fill with epoxy mixed with wood dust or use a thin CA glue wicking method.
  • Final finish: Re-sand and polish the handle after assembly. Seal wood with oil or wax; buff synthetics.
  • Edge protection: Fit a sheath or blade guard before delivery. A finished knife without a sheath is a liability for the customer and a lost upsell for you.
  • Quality check: Test for handle movement, pin tightness, surface defects, and comfort. A knife that passes your own hand test will pass the customer's.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Moisture-check wood to 810% before shaping; degrease bone and antler.
  2. Design the handle around the actual user's grip, not the display stand.
  3. Use slow-cure structural epoxy plus at least two mechanical pins.
  4. Rough the tang and scale faces for epoxy keying; degrease before glue-up.
  5. Wear P2/N95 respirator, eye protection, and gloves when sanding bone, antler, G10, or Micarta.
  6. Dry-fit hidden tangs before epoxy; check alignment immediately after assembly.
  7. Progress sanding from 120 to 4001200 grit depending on market; seal wood, buff synthetics.
  8. Final test: no gaps, no movement, comfortable wet-hand grip, sheath fitted.
