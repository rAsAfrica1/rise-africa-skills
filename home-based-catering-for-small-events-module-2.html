Lessons

1. Understanding Client Preferences

Start every catering job by asking the client 10 key questions. Write them in a notebook or phone note. Ask: How many guests? Any allergies or religious restrictions? What is your budget per person? Do you want a buffet or plated service? What drinks? Any favourite dishes from childhood? What time is the event? Where will you serve? Do you need serving staff? What is the main goal: impress, comfort, or celebrate? For example, a client in Nairobi might say, No pork, but beef and chicken are fine. My grandmother loves pilau. That tells you to include a beef pilau or chicken biryani. In Lagos, a client might request jollof rice with grilled tilapia and no pepper for the children. Write down every detail. Then repeat it back to the client: So you want 50 guests, no nuts, mild spice, and you love chapati and stew. This prevents disasters. Also ask about equipment: Do they have a gas cooker, pots, or a jiko? If not, you must bring your own. For a small event of 20 people, you need at least two large pots, a serving spoon, and a reliable heat source. If the client wants a traditional dish like ugali and sukuma wiki, confirm the exact texture and salt level. Always ask for a deposit of 50% before buying ingredients. This shows commitment. Finally, note the clients phone number and send a written summary via WhatsApp. That becomes your contract. Good listening saves you from cooking the wrong food and losing money.

2. Creating Balanced Menus

A balanced menu has five parts: a starch, a protein, a vegetable, a sauce or stew, and a fruit or dessert. For 30 guests, plan 3 starches, 2 proteins, 2 vegetables, and 1 dessert. Example: rice, chapati, and ugali as starches; beef stew and grilled chicken as proteins; cabbage and spinach as vegetables; and a tomato-onion sauce. Add sliced watermelon or banana for dessert. In West Africa, use jollof rice, fried plantain, and eba as starches; chicken and fish as proteins; and efo riro (spinach stew) as vegetable. Always include one mild dish for children and one spicy dish for adults. Use the plate method: half the plate vegetables and fruits, a quarter starch, a quarter protein. For a buffet, place starches first, then proteins, then vegetables. This encourages guests to take more vegetables. Avoid all-white menus (rice, pasta, bread) because they lack colour and nutrients. Add orange sweet potato, green sukuma wiki, red tomatoes, and yellow maize. For a 3-hour event, serve water, juice, and tea. If the client has diabetics, use brown rice and reduce sugar in desserts. For vegetarians, replace meat with beans, lentils, or eggs. A balanced menu costs about 20% more than a meat-heavy one, but it satisfies more guests. Write your menu on a single page with dish names and key ingredients. Show it to the client for approval before shopping.

3. Scaling Recipes for Groups

Scaling means multiplying a recipe for more people. First, know your base recipe for 10 people. For example, a beef pilau for 10 uses 1 kg beef, 1 kg rice, 2 large onions, 4 tomatoes, 2 tablespoons pilau masala, and 4 cups water. To serve 50, multiply by 5: 5 kg beef, 5 kg rice, 10 onions, 20 tomatoes, 10 tablespoons masala, and 20 cups water. But not everything multiplies exactly. Spices and salt should be increased by 4.5 times, not 5, because too much salt ruins the dish. Oil can be increased by 4 times. Water for rice: use 2 cups water per 1 cup rice, but for large pots, reduce to 1.75 cups per cup because less evaporation occurs. Cooking time increases by about 25% for every doubling of quantity. So if 10-person pilau takes 30 minutes, 50-person takes about 45 minutes. Use a kitchen scale for accuracy. For 100 guests, cook in batches to avoid burning. Never fill a pot more than two-thirds full. For chapati, a recipe for 20 uses 1 kg flour, 2 cups water, 1 teaspoon salt, and 4 tablespoons oil. For 60, use 3 kg flour, 6 cups water, 3 teaspoons salt, and 12 tablespoons oil. But kneading 3 kg by hand is hard; use a mixer or hire help. Always test a small batch first. Write your scaled recipe on a card and tape it to the wall. This prevents mistakes when you are tired.

4. Seasonal and Local Ingredients

Buying seasonal and local ingredients saves money and improves taste. In East Africa, mangoes are cheapest in December to February. Use them for chutney, salsa, or juice. In June to August, oranges and avocados are plentiful. In West Africa, tomatoes are cheapest in the dry season (November to March). Buy in bulk and sun-dry or freeze them. Local ingredients like cassava, plantain, millet, sorghum, and amaranth are cheaper than imported rice or wheat. For a wedding in Uganda, use matooke (green banana) instead of potatoes. In Kenya, use sukuma wiki (collard greens) instead of spinach. In Nigeria, use ugu (fluted pumpkin leaves) instead of kale. Visit your local market early in the morning for the best prices. Build relationships with farmers. For example, buy 10 kg of tomatoes directly from a farmer for 20% less than retail. Use a cooler box with ice to keep vegetables fresh for 2 days. If a client wants strawberries in July, tell them they are out of season and suggest mango or pineapple instead. Seasonal menus are also more sustainable. For a 50-person event, a seasonal menu can cost 30% less than one with imported ingredients. Plan your menu around what is abundant. That week, if cabbages are cheap, make cabbage stew and coleslaw. Write a list of what is in season each month. Share it with your client as a selling point.

5. Costing and Pricing Menus

To cost a menu, list every ingredient with its market price. For 30 guests, a simple menu of rice, beef stew, cabbage, and chapati might need: 3 kg rice (300 KES), 2 kg beef (800 KES), 1 cabbage (50 KES), 2 kg flour (120 KES), onions, tomatoes, oil, spices (200 KES). Total food cost = 1,470 KES. Add 10% for gas or charcoal (147 KES). Add 15% for transport (220 KES). Add 20% for your labour (294 KES). Total cost = 2,131 KES. That is 71 KES per person. Now add profit: 30% profit = 639 KES. Final price = 2,770 KES, or 92 KES per person. Round up to 100 KES per person for simplicity. For a 50-person event, you might charge 120 KES per person because of larger scale and more serving time. Always include disposable plates, cups, and napkins if the client wants them. Those cost about 20 KES per person. Never guess prices. Keep a notebook of current market prices. Update it weekly. If a client says your price is too high, offer a cheaper menu: replace beef with chicken or beans. Show them the cost breakdown. That builds trust. For a deposit, ask for 50% upfront. The remaining 50% on delivery. Never buy ingredients before receiving the deposit. This protects you from loss.

6. Recipe Testing and Standardization

Standardization means writing your recipe so anyone can cook it the same way every time. Start by testing each dish three times. For example, test your chicken curry. First time, use 1 teaspoon curry powder. Second time, 1.5 teaspoons. Third time, 2 teaspoons. Ask three people to taste each and tell you which is best. Suppose 1.5 teaspoons wins. Write that down. Your standard recipe for 10 people: 1 kg chicken, 2 onions, 3 tomatoes, 1.5 teaspoons curry powder, 1 teaspoon salt, 2 tablespoons oil, 2 cups water. Cook for 25 minutes. Now you have a fixed recipe. Use a digital scale for all dry ingredients and a measuring jug for liquids. Write the recipe on a card with the date. When you scale for 50, multiply by 5 but adjust salt to 4 teaspoons (not 5). Test the scaled version once. For baking, standardization is critical. A cake recipe for 20 uses 500g flour, 400g sugar, 250g butter, 4 eggs, 2 teaspoons baking powder. Test at 180C for 40 minutes. If you change the oven, test again. Keep a logbook of every test: date, ingredients, cooking time, taste score (1-5). After three successful tests, your recipe is standard. Train your assistant to follow the card exactly. This ensures consistency. Clients will return if your food tastes the same every time. Also, standardize your plating: use the same size spoon for each serving. For 30 guests, each plate gets 1 cup rice, 2 pieces beef, and 1/2 cup cabbage. That controls portions and costs.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Menu Planning and Recipe Development for Home-Based Catering

This practical reference is designed for home-based caterers in Africa who serve small eventsweddings, birthdays, corporate lunches, church gatherings, and community functions. It consolidates the six core lessons of this module into a single, business-first resource you can print, pin to your kitchen wall, and use every time you quote a client or plan a menu. The goal is simple: help you deliver consistent, profitable, and culturally appropriate food that keeps clients coming back.

1. Understanding Client Preferences

Every catering job begins with a conversation, not a menu. In the African context, client preferences are shaped by region, religion, ethnicity, and the occasion itself. A wedding in Lagos may require jollof rice, pounded yam, and asun, while a church function in Nairobi may expect pilau, chapati, and sukuma wiki. Your job is to ask the right questions before you quote.

Key questions to ask every client:

  • What is the occasion, date, and venue?
  • How many guests, and are there children or elderly attendees?
  • Are there dietary restrictions (halal, vegetarian, allergies, no pork)?
  • What dishes are non-negotiable? What is flexible?
  • What is your budget per head, and what is the service style (buffet, plated, family-style)?
  • Do you have a preferred caterer or a family recipe you want replicated?

Document these answers in a simple client brief. This protects you from disputes and helps you design a menu that feels personal rather than generic.

2. Creating Balanced Menus

A balanced menu is not just about nutritionit is about variety in colour, texture, temperature, and preparation method. For small events, aim for a structure that covers starch, protein, vegetable, sauce, and a small sweet or fruit. In many African cuisines, the starch (rice, ugali, fufu, matoke) anchors the plate, but a skilled caterer balances heavy and light dishes so guests do not feel overfed.

Use the "rule of thirds" for buffet lines: one-third starches, one-third proteins, one-third vegetables and salads. Always include at least one vegetarian main and one dish that is mild for children or guests who cannot handle pepper.

3. Scaling Recipes for Groups

Scaling is where many home caterers lose money or quality. Doubling a recipe rarely means simply doubling every ingredient. Salt, spices, and leavening agents do not scale linearly. A recipe for 10 that becomes a recipe for 100 needs careful adjustment.

Scaling rules of thumb:

  • Salt and strong spices: start with 75% of the linear increase, then taste and adjust.
  • Leavening agents (baking powder, yeast): use 8090% of the linear increase.
  • Liquids: reduce by 1015% when scaling up, as evaporation is lower in large pots.
  • Cooking time: increase by 2550%, not double, and stir more frequently.
  • Equipment: a 100-litre pot behaves differently from a 10-litre pot. Use a heavy base to prevent burning.

4. Seasonal and Local Ingredients

Africa's markets are rich with seasonal produce. Buying locally and in season reduces cost, improves freshness, and supports your community. In West Africa, tomatoes are cheapest and most flavourful from December to March. In East Africa, mangoes peak from November to February, and avocados from March to September. In Southern Africa, maize and pumpkin are abundant in autumn.

Build your menu around what is abundant. If tomatoes are expensive, use more onions, peppers, and local greens. If fish is scarce, pivot to beans, lentils, or chicken. Seasonal menus also give you a marketing advantage: "Made with fresh, local, in-season ingredients" resonates with clients who value authenticity.

Practical tips:

  • Visit your local market 23 times before the event to track prices and quality.
  • Build relationships with 23 reliable suppliers for bulk staples (rice, oil, flour).
  • Preserve seasonal produce when cheapdry, freeze, or pickleto use later.
  • Include at least one dish that showcases a local, seasonal ingredient as a signature.

5. Costing and Pricing Menus

Costing is the difference between a hobby and a business. Every menu item must be costed to the gram, then priced to cover ingredients, labour, transport, gas, packaging, and profit. A common mistake is to price based on what competitors charge without knowing your own costs.

Step-by-step costing method:

  1. List every ingredient and its quantity per serving.
  2. Divide the purchase price by the usable quantity to get cost per gram or millilitre.
  3. Multiply by the quantity used to get ingredient cost per serving.
  4. Add a percentage for gas, electricity, water, and transport (typically 1015%).
  5. Add labour: decide an hourly rate for yourself and your helpers.
  6. Add packaging and rental costs (chafing dishes, plates, cutlery).
  7. Add your profit marginaim for 2540% on small events.
  8. Divide total by number of guests to get price per head.

Example: If a jollof rice dish costs 350 NGN per serving in ingredients, plus 50 NGN overhead, 100 NGN labour, and 50 NGN packaging, your cost is 550 NGN. At a 30% margin, you charge 715 NGN per serving. For 100 guests, that is 71,500 NGN for that dish alone.

SAFETY WARNING: Food Safety and Hygiene

Home-based caterers must comply with local food safety regulations. Always:

  • Wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds before handling food.
  • Keep raw and cooked foods separate to prevent cross-contamination.
  • Cook poultry, meat, and eggs to safe internal temperatures (75C/167F).
  • Refrigerate perishables below 5C and reheat to 75C.
  • Never leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours.
  • Use clean, covered containers for transport and serving.
  • Check with your local public health authority for licensing requirements.

Source: World Health Organization, Food Safety Fact Sheet (WHO, 2024).

6. Recipe Testing and Standardization

Standardized recipes are your business insurance. They ensure that every client gets the same taste, portion, and qualitywhether you cook it yourself or a helper does. A standardized recipe includes: exact ingredient quantities, preparation steps, cooking time and temperature, portion size, yield, and plating instructions.

How to standardize a recipe:

  1. Cook the dish three times, measuring everything precisely.
  2. Record the exact quantities, times, and temperatures.
  3. Test with a small group and gather feedback on taste, texture, and portion.
  4. Adjust and re-test until consistent.
  5. Write the final recipe in a clear, step-by-step format.
  6. Train your helpers on the standardized recipe and keep a copy in the kitchen.
  7. Review and update seasonally or when supplier quality changes.

Standardization also makes costing easier, reduces waste, and allows you to delegate cooking without losing quality. It is the foundation of a scalable catering business.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Client brief completed: occasion, guest count, dietary needs, budget, and non-negotiables documented.
  2. Balanced menu designed: starch, protein, vegetable, sauce, and dessert represented; vegetarian and mild options included.
  3. Recipes scaled correctly: salt, spices, leavening, and liquids adjusted; cooking time and equipment considered.
  4. Seasonal and local ingredients prioritized: market prices checked; suppliers confirmed; preservation plan in place.
  5. Menu fully costed: ingredients, overhead, labour, packaging, and profit margin calculated per head.
  6. Food safety plan in place: hygiene, temperature control, and safe transport confirmed.
  7. Recipes standardized and tested: written, portioned, and trained to helpers.
  8. Client quote and contract signed: deposit received, terms clear, and event logistics confirmed.
