Packaging, Labelling & Compliance

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Food-grade packaging materials

Not all packaging is safe for food. Packaging must be food-grade — approved to touch food without releasing chemicals.

Food-grade materials:

  • Glass — inert, best for sauces, jams, oils
  • Food-grade PET/PP plastic — bottles and jars
  • Foil-laminated pouches — for dried products
  • Lacquered tin cans — for canned goods

Never use: industrial plastic bags, non-food paint, recycled containers from other products. They leach chemicals and can cause food poisoning.

⚠ Always ask your supplier for a food-grade certificate. It should say "FDA approved" or "EU 10/2011 compliant".

2 Glass vs plastic vs pouches

Each material has strengths and weaknesses.

Glass: Premium look, fully recyclable, inert. Heavy, breakable, expensive to ship.

Plastic (PET/PP): Light, cheap, doesn't break. Feels "cheap" to some buyers, less premium look.

Foil pouches: Light, airtight, ideal for dried goods. Not resealable unless zipped. Can look premium if designed well.

Tin cans: Excellent barrier, stackable, traditional. Expensive equipment to seal.

Choose based on product: glass for sauces and jams, pouches for dry snacks and powders, plastic for juices and liquids.

3 Shelf life and barrier properties

Packaging slows down spoilage. The barrier determines shelf life.

Oxygen barrier: Prevents oxidation (rancidity in oils, browning in fruit).

Moisture barrier: Prevents dried products absorbing water (sogginess, mould).

Light barrier: Prevents nutrient loss and colour fade (oils, powders, dairy).

Best barriers: Glass (all-around), aluminium foil (excellent), metallised films (good), clear plastic (poor).

Foil-laminated pouches with a clear plastic window are the compromise — you see the product but the barrier still works.

4 Mandatory label elements

Most countries require the same core label elements. Check local rules but always include:

  • Product name — clear and truthful
  • Net weight/volume — accurate to 1 g/ml
  • Ingredients list — in descending order by weight
  • Allergen statement — bold, prominent
  • Best-before or expiry date
  • Batch/lot code — for traceability
  • Producer name and contact
  • Country of origin

Missing elements can get your product seized and your business fined.

5 Ingredient and allergen lists

Ingredients: List every ingredient, in order from largest to smallest by weight. If your tomato sauce is 70% tomato, list "Tomatoes (70%)" first.

Allergens: The top 8 allergens must be declared:

  • Peanuts
  • Tree nuts (almonds, cashews, etc.)
  • Milk
  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Crustaceans (shrimp, crab)
  • Soy
  • Wheat/gluten

Plus sesame in some countries. Bold them or put them in a "Contains" statement. Don't hide them — allergies kill.

6 Best-before and batch codes

Best-before: Quality date, not safety date. Product is still safe after but may lose flavour or texture.

Use-by / expiry: Safety date. Product must not be sold or eaten after.

Batch code: Every production run gets a unique code (e.g. 20261005-A). This lets you trace any problem back to a specific day and batch.

If you get a complaint, the batch code lets you check the whole batch — not just one jar. Essential for recalls.

7 Barcodes and SKUs

Barcode: A unique number for your product. Required by supermarkets. Get one from GS1 (global standards body) or a national authority. Cost: $50–$500 one-off plus annual fees.

SKU (Stock Keeping Unit): Your internal code for each product variant (size, flavour, packaging). E.g. PB-500-SMOOTH = peanut butter 500g smooth.

Barcodes let supermarkets scan, track, and reorder your products automatically. Without one, you can't get into most retail chains.

8 Country-specific food rules

Each country has different rules. Check before you export or launch.

Zimbabwe: SAZ (Standards Association of Zimbabwe) for food standards; local council for premises; ZIMRA for tax.

Nigeria: NAFDAC registration required for packaged food. Product registration number on the label.

Kenya: KEBS (Kenya Bureau of Standards) permits. Import standards alignment.

UK/EU: Food Standards Agency + local council. Nutrition declaration required. FIC regulations for labelling.

USA: FDA registration. Nutrition Facts panel required. FSMA compliance for larger producers.

💰 Good packaging and compliance opens doors. Bad packaging closes them — supermarkets won't stock unlabelled products, and one complaint can end your business.
