1. Understanding Seed Varieties

A seed variety is a group of plants with the same characteristics, like fruit size, taste, or days to maturity. When you buy seeds, you will see two main types: hybrid and open-pollinated. Hybrid seeds (often labeled F1) are made by crossing two parent lines. They grow strong and uniform, but you cannot save their seeds for next seasonthe next generation will be mixed and often poor. Open-pollinated (OP) varieties, including heirloom types, produce seeds that stay true to type if you save them carefully. For example, 'Kilele F1' tomato is a hybrid with high yield and disease resistance, while 'Money Maker' is open-pollinated and can be saved.

Another key difference is days to maturity. A variety like 'Padma' tomato matures in 75 days, while 'Tengeru' takes 90 days. Choose based on your rainy season length. If rains last only 3 months, pick early maturing varieties. Also consider disease resistance. In East Africa, late blight is a big problem for tomatoes. Varieties like 'Meru' and 'Tumaini' have some resistance. For maize, 'DK8031' is drought-tolerant, while 'Pioneer 30G19' is high-yielding but needs more water.

You should also think about market demand. If local buyers want large onions, plant 'Red Creole' or 'Bombay Red'. If they prefer small, pungent ones, grow 'Kitale'. For leafy greens like kale, 'Sukuma Wiki' is common, but 'Thousand Headed' gives more leaves per plant. Always read the seed packet or ask the seller for the variety name, maturity days, and any special notes. Avoid buying seeds without a labelyou might get a wrong variety that fails.

Finally, test a small plot before planting a whole field. Sow a few seeds of a new variety and see how it performs on your soil and climate. Keep records: planting date, germination rate, pest problems, and yield. After two seasons, you will know which varieties work best for you. Remember, a good variety is the foundation of a healthy nursery and a profitable farm.

2. Choosing the Right Seeds for Your Region

Africa has many agro-ecological zones: highlands, dry lowlands, humid forests, and coastal areas. A variety that thrives in one zone may fail in another. Start by knowing your altitude and rainfall. For example, in the highlands above 1,500 meters, you can grow cool-season crops like cabbage, carrots, and potatoes. In lowlands below 1,000 meters, focus on heat-tolerant crops like okra, amaranth, and cowpeas. If you are in a dry area with less than 500 mm of rain per year, choose drought-tolerant varieties like 'Katumani' maize or 'Nerica' rice.

Next, check the length of your growing season. In many parts of Africa, rains come in two seasons. If you have a short rainy season (3-4 months), plant quick-maturing varieties. For beans, 'Rosecoco' takes 90 days, but 'KAT B1' matures in 70 days. For tomatoes, 'Roma VF' takes 75 days, while 'Tanya' takes 90 days. In areas with a long season (6+ months), you can grow long-duration crops like cassava or sweet potatoes.

Soil type also matters. Sandy soils drain fast and need frequent watering. Clay soils hold water but can become waterlogged. If your soil is poor, add compost or manure before planting. Choose varieties that tolerate poor soil, like 'Mwezi Moja' beans or 'Sosuma' sorghum. For acidic soils, which are common in high-rainfall areas, grow acid-tolerant crops like tea, pineapple, or certain potato varieties.

Lastly, talk to local extension officers or experienced farmers. They know which varieties perform well in your area. Visit a nearby research station or agricultural show to see demonstrations. Buy seeds from trusted agro-dealers who stock varieties recommended for your region. The Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) and similar bodies in other countries publish lists of recommended varieties. Using the right seed for your region reduces risk and increases your chances of a good harvest.

3. Seed Quality Tests: Viability and Germination

Before you plant, you need to know if your seeds will grow. Two simple tests can save you time and money: the viability test and the germination test. Viability tells you if the seed is alive; germination tells you what percentage will sprout. You can do both at home with minimal tools.

For a quick viability test, drop seeds in a glass of water. Good seeds sink; dead or hollow seeds float. This works well for beans, maize, and sunflower. But for small seeds like tomato or onion, use the rag-doll test. Moisten a piece of cloth or paper towel, place 10 seeds evenly, roll it up, and keep it warm and damp. Check after 5-7 days. Count how many have sprouted. If 8 out of 10 sprout, your germination rate is 80%. For a more accurate test, use a seed tray with sterile soil. Plant 100 seeds, keep moist, and count seedlings after 10-14 days. A germination rate above 80% is good; below 60% means you should buy new seeds.

Another test is the float test for specific crops. For maize, put seeds in water with a pinch of salt. Good seeds sink; bad ones float. For rice, use a salt solution (1 part salt to 10 parts water). Viable seeds sink. Always rinse seeds after float tests to remove salt.

You can also check seed vigor by pressing a seed with your fingernail. If it is hard and plump, it is likely viable. If it crumbles or is soft, it is dead. For large seeds like beans, cut one open. A healthy seed has a white, creamy embryo. A black or brown embryo means it is dead.

Record your results. If germination is low, increase seeding rate. For example, if you need 100 plants and germination is 70%, sow 143 seeds (100 divided by 0.7). Always test seeds from new sources or from your own saved seeds. This simple step prevents empty nurseries and wasted labor.

4. Seed Sources and Certification

Where you buy your seeds matters as much as the variety. In Africa, you can get seeds from several sources: agro-dealers, research stations, NGOs, fellow farmers, and your own saved seeds. Each has pros and cons. Certified seeds from agro-dealers are tested for quality and are usually free of pests and diseases. They come in sealed packets with labels showing variety, germination rate, and lot number. Always check the expiry date. For example, a packet of 'H614D' maize from Kenya Seed Company should have a germination rate of at least 85% and be less than one year old.

Research stations like KALRO in Kenya, TARI in Tanzania, and NARO in Uganda produce foundation and certified seeds. They also sell small packs to farmers. NGOs sometimes distribute free or subsidized seeds, especially after disasters. But be careful: free seeds may not be the best variety for your area, and they may be older. Always ask for the variety name and planting instructions.

Farmer-saved seeds are cheap and adapted to local conditions, but they can carry diseases and have low germination. If you save seeds, select from your healthiest plants, harvest at full maturity, and dry properly. For cross-pollinated crops like maize, you must isolate to avoid mixing varieties. For self-pollinated crops like beans and tomatoes, saving is easier.

Certification is a system where a government agency inspects seed production and tests quality. Certified seed bags have a blue or green label with details. In Kenya, the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) certifies seeds. In Tanzania, it is the Tanzania Official Seed Certification Institute (TOSCI). Look for the label. Avoid buying seeds from open buckets or unlabeled containersthey could be fake or expired. If you buy from a new seller, ask for a receipt and test a small sample before planting your whole field. Good seeds are an investment, not an expense.

5. Seed Storage Best Practices

Proper storage keeps seeds alive from one season to the next. The three enemies of seeds are moisture, heat, and pests. If you store seeds in a damp, hot place, they will die or rot. Aim for a cool, dry, dark place. A temperature of 10-15C and relative humidity below 50% is ideal. In many African homes, this means storing seeds in sealed containers in the coolest room, away from the kitchen fire.

Start by drying seeds well. Sun-dry seeds on a mat for 2-3 days until they are crisp. For maize, the kernel should crack when bitten, not dent. For beans, the seed should shatter when hit with a hammer. After drying, put seeds in an airtight container. Glass jars with rubber seals, plastic buckets with tight lids, or metal cans work well. Add a desiccant like silica gel or dry wood ash to absorb moisture. You can also use dry rice grains: put a handful in a cloth bag and place it in the container.

To prevent pests, mix seeds with natural repellents. For every 10 kg of seed, add 100 g of dry neem leaves or 50 g of wood ash. For maize, you can use actellic dust (follow label instructions). Store different crops separately to avoid mixing and cross-contamination. Label each container with crop name, variety, and date of storage.

Check your seeds every 2-3 months. Look for mould, insects, or a musty smell. If you see weevils, sun-dry the seeds again and clean the container. For small quantities, you can store seeds in the refrigeratorbut let them warm to room temperature before planting to avoid condensation.

Never store seeds in plastic bags that are not sealed, as moisture can enter. Also, keep seeds away from chemicals like fertiliser or petrol. With good storage, seeds can remain viable for 2-3 years for most crops, and up to 5 years for beans and maize. Always test germination before planting stored seeds.

6. Handling Hybrid and Open-Pollinated Seeds

Hybrid and open-pollinated (OP) seeds require different handling. Hybrid seeds are the result of controlled crosses between two distinct parent lines. They are expensive but give higher yields, uniform growth, and better disease resistance. However, you cannot save seeds from hybrid plants for replanting. The next generation will be a mix of traits, often with lower yield and poor quality. For example, if you plant hybrid tomato 'Kilele F1' and save its seeds, the resulting plants may produce small, tasteless fruits. So for hybrids, you must buy new seeds each season.

Open-pollinated seeds, on the other hand, can be saved and replanted. They are cheaper and often more adaptable to local conditions. But to keep the variety pure, you must prevent cross-pollination with other varieties of the same crop. For example, if you grow two varieties of maize close together, they will cross-pollinate and the saved seeds will be mixed. To avoid this, plant different varieties at least 200 meters apart, or plant them at different times so they flower at different times. For crops like beans and tomatoes, which self-pollinate, isolation distance can be smaller (10-20 meters).

When saving OP seeds, select from your best plants. Choose plants that are healthy, productive, and have the traits you want. Harvest seeds from mature fruits or pods. For tomatoes, scoop out seeds, ferment them in water for 2 days to remove the gel, then rinse and dry. For beans, let pods dry on the plant, then shell and store. For maize, leave cobs on the plant until fully dry, then shell.

Always label your saved seeds with the variety name and date. Store them properly. If you are new to seed saving, start with easy crops like beans, tomatoes, and peppers. Avoid saving seeds from crops that cross-pollinate easily, like maize, unless you can isolate them. Remember, hybrid seeds are a recurring cost, while OP seeds can be a long-term resource if managed well. Choose based on your budget, market, and farming goals.

<h2>Practical Reference: Seed Selection and Quality for African Nurseries</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core decisions a nursery operator must make when selecting, testing, storing, and handling seed. It is written for commercial and semi-commercial seedling producers who sell to farmers, NGOs, government programmes, or agro-dealers. Every recommendation here is designed to protect your germination rates, reduce waste, and protect your reputation because in the nursery business, a failed batch of seedlings is not just lost input cost, it is lost customer trust and lost future orders.</p>

<p>Seed is the single most important input in your nursery. It typically represents only 515% of total production cost, yet it determines 100% of the genetic potential of the seedlings you sell. A farmer who buys your tomato seedling and harvests poorly will blame the seedling, not the seed lot. Your job is to buy, test, and store seed so that this never happens.</p>

<h3>1. Matching Seed Varieties to Market and Region</h3>

<p>Before you buy a single gram of seed, define your customer. A nursery supplying peri-urban vegetable growers near Nairobi needs different varieties from one supplying maize and legume farmers in the drylands of northern Ghana. Selection criteria, in order of business importance:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Customer demand:</strong> What do your buyers actually ask for? A hybrid tomato that yields 30 t/ha is worthless if your market wants local leafy amaranth.</li>
<li><strong>Agro-ecological fit:</strong> Rainfall, altitude, soil pH, and disease pressure determine which varieties will perform. A variety bred for highland Kenya will fail in lowland Mozambique.</li>
<li><strong>Maturity period:</strong> Short-season varieties reduce risk in areas with erratic rains and allow faster turnover for your customers.</li>
<li><strong>Market class:</strong> Grain colour, fruit shape, and shelf life affect the price your customers receive.</li>
<li><strong>Seed availability and cost:</strong> A superior variety you cannot re-order next season is a business risk.</li>
</ul>

<p>For African conditions, always check whether a variety is officially released and registered in your country. National variety lists are maintained by bodies such as KEPHIS in Kenya, the National Agricultural Seeds Council in Nigeria, and the Tanzania Official Seed Certification Institute (TOSCI). Buying unregistered seed exposes you to legal risk and unpredictable performance.</p>

<h3>2. Seed Quality Tests You Can Run On-Site</h3>

<p>You do not need a laboratory to protect yourself. Two simple tests a float test and a germination test will tell you whether a lot is worth sowing.</p>

<p><strong>Float test (quick viability screen):</strong> Place a sample of seed in a glass of water. Seeds that float are usually empty, insect-damaged, or dead. Seeds that sink are more likely to be viable. This is a rough screen, not a substitute for germination testing.</p>

<p><strong>Germination test (the real number):</strong> Count 100 seeds at random. Place them on moistened tissue, cloth, or sand in a shallow tray. Cover, keep moist, and keep at room temperature (2030C). Count normal seedlings at the recommended interval for the species. Calculate:</p>

<p style="text-align:center;font-weight:bold">Germination % = (normal seedlings seeds sown) 100</p>

<p>If the result is below the certified minimum on the label, reject the lot or increase sowing density to compensate. Never sell seedlings from a lot that failed germination testing.</p>

<h3>3. Seed Sources and Certification</h3>

<p>In most African countries, the seed supply chain has three tiers: certified seed (government-inspected, labelled, high genetic purity), quality declared seed (QDS locally produced, inspected at lower cost, increasingly important for legumes and cereals), and farmer-saved or informal seed (cheap but variable). For a nursery selling to paying customers, certified seed is the default. QDS is acceptable for crops where certified seed is scarce or expensive, provided you test germination yourself.</p>

<p>Always buy from an authorised agro-dealer or directly from a registered seed company. Ask for the lot number, germination percentage, and test date. Keep the receipt and the label if a lot fails, this is your evidence for a claim.</p>

<h3>4. Hybrid vs Open-Pollinated Seed: The Business Decision</h3>

<p>Hybrid seed gives higher yields, more uniformity, and often better disease resistance but it must be bought every season, and it costs more. Open-pollinated varieties (OPVs) can be saved and replanted, but yields are usually lower and genetic quality drifts over generations unless carefully managed.</p>

<p>For a nursery business, the rule is simple: sell what your customer will pay for. Hybrid vegetable seedlings (tomato, cabbage, kale) command premium prices in peri-urban markets. OPVs suit low-input, dryland customers and seed-saving programmes. Never sell farmer-saved seed as if it were certified this is both an ethical and a legal breach in most African jurisdictions.</p>

<h3>5. Storage Best Practices</h3>

<p>Seed is alive. Heat and moisture kill it faster than anything else. The three enemies are heat, humidity, and pests. Practical rules for an African nursery:</p>

<ul>
<li>Store seed in airtight containers (glass jars, sealed plastic drums, or vacuum bags) with a desiccant such as silica gel or dry charcoal.</li>
<li>Keep the storage room cool, dark, and ventilated. A shaded mud-brick or insulated room is far better than a hot iron-sheet store.</li>
<li>Label every container with crop, variety, lot number, germination %, and date of test.</li>
<li>Use the oldest tested seed first (first-in, first-out).</li>
<li>Inspect monthly for insects, mould, or moisture. Discard any lot showing mould or a musty smell.</li>
<li>Never store seed on the floor or against an outside wall moisture migrates there.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Define your customer and market class before choosing a variety.</li>
<li>Confirm the variety is officially released and suited to your agro-ecological zone.</li>
<li>Buy only from authorised agro-dealers or registered seed companies.</li>
<li>Record lot number, germination %, and test date for every purchase.</li>
<li>Run a germination test on every new lot before sowing in volume.</li>
<li>Reject any lot below the certified minimum germination standard.</li>
<li>Store seed cool, dry, dark, and pest-free in labelled airtight containers.</li>
<li>Handle treated seed as poison never eat, feed, or reuse the packaging.</li>
</ol>
