Lessons

1. Where to find end-of-life vehicles: auctions, police pounds, and private sellers

Start with the three main rivers that feed a scrapyard: auctions, police pounds, and private sellers. Auctions in most African cities are run by banks, insurance companies, and government disposal boards. Bank auctions sell repossessed cars that did not fetch their reserve price; insurance auctions sell write-offs from accidents and floods. Register with two or three auction houses, pay the refundable deposit (often 10,00050,000 in local currency), and attend every sale for a month before you bid. Read the catalogue carefully: a 2005 Toyota Corolla with frontal damage might go for 150,000 shillings, while a similar car with rear damage goes for 90,000 because panel beaters need the front end. Police pounds sell unclaimed vehicles after a notice period; you usually bid per lot and must remove the vehicle within 7 days. Private sellers are the cheapest but riskiest: walk your neighbourhood, talk to watchmen, and leave your number at petrol stations. Offer to remove a dead car for free in exchange for the logbook, or pay 20,00050,000 for a complete vehicle with papers. Never buy a car without seeing the chassis number stamped on the frame and matching it to the logbook. If the seller refuses to meet at the vehicle or rushes you, walk away. Keep a notebook with date, seller name, phone number, vehicle make, plate, chassis number, price paid, and witness name. This single habit protects you from stolen-vehicle charges and helps you track which source gives the best metal per shilling.

2. Negotiating prices with vehicle owners and tow truck operators

Negotiation in this trade is not about arguing; it is about knowing the weight and the market before you open your mouth. Weigh a typical sedan at 1,2001,500 kg, a pickup at 1,8002,200 kg, and a minibus at 2,5003,000 kg. Scrap steel in most African cities sells for 2545 per kg, copper for 600900 per kg, and aluminium for 200350 per kg. That means a complete sedan is worth roughly 30,00060,000 in scrap value alone, plus reusable parts. Use this as your ceiling. When a private owner asks 200,000 for a dead car, do not laugh; ask to see the logbook, point out the missing gearbox, the rusted floor, and the cost of towing, then offer 60,000 cash today. Owners who need space or cash usually settle at 4070% of their first price. Tow truck operators are your best allies: they see dead cars every day. Agree on a fixed rate per trip (for example 15,000 within 10 km, 25,000 beyond) and pay promptly. Offer them 510% of the scrap value of any car they bring you, or a flat 10,000 referral fee. Always confirm the tow truck has a valid licence and insurance; if the vehicle falls and damages property, you do not want to be liable. Get a signed receipt with the seller's ID number, phone, and the vehicle's plate and chassis number. Never pay the full amount before the vehicle is on your yard. Pay half on loading, half on arrival. This protects you from disappearing sellers and from vehicles that turn out to be stolen.

3. Identifying valuable scrap metal sources: construction sites, farms, and households

Construction sites are the richest and most reliable source of clean scrap. Visit sites at the end of a project, when contractors want the site cleared. Expect offcuts of rebar, angle iron, roof sheets, scaffolding pipes, and wire mesh. Offer to remove everything for free, or pay a small fee (5,00020,000) for a full truckload. Talk to the site foreman, not the main contractor; the foreman decides who takes the waste. Farms give you old ploughs, harrows, water tanks, fencing wire, and broken tractor parts. A single farm can yield 5002,000 kg of mixed metal. Build a route and visit the same farms every 3 months; farmers remember a buyer who pays fairly and removes rubbish. Households produce smaller but steady volumes: old beds, iron sheets, pots, bicycles, and car parts. Work with waste collectors (chokoras, zabbaleen, or local youth groups) and pay per kilogram delivered to your yard. Set a clear price list on a board at your gate so nobody argues. Separate metals on arrival: steel, cast iron, aluminium, copper, brass, and stainless steel. Cast iron (brake drums, engine blocks) is heavy and pays well. Copper from cables and radiators is the most valuable; strip it with a knife and pliers, never burn it, because burning is illegal in many countries and loses weight. Aluminium from radiators, gearboxes, and window frames is light but pays 200350 per kg. Keep a magnet: it sticks to steel and cast iron, not to aluminium or copper. Sort into separate bins and weigh each bin separately. Clean, sorted metal sells for 2030% more than mixed scrap.

4. Building relationships with local mechanics and panel beaters for referrals

Mechanics and panel beaters are the gatekeepers of the vehicle scrap trade. A single busy garage replaces 1030 engines, gearboxes, and body panels every month, and they need someone to take the old parts away. Your job is to become that person, reliably and cheaply. Start by visiting 10 garages within a 5 km radius of your yard. Introduce yourself, leave a card or a painted sign with your phone number, and offer a simple deal: you collect any unwanted metal for free, and you pay 1020% of the scrap value in cash for anything valuable like copper radiators or aluminium gearboxes. Bring a small gift on the first visit: a cold drink, a packet of tea, or 2,000 in cash. Do not talk business for the first 10 minutes; ask about their work, their suppliers, and their problems. Then explain your service. Visit the same garages every week on the same day. Consistency matters more than price. When a mechanic calls, arrive within 2 hours, even for a small load. Pay immediately in cash and give a written receipt. Ask for referrals to other garages, and offer a bonus of 5,000 for every new garage that sends you 500 kg or more. Panel beaters are especially valuable because they generate bent body panels, doors, and bonnets that are heavy steel. Some will also sell you complete accident write-offs they cannot repair. Keep a ledger of every garage: name, owner, phone, weekly volume, and what they usually have. After 3 months, you will have a network that calls you first, and you will spend less time chasing vehicles and more time processing metal.

5. Transporting vehicles and scrap to your yard safely and legally

Transport is where most beginners lose money and get into trouble. A complete car needs a tow truck or a flatbed; a truckload of scrap needs a lorry with high sides and a tarpaulin. Never overload. A 3-tonne lorry should carry no more than 3,000 kg of steel, and you must distribute weight evenly over the axles. Overloading breaks springs, causes accidents, and attracts fines. Before every trip, check tyre pressure, brakes, lights, and the tow hitch. Use two ratchet straps per vehicle, crossed over the wheels, plus a chain on the chassis. For scrap, tie down with ropes every 1 metre and cover with a tarpaulin so pieces do not fall on the road. Legally, you need: a valid driving licence for the class of vehicle, third-party insurance, a roadworthy certificate for the truck, and a permit to transport scrap if your municipality requires one. In many countries, moving a vehicle without a logbook or transfer document is treated as stolen-goods transport. Carry the signed seller's receipt, a copy of the seller's ID, and the logbook or police clearance. If you are stopped, stay calm, show the documents, and do not pay a bribe; ask for the officer's name and a receipt for any fine. Plan your route to avoid narrow market roads and school zones during peak hours. Drive during daylight; night transport of scrap invites theft and accidents. Keep a logbook for your truck: date, driver, load, source, destination, and fuel. This log helps you calculate cost per tonne and prove legality if questioned. Finally, insure your load if the value is above 100,000; a single accident can wipe out a month of profit.

6. Documenting sources to avoid buying stolen vehicles or metal

Buying stolen property is the fastest way to lose your yard, your licence, and your freedom. In most African countries, receiving stolen goods carries a prison sentence of 310 years, and ignorance is not a defence. Build a simple paper trail for every purchase. For vehicles, record: date, seller's full name, ID number, phone number, vehicle make, model, colour, plate number, chassis number, engine number, price paid, and the seller's signature or thumbprint. Ask to see the original logbook and a copy of the seller's ID. If the logbook is in someone else's name, get a signed letter of authority from that person, plus a copy of their ID. If the vehicle is from an auction or police pound, keep the auction receipt and the release letter. For scrap metal, record: date, source (garage, farm, household, construction site), name of person delivering, ID number, phone, weight, type of metal, and price paid. Take a photo of the seller with the load and their ID on the same day. Keep all documents in a locked file for at least 5 years. Check chassis numbers against your national stolen-vehicle database if one exists; many police stations will run a free check. For high-value copper, ask where it came from and refuse anything that looks freshly cut from live cables, transformers, or railway lines. If a seller cannot explain the source, refuse the load, even if the price is good. Register your yard with the local authority and keep a visitors' book. When police visit, your records prove you are a legitimate buyer, not a fence. A clean reputation also makes auctions, garages, and farms more willing to deal with you.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Sourcing Vehicles and Scrap Metal</h2>

<p>In the African car-breaking and metal scrapyard business, profit is made at the point of sourcingnot at the point of sale. A breaker who pays too much for a wreck, or who accepts stolen metal, destroys margin and risks prosecution. This Practical Reference gives you a business-first framework for finding end-of-life vehicles (ELVs), negotiating hard, identifying high-value scrap, building a referral network, moving loads legally, and documenting every source. Treat it as your operating manual.</p>

<h3>1. Where to Find End-of-Life Vehicles</h3>
<p>Your supply pipeline should have at least three channels so no single source can starve your yard:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Public auctions:</strong> Insurance salvage auctions, bank repossessions, and government disposal auctions are the highest-volume source. In Kenya, the Insurance Regulatory Authority (IRA) requires insurers to dispose of salvage through transparent processes; in South Africa, salvage is often sold via auction houses such as those regulated under the Second-Hand Goods Act (Act No. 6 of 2009). Register as a buyer, pay the refundable deposit, and inspect lots before bidding.</li>
<li><strong>Police pounds and municipal impounds:</strong> Unclaimed vehicles are periodically disposed of by tender or auction. These are cheap but paperwork-heavy. Always demand the disposal order, tender award letter, and a release note before towing.</li>
<li><strong>Private sellers:</strong> Individual owners with broken-down or accident-damaged cars. These are your best margin if you negotiate well, but your highest risk for stolen vehicles. Always verify chassis and engine numbers against the national register (e.g., NTSA in Kenya, eNaTIS in South Africa, DVLA in Ghana, FRSC in Nigeria).</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Negotiating Prices with Vehicle Owners and Tow Truck Operators</h3>
<p>Negotiation is a repeatable process, not a personality trait. Use these rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Price on weight, not sentiment.</strong> Calculate the vehicle's scrap value: kerb weight local scrap price per tonne. Then add value for recoverable parts (engine, gearbox, alternator, catalytic converter, battery). Your maximum offer should be 6070% of recoverable value.</li>
<li><strong>Anchor low with justification.</strong> Show the owner the tow cost, the removal cost, and the paperwork cost. "I'm offering KES 25,000 because towing and deregistration will cost me KES 15,000."</li>
<li><strong>Tow truck operators are partners, not vendors.</strong> Offer them a fixed referral fee per vehicle delivered, or a per-kilometre haulage rate. A reliable operator who brings you 10 cars a month is worth more than a one-off discount.</li>
<li><strong>Never negotiate on the roadside without inspection.</strong> Check for missing engines, cut catalytic converters, and flood damage before agreeing on price.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Identifying Valuable Scrap Metal Sources</h3>
<p>Not all scrap is equal. Your buyers pay by grade, so your sourcing should target high-grade streams:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Construction sites:</strong> Rebar offcuts, structural steel, aluminium window frames, copper wiring, and brass fittings. Build a relationship with the site foreman or main contractor. Offer a fixed monthly collection slot.</li>
<li><strong>Farms:</strong> Old implements, tractor parts, fencing wire, water pumps, and irrigation pipes. Farms often have decades of accumulated metal. Negotiate a per-load price rather than per-kg to save time.</li>
<li><strong>Households:</strong> Old appliances, roofing sheets, pots, and bicycles. This is low-value but high-volume if you run a collection route or work with local scrap pickers (informal collectors).</li>
<li><strong>Industrial estates:</strong> Machine shops, fabricators, and factories generate swarf, offcuts, and end-of-life machinery. These are your highest-value relationships.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Building Relationships with Local Mechanics and Panel Beaters</h3>
<p>Mechanics and panel beaters see broken vehicles before anyone else. They are your cheapest and most reliable referral channel. To win their loyalty:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pay a referral fee on time, every time.</strong> A fixed amount per vehicle (or a percentage of the final purchase price) beats vague promises.</li>
<li><strong>Buy their scrap too.</strong> Old parts, batteries, and offcuts are a steady income stream for themand a steady supply for you.</li>
<li><strong>Offer a swap.</strong> If they need a specific used part, give it to them at cost in exchange for referrals.</li>
<li><strong>Be visible.</strong> Visit their workshops monthly. Bring branded stickers or a simple price list for scrap metals.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a referral register.</strong> Track who sent what, so you can pay accurately and spot your best partners.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Transporting Vehicles and Scrap Safely and Legally</h3>
<p>Transport is where many yards lose moneyand where they get shut down. Key requirements across most African jurisdictions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vehicle registration and licensing:</strong> Your tow truck or lorry must be registered, insured, and have a valid roadworthy certificate.</li>
<li><strong>Load securing:</strong> Use chains and straps rated for the load. Overloaded or unsecured loads cause accidents and fines.</li>
<li><strong>Transit documentation:</strong> Carry the purchase agreement, seller's ID copy, vehicle logbook (or deregistration certificate), and a movement permit where required.</li>
<li><strong>Hazardous materials:</strong> Drain fuel, oil, and coolant before transport where possible. Batteries must be transported upright and secured.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-border rules:</strong> If you move scrap across borders, check the Basel Convention requirements and your national environmental authority (e.g., NEMA in Kenya, SAWIC in South Africa).</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Documenting Sources to Avoid Buying Stolen Vehicles or Metal</h3>
<p>Documentation is your legal shield and your business intelligence. Keep a sourcing file for every purchase:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Vehicle file:</strong> Copy of seller's ID, signed sale agreement, copy of logbook or deregistration certificate, VIN/chassis photo, and payment receipt.</li>
<li><strong>Scrap file:</strong> Seller's name and ID, source description (site, farm, household), weight ticket, and payment receipt.</li>
<li><strong>Referral file:</strong> Name of mechanic or tow operator, date, vehicle/scrap description, and referral fee paid.</li>
<li><strong>Digital backup:</strong> Photograph every document and store it in a cloud folder or external drive. Paper gets lost; photos don't.</li>
</ul>
<p>This documentation also helps you claim input costs, prove compliance during inspections, and build a credit history with buyers and banks.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Register with at least two auction houses and one police/municipal disposal list.</li>
<li>Verify VIN/chassis and seller ID before any payment.</li>
<li>Price vehicles on scrap weight + recoverable parts, not on the seller's asking price.</li>
<li>Sign a written purchase agreement for every vehicle and scrap load.</li>
<li>Pay referral fees to mechanics and tow operators on time.</li>
<li>Secure all loads with rated chains/straps and carry transit documents.</li>
<li>Keep a photo and document file for every source.</li>
<li>Refuse any metal that could be public infrastructure or stolen.</li>
</ol>
