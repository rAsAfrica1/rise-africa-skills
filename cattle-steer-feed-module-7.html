Module 7

🐄 Practical Cattle Feed Formulation

The arithmetic module: build an ingredient database from feeds you can actually buy, balance rations with Pearson's Square, mix your own mineral premix, drive down cost per unit of nutrient, and keep the records that prove what you fed.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Build a working ingredient table with dry matter, crude protein and energy for every feed you can buy
  • State the published crude protein and energy target for each class of cattle on your farm
  • Use Pearson's Square to blend two feeds to an exact crude protein target
  • Calculate the quantities of limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt and magnesium oxide needed for a herd
  • Rank ingredients by cost per unit of the nutrient that is actually limiting your ration
  • Keep a batch sheet that records exactly what went into every mix and when

Lesson 7.1~13 min

Feed Ingredient Database for Cattle

In this lesson
  • Build a working ingredient table with dry matter, crude protein and energy for every feed you can buy
  • Convert a bag price into cost per kg of dry matter, per kg of crude protein and per Mcal
  • Apply the published inclusion limits that cap how much of each byproduct is safe

You cannot formulate anything until you know what you are formulating with. The ingredient database is the foundation of every calculation in this module, and it has four columns that matter: dry matter, crude protein, energy, and delivered price.

Dry matter comes first because feeds are bought wet and fed dry. Dry hay is about 88 percent dry matter, silage 28 to 35 percent, and wet brewers grains are wetter still. If you compare a tonne of silage with a tonne of hay you are comparing about 0.30 tonnes of feed with 0.88 tonnes of feed. Every price and every ration in this module is therefore expressed on a dry matter basis.

Energy is derived rather than measured on most farms. If you have an ADF figure from a laboratory, use TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%). From TDN you get net energy: NEL Mcal/kg = (0.0245 x TDN%) - 0.12 for dairy, and NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315 for growing beef. For concentrates and byproducts, use the supplier's declared figures where they exist and mark them as supplier claims until you verify them.

The following working table gives typical values for feeds common in African cattle systems. Treat every figure as an approximate starting point to be replaced by your own analysis; composition varies enormously with processing, season and adulteration.

  1. Maize bran: about 88 percent dry matter, moderate energy, crude protein commonly in the region of 9 to 11 percent of dry matter. Bulk energy carrier, widely available, frequently adulterated with maize cob.
  2. Wheat bran: similar dry matter, slightly higher protein than maize bran, high phosphorus relative to calcium.
  3. Cottonseed cake: about 90 percent dry matter, crude protein commonly around 35 to 45 percent of dry matter depending on oil extraction method. The main affordable protein concentrate in much of Africa.
  4. Sunflower cake: about 90 percent dry matter, crude protein commonly around 28 to 35 percent, more fibrous than cottonseed cake.
  5. Wet brewers grains: very high moisture, good protein and energy, and cheap where a brewery is close.
  6. Molasses: roughly 75 percent dry matter, almost pure fermentable energy, negligible protein, excellent as a carrier and palatability aid.
  7. Maize stover and cereal straws: about 88 percent dry matter, crude protein below the 7.5 percent minimum used in NRC recommendations, ADF often above 45 percent.
  8. Urea: not a feed but a nitrogen source, usable only by ruminants and only in small, well-mixed, gradually introduced quantities.

The published inclusion limits are not optional and they belong in the database as a hard column. Distillers dried grains up to 50 percent of diet dry matter, above which sulphur toxicity becomes a risk and the phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting. Wet corn gluten feed 25 to 30 percent. Soybean hulls 15 to 20 percent. Whole cottonseed up to 25 percent of diet dry matter and no more than 0.5 percent of body weight daily because of gossypol. Cottonseed meal for bulls limited to 3 to 5 lb per day and withdrawn two to three months before breeding. Wet brewers grains 30 to 40 percent of diet dry matter, with storage losses of about 15 percent and palatability problems above 30 percent. Beet pulp 15 to 20 percent. The general principle is that byproducts should not exceed 50 percent of the concentrate fraction, and forage quality should be maintained to buffer their variable composition.

Worked example: which protein is actually cheapest? Assumed delivered prices.

  1. Cottonseed cake at 0.42 USD per kg as fed, 90 percent dry matter, 40 percent crude protein of dry matter. Dry matter cost: 0.42 / 0.90 = 0.467 USD per kg DM. Crude protein per kg DM: 0.40 kg. Cost per kg of crude protein: 0.467 / 0.40 = 1.17 USD.
  2. Sunflower cake at 0.31 USD per kg as fed, 90 percent dry matter, 30 percent crude protein. Dry matter cost: 0.31 / 0.90 = 0.344 USD. Cost per kg of crude protein: 0.344 / 0.30 = 1.15 USD.
  3. Wet brewers grains at 0.09 USD per kg as fed, 24 percent dry matter, 25 percent crude protein of dry matter. Dry matter cost: 0.09 / 0.24 = 0.375 USD. Cost per kg of crude protein: 0.375 / 0.25 = 1.50 USD.

The cheapest bag, brewers grains, turns out to be the dearest protein once water is removed, while the two cakes are almost identical. That reversal is the single most useful thing an ingredient database does. Brewers grains may still be the right buy for their energy and their price per kg of dry matter, but you should know which nutrient you are actually buying.

Do the same for energy. Divide cost per kg of dry matter by TDN as a decimal to get cost per kg of TDN, or by the NEL value to get cost per Mcal. Rank the list. In most seasons the ranking tells you something you did not expect.

Concrete African context. A feed trader in Lusaka, Zambia, sells maize bran, sunflower cake and molasses to small dairy units. The producers who buy well keep a laminated card with three columns: price per bag, kg of dry matter per bag, and cost per kg of crude protein. When the bran price jumped one season, the card showed immediately that molasses plus a slightly larger cake allowance was cheaper per unit of energy and protein than the bran-heavy mix they had used for years.

Update prices monthly, update analyses seasonally, and never formulate from memory.

Lesson 7.2~13 min

Formulation for Different Life Stages

In this lesson
  • State the published crude protein and energy target for each class of cattle on your farm
  • Build a ration that meets the target without exceeding the animal's physical intake capacity
  • Recognise when a life-stage ration is limited by intake rather than by ingredient supply

Every ration starts with a requirement, and requirements are published by class. Copy the following targets into your ration book, because almost every formulation mistake is a failure to look them up.

Crude protein as a percentage of diet dry matter, or as pounds per day where the published figure is expressed that way:

  1. Neonatal calves on a milk-only diet: 20 to 24 percent of dry matter.
  2. Pre-weaning calves at 100 to 150 kg: 16 to 18 percent for optimal gain.
  3. Post-weaning calves: 14 to 16 percent.
  4. Growing cattle at 400 kg: 10.2 percent at 1.0 lb/day gain, 10.5 percent at 1.5 lb/day, 10.8 percent at 2.0 lb/day, which is 0.88, 1.33 and 1.60 lb of crude protein per day respectively.
  5. Finishing cattle at 600 kg gaining 2.0 to 3.5 lb/day: 8.5 to 9.5 percent of diet dry matter.
  6. Mature breeding bulls at 1,400 to 1,600 lb: 1.8 to 2.1 lb per day, 7.5 to 8.5 percent of dry matter.
  7. Pregnant replacement heifers in early gestation at 1,000 to 1,200 lb: 1.5 to 1.7 lb per day, about 7.8 percent.
  8. Lactating cows at 1,200 to 1,400 lb: 2.2 to 2.5 lb per day at 10 lb of milk, 3.0 to 3.4 lb at 20 lb of milk, 3.4 lb or more at 25 lb.
  9. Absolute floor for any class: 7.5 percent of diet dry matter.

Energy targets, using TDN because it is the unit most African suppliers can talk about: a 500 lb steer gaining 1.5 lb/day needs 8.0 lb of TDN per day at 59.5 percent of diet dry matter; a replacement heifer gaining 0.5 lb/day needs 5.0 to 5.1 lb of TDN and one gaining 2.0 lb/day needs 6.8 to 7.0 lb; a 1,200 lb cow needs 12.8 lb of TDN at 10 lb of milk and 17.5 lb at 20 lb of milk.

Note the shape of that list. Protein requirement as a percentage falls as the animal grows and rises again at lactation, while total energy requirement rises steadily with size and production. A single farm ration cannot serve a calf at 16 percent protein and a finishing steer at 9 percent without wasting money on one of them.

Worked example: a growing steer ration. Target a 400 kg steer gaining 1.5 lb per day, requiring 10.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter and about 1.33 lb, or 0.60 kg, of crude protein per day.

  1. Intake. Your hay tests 60 percent NDF, so DMI = 120 / 60 = 2.0 percent of body weight. The steer can eat 400 x 0.02 = 8.0 kg of dry matter per day.
  2. Required crude protein concentration: 10.5 percent of 8.0 kg = 0.84 kg per day. Take the higher of this and the published 0.60 kg per day figure and work to 0.84 kg for safety.
  3. Hay at 8 percent crude protein contributes, if fed alone, 8.0 x 0.08 = 0.64 kg. Shortfall 0.20 kg.
  4. Replace 1.5 kg of hay with 1.5 kg of sunflower cake at 30 percent crude protein. New protein total: (6.5 x 0.08) + (1.5 x 0.30) = 0.52 + 0.45 = 0.97 kg, which is 12.1 percent of dry matter. Comfortably above target.
  5. Trim to 1.0 kg of cake: (7.0 x 0.08) + (1.0 x 0.30) = 0.56 + 0.30 = 0.86 kg, or 10.75 percent. On target.
  6. Energy check. Hay at 36 percent ADF gives TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 36) = 60.9 percent. Cake assume 70 percent TDN. Total TDN = (7.0 x 0.609) + (1.0 x 0.70) = 4.26 + 0.70 = 4.96 kg, which is 10.9 lb, against the published 8.0 lb needed by a 500 lb steer at 1.5 lb/day. A 400 kg steer is larger, so this is in a plausible range for the target gain.
  7. Cost. 1.0 kg of cake at 0.31 USD = 0.31 USD per head per day plus the hay.

Now the important observation. Step 1 capped everything. If the hay had been 70 percent NDF instead of 60, intake would have been 120 / 70 = 1.71 percent, or 6.8 kg, and no amount of cake within the concentrate ceiling of 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight would have delivered the required energy. When a ration will not balance, check intake capacity before you blame the ingredients. Improving the forage is often the only real solution.

Calves are a special case worth stating plainly. A pre-weaning calf needs 16 to 18 percent crude protein and cannot be fed on adult forage; the rumen is not yet functional enough. Post-weaning at 14 to 16 percent, the requirement is still far above anything a grass hay supplies. Budget a purchased or home-mixed calf starter and treat it as a separate ration, not as a scoop of the cow ration.

Concrete African context. A mixed farm outside Arusha, Tanzania, ran one bucket of the same maize bran and cottonseed cake mix for calves, heifers, cows and the bull, adjusting only quantity. Writing the four requirement lines on the store wall showed that the calves were being fed a mix at about 12 percent crude protein against a 16 to 18 percent requirement, and the bull was getting a mix well above his 7.5 to 8.5 percent need. Splitting into two mixes cost nothing extra in total and fixed both ends at once.

Look up the requirement, check intake capacity, then formulate. In that order.

Lesson 7.3~13 min

Balancing for Energy and Protein

In this lesson
  • Use Pearson's Square to blend two feeds to an exact crude protein target
  • Extend the square to a three-ingredient mix using a fixed forage base
  • Check every protein-balanced ration against its energy requirement before feeding it

Pearson's Square is a two-ingredient blending method that produces an exact protein target in about thirty seconds with a pencil. It is the most useful piece of arithmetic in practical feed formulation and it works for energy just as well as for protein, provided both ingredients are expressed on the same dry matter basis.

The method. Draw a square. Put the target value in the middle. Put the crude protein of the higher-protein ingredient at the top left and the lower-protein ingredient at the bottom left. Subtract diagonally, always taking the smaller from the larger and ignoring signs. The number that appears at the bottom right is the number of parts of the top-left ingredient; the number at the top right is the parts of the bottom-left ingredient. Add the parts and convert to percentages.

Worked example one: a growing ration at 12 percent crude protein from maize bran and cottonseed cake.

  1. Cottonseed cake at 40 percent crude protein, top left. Maize bran at 10 percent, bottom left. Target 12 in the middle.
  2. Top-left diagonal: 12 - 10 = 2. That is the parts of cottonseed cake.
  3. Bottom-left diagonal: 40 - 12 = 28. That is the parts of maize bran.
  4. Total parts: 2 + 28 = 30.
  5. Cottonseed cake: 2 / 30 = 6.7 percent of the mix. Maize bran: 28 / 30 = 93.3 percent.
  6. Check: (0.067 x 40) + (0.933 x 10) = 2.68 + 9.33 = 12.0 percent. Correct.
  7. For a 100 kg batch: 6.7 kg of cake and 93.3 kg of bran. At 0.42 and 0.24 USD per kg that is 2.81 + 22.39 = 25.20 USD, or 0.252 USD per kg of mix.

Worked example two: a dairy concentrate at 18 percent crude protein from the same two feeds.

  1. Diagonals: 18 - 10 = 8 parts of cake; 40 - 18 = 22 parts of bran. Total 30.
  2. Cake: 8 / 30 = 26.7 percent. Bran: 22 / 30 = 73.3 percent.
  3. Check: (0.267 x 40) + (0.733 x 10) = 10.68 + 7.33 = 18.0 percent. Correct.
  4. Cost per kg: (0.267 x 0.42) + (0.733 x 0.24) = 0.112 + 0.176 = 0.288 USD.

Note what the second example shows: raising the concentrate from 12 to 18 percent protein raised the cost per kg by only 3.6 cents, because the expensive ingredient went from 6.7 to 26.7 percent of a mix that is mostly cheap bran. Protein is usually far less expensive to add than producers assume.

Extending to three ingredients. Real rations have a forage base you cannot change, so treat the forage as fixed, calculate what the remaining concentrate portion must supply, and then run the square on the concentrate.

Worked example three: a 450 kg lactating cow giving 12 litres.

  1. Intake. Napier silage at 64 percent NDF gives DMI = 120 / 64 = 1.88 percent, so 450 x 0.0188 = 8.5 kg of total dry matter capacity.
  2. Feed 6.0 kg of that as silage dry matter and reserve 2.5 kg for concentrate. Check the concentrate ceiling: 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight allows well above 2.5 kg for a 450 kg cow, so this is safe.
  3. Requirement. A cow of about this size at this yield needs roughly 3.0 lb, or 1.36 kg, of crude protein per day by the published 20 lb milk anchor.
  4. Silage at 9 percent crude protein contributes 6.0 x 0.09 = 0.54 kg. Concentrate must supply 1.36 - 0.54 = 0.82 kg from 2.5 kg, which is 0.82 / 2.5 = 32.8 percent crude protein.
  5. Run the square on cottonseed cake at 40 and maize bran at 10, target 32.8: cake parts = 32.8 - 10 = 22.8; bran parts = 40 - 32.8 = 7.2; total 30. Cake = 22.8 / 30 = 76 percent, bran = 24 percent.
  6. So 1.9 kg of cake and 0.6 kg of bran per cow per day. Cost: (1.9 x 0.42) + (0.6 x 0.24) = 0.80 + 0.14 = 0.94 USD per head per day.

Now the energy check, which must never be skipped. Silage at 40 percent ADF gives TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 57.7 percent. Assume cake at 70 and bran at 78 percent TDN. Total TDN = (6.0 x 0.577) + (1.9 x 0.70) + (0.6 x 0.78) = 3.46 + 1.33 + 0.47 = 5.26 kg, which is 11.6 lb, against a published 17.5 lb for a 1,200 lb cow at 20 lb of milk. This cow is smaller and giving less, but the ration is clearly energy-tight, and that is the honest conclusion. The fix is either better forage, which raises both intake and digestibility at once, or shifting some cake to bran and molasses, which lowers protein toward a still-adequate level while raising energy.

That tension between protein and energy is the whole craft. Pearson's Square gets you the protein exactly; only the TDN check tells you whether the animal can actually perform.

Two safety rails. Never let total diet crude protein fall below 7.5 percent of dry matter. And when moving cattle onto a higher-concentrate ration, step up gradually rather than switching, keeping any change between feeding groups within 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter.

Concrete African context. A dairy training group in Meru County, Kenya, teaches Pearson's Square with two buckets and a stick in the sand before anyone touches a calculator. The measurable outcome is not better rations on paper; it is that farmers who can do the square themselves stop buying a branded 18 percent dairy meal at a premium when their own bran and cake mix hits the same figure for less.

Lesson 7.4~13 min

Mineral Premix for Cattle

In this lesson
  • Calculate the quantities of limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt and magnesium oxide needed for a herd
  • Blend a trace element premix that meets requirements without approaching tolerable limits
  • Compare the cost of a home-mixed premix against a commercial product on a like-for-like basis

A mineral premix is the cheapest insurance in cattle farming and the easiest thing to buy badly. Commercial products vary from excellent to almost inert, and the only way to know which you have bought is to read the guaranteed analysis and do the arithmetic. Doing that arithmetic also tells you whether you should be mixing your own.

Targets first, as a percentage of total diet dry matter. Growing cattle: calcium 0.35 to 0.65, phosphorus 0.20 to 0.30, magnesium 0.10, potassium 0.60, sodium 0.06 to 0.08. Gestating: calcium 0.20 to 0.35, phosphorus 0.15 to 0.20, magnesium 0.12 to 0.15. Lactating: calcium 0.35 to 0.65, phosphorus 0.25 to 0.35, magnesium 0.20 to 0.25, potassium 0.70, sodium 0.10. Sulphur 0.15 percent for all. Calcium to phosphorus ratio 2.0 to 2.5 to 1 in every case.

Trace elements in mg per kg of diet dry matter, with the maximum tolerable level in brackets: copper 10 (40), zinc 30 (500), manganese 20 to 40 (2,000), selenium 0.10 (5), cobalt 0.10 to 0.15 (25), iodine 0.50 (50). Molybdenum is not required and its tolerable ceiling is 5 ppm.

Worked example: mixing 100 kg of premix for a lactating herd. Assume cows eat 10 kg of dry matter a day and will receive 100 g of premix each per day, so the premix must supply the shortfall within that 100 g.

  1. Decide the shortfall per cow per day. Suppose the forage supplies 0.30 percent calcium and 0.18 percent phosphorus, and you are targeting 0.50 and 0.25 percent. Shortfall: calcium 10,000 g x (0.005 - 0.003) = 20 g; phosphorus 10,000 x (0.0025 - 0.0018) = 7 g.
  2. Dicalcium phosphate at roughly 18 percent phosphorus and 23 percent calcium: 7 / 0.18 = 38.9 g per cow per day, carrying 38.9 x 0.23 = 8.9 g of calcium.
  3. Remaining calcium: 20 - 8.9 = 11.1 g. Limestone at about 38 percent calcium: 11.1 / 0.38 = 29.2 g.
  4. Magnesium at 0.22 percent target: 10,000 x 0.0022 = 22 g. If forage supplies 0.12 percent, that is 12 g, so the shortfall is 10 g. Magnesium oxide at about 54 percent Mg: 10 / 0.54 = 18.5 g.
  5. Salt for sodium at 0.10 percent: 10 g of sodium needed; sodium chloride is about 39 percent sodium, so 10 / 0.39 = 25.6 g.
  6. Trace premix: use a commercial trace pack at the manufacturer's stated inclusion, typically a small fraction of the total; assume 3 g per cow per day.
  7. Running total: 38.9 + 29.2 + 18.5 + 25.6 + 3 = 115.2 g. Slightly over the 100 g target, so either raise the daily allowance to 115 g or reduce the calcium ambition to the lower end of the range.

Now scale to a 100 kg batch. The proportions are 38.9 / 115.2 = 33.8 percent DCP, 25.3 percent limestone, 16.1 percent magnesium oxide, 22.2 percent salt and 2.6 percent trace premix. So a 100 kg batch is 33.8 kg DCP, 25.3 kg limestone, 16.1 kg magnesium oxide, 22.2 kg salt and 2.6 kg trace premix.

Cost it. At assumed local prices of 0.55, 0.12, 0.60, 0.20 and 3.50 USD per kg respectively: (33.8 x 0.55) + (25.3 x 0.12) + (16.1 x 0.60) + (22.2 x 0.20) + (2.6 x 3.50) = 18.59 + 3.04 + 9.66 + 4.44 + 9.10 = 44.83 USD per 100 kg, or 0.448 USD per kg. At 115 g per cow per day that is 0.052 USD per head per day. If a commercial equivalent costs more than about 0.45 USD per kg at the same guaranteed analysis, mixing your own pays; if it costs less, buy it and spend the time elsewhere.

Three safety checks before any premix is fed. First, run every trace element against its maximum tolerable level using total diet dry matter, not premix weight. Copper is the dangerous one because the requirement is 10 ppm and the ceiling only 40 ppm, a fourfold margin. Second, account for antagonists: molybdenum above 5 ppm binds copper, dietary sulphur above 0.20 percent binds copper as sulphide, and zinc above 500 ppm inhibits copper absorption, so high-sulphate borehole water is part of the calculation. Third, check that mineral source affects availability: oxide, sulphate and proteinate forms are not equivalent, and published requirement tables assume inorganic sources.

Mixing matters as much as formulation. Minerals are fed in grams against a ration measured in kilograms, so poor mixing means some animals get several times the intended dose and others get none. Mix the trace premix into a carrier such as maize bran first, then blend that into the macro minerals, then into the ration. Never top-dress a loose trace premix onto silage and hope.

Concrete African context. A cattle group in Chinsali, Zambia, was buying a low-cost mineral block that its label showed contained salt and little else, at a price close to that of a fully fortified product. Switching to a home mix of DCP, limestone, salt and a bought trace pack raised the cost per head per day by roughly two cents and delivered magnesium and selenium that the block had never contained.

Read the guaranteed analysis, do the shortfall arithmetic, and check the ceiling before you check the price.

Lesson 7.5~13 min

Least-Cost Formulation

In this lesson
  • Rank ingredients by cost per unit of the nutrient that is actually limiting your ration
  • Substitute one ingredient for another without breaking a nutritional or safety constraint
  • Set the constraint list that any least-cost solution must respect before price is considered

Least-cost formulation means meeting a specification at the lowest price, not buying the cheapest ingredients. The difference is enormous. The cheapest bag on the market can raise your cost per kilogram of gain, and a dear-looking cake can lower it. The method is to price nutrients rather than feeds, and then to let the constraints decide what is allowed.

Step one is always the constraint list, written before any price is looked at. For a cattle ration the constraints are:

  1. Crude protein at or above the published requirement for the class, and never below 7.5 percent of diet dry matter.
  2. Energy, expressed as TDN or net energy, at or above requirement.
  3. Total diet NDF of 25 to 38 percent, with forage NDF at least 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter.
  4. Concentrate no more than 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day.
  5. Byproduct inclusion caps: distillers grains 50 percent of diet dry matter, wet corn gluten feed 25 to 30 percent, soybean hulls 15 to 20 percent, whole cottonseed 25 percent and no more than 0.5 percent of body weight, wet brewers grains 30 to 40 percent, beet pulp 15 to 20 percent, and byproducts no more than 50 percent of the concentrate fraction.
  6. Calcium 0.35 to 0.65 percent and phosphorus 0.25 to 0.35 percent for lactating cattle at a 2.0 to 2.5 to 1 ratio, with all trace elements below their maximum tolerable levels.
  7. Total intake at or below capacity, from DMI as a percentage of body weight = 120 / NDF%.

Any solution that breaks one of those is not cheap; it is a future veterinary bill.

Step two is nutrient pricing. Convert every ingredient to cost per kg of dry matter, then divide by its content of the limiting nutrient.

Worked example: which energy source is cheapest this month? Assumed delivered prices.

  1. Maize bran at 0.24 USD/kg as fed, 88 percent dry matter, 78 percent TDN. Cost per kg DM: 0.24 / 0.88 = 0.273. Cost per kg TDN: 0.273 / 0.78 = 0.350 USD.
  2. Molasses at 0.45 USD/kg as fed, 75 percent dry matter, 72 percent TDN. Cost per kg DM: 0.45 / 0.75 = 0.600. Cost per kg TDN: 0.600 / 0.72 = 0.833 USD.
  3. Wet brewers grains at 0.09 USD/kg as fed, 24 percent dry matter, 70 percent TDN. Cost per kg DM: 0.09 / 0.24 = 0.375. Cost per kg TDN: 0.375 / 0.70 = 0.536 USD.
  4. Home-made hay at 68 USD per tonne of dry matter, 61 percent TDN. Cost per kg TDN: 0.068 / 0.61 = 0.111 USD.

The ranking is unambiguous: your own hay is the cheapest energy by a factor of three, then maize bran, then brewers grains, then molasses. That single table explains why the highest-return investment on most cattle farms is better forage, not a better concentrate. It also tells you that molasses should be bought as a palatability and fermentation aid, not as an energy source.

Step three is substitution, done one ingredient at a time with the constraint list open.

Worked example: replacing part of the cake. A lactating cow ration uses 1.9 kg of cottonseed cake at 0.42 USD per kg, costing 0.80 USD per day, and supplies 0.76 kg of crude protein.

  1. Wet brewers grains at 24 percent dry matter and 25 percent crude protein of dry matter would need, to replace 0.30 kg of that protein, 0.30 / 0.25 = 1.2 kg of dry matter, which as fed is 1.2 / 0.24 = 5.0 kg.
  2. Cost: 5.0 x 0.09 = 0.45 USD, against the 0.30 / 0.40 = 0.75 kg of cake it displaces, worth 0.75 x 0.42 = 0.32 USD.
  3. So brewers grains are dearer here for this protein, by 0.13 USD per cow per day.
  4. But check the whole picture. The 1.2 kg of brewers grains dry matter also brings roughly 1.2 x 0.70 = 0.84 kg of TDN, against 0.75 x 0.70 = 0.53 kg from the displaced cake, a net gain of 0.31 kg of TDN. If the ration was energy-tight, as it was in the previous lesson, that energy may be worth more than the 13 cents.
  5. Constraint check: 1.2 kg of brewers grains dry matter in an 8.5 kg total intake is 14 percent, well inside the 30 to 40 percent cap, and inside the 30 percent palatability threshold.

That is what least-cost work actually looks like. Not a single answer, but a series of small substitutions each tested against price, against the limiting nutrient, and against the constraint list.

Two practical warnings. First, prices move and rankings move with them; a least-cost ration is a monthly exercise, not a permanent formula. Second, ingredient composition varies, and a cheap ingredient that has been adulterated is not cheap at all. Maize bran diluted with ground cob and cottonseed cake diluted with hulls are both common; a laboratory crude protein on a suspicious delivery costs less than one wasted month.

Concrete African context. A commercial dairy in Kiambu County, Kenya, keeps a one-page spreadsheet with a row for each available ingredient and columns for as-fed price, dry matter, crude protein, TDN, cost per kg of dry matter, cost per kg of crude protein and cost per kg of TDN. It takes ten minutes to update when prices change and it has repeatedly caught situations where the familiar ingredient was no longer the right buy. No software was involved.

Set the constraints, price the nutrients, substitute one thing at a time, and re-run it every month.

Feed Record Management

In this lesson
  • Keep a batch sheet that records exactly what went into every mix and when
  • Reconcile feed purchased against feed fed and identify shrink and theft
  • Calibrate a mixer or a measuring container so that the written ration is the ration delivered

A ration exists on paper. What the animals eat is whatever was actually weighed, mixed and delivered. The gap between the two is where most of the value of formulation is lost, and closing it needs only three cheap records: a batch sheet, a stock reconciliation and a calibration check.

The batch sheet is the first. Every time you mix, write one line per ingredient with the weight actually used, plus the batch date, the batch size, who mixed it and which group it is for. This takes two minutes and does four jobs: it proves the mix was correct when performance is questioned, it identifies which batch an animal was on if something goes wrong, it allows you to reconcile stock, and it stops the slow drift where one scoop becomes one heaped scoop over six months.

Worked example of the drift problem. A ration calls for 1.0 kg of cottonseed cake per cow per day, measured with an unmarked tin. If the tin actually holds 1.25 kg when heaped, then across 20 cows for 300 days you use 20 x 300 x 0.25 = 1,500 kg of extra cake. At 0.42 USD per kg that is 630 USD of unbudgeted cost, and nobody in the operation can see it because the ration on the wall still says 1.0 kg. Weigh the tin once, mark the correct level with a paint line, and the problem disappears permanently.

The second record is stock reconciliation. At the start of each month record opening stock of every ingredient, add purchases, subtract what the batch sheets say was used, and compare against a physical count of what is in the store.

  1. Opening stock of maize bran: 900 kg.
  2. Purchases during the month: 1,500 kg.
  3. Batch sheets show usage: 1,720 kg.
  4. Expected closing stock: 900 + 1,500 - 1,720 = 680 kg.
  5. Physical count: 610 kg.
  6. Unexplained difference: 70 kg, which is 4.1 percent of throughput.

Now interpret it. Published guidance is to account for storage losses of about 3 percent on dry feed and 15 percent on high-moisture feeds. A 4.1 percent loss on a dry ingredient is slightly above the expected shrink, so the first month it appears you check for spillage, rodents and a torn store roof. If it persists at 8 or 10 percent, you have a different problem and you now have the evidence to act on it. Without the reconciliation, feed simply disappears and everyone blames the price.

The third record is calibration. Published quality control guidance for total mixed rations includes testing mix consistency weekly by calibrating the mixer. On a small farm this means confirming that your scale reads true against a known weight, that your measuring containers hold what you think they hold, and that a mix looks uniform rather than layered. Do it on the same day each week and write down that you did it.

Four things every feed record system must capture:

  1. What was mixed, in what weights, on what date, by whom, for which group.
  2. What was purchased, from whom, at what price, in what quantity, with the delivery date and any lot or batch number on the bag.
  3. What was fed to which group each day, and any refusals.
  4. What analysis you hold on each forage and ingredient, with its sampling date.

The second of those has a compliance function as well as a commercial one. Aflatoxin is limited to 20 micrograms per kg in feed and 0.5 micrograms per kg in milk, with 2.0 to 6.2 percent of intake transferring to milk in dairy cattle. If a milk buyer ever raises an aflatoxin result, the only thing that will protect you is a purchase record showing which supplier and which lot the maize by-product came from. The same logic applies to fumonisin at an FDA guidance level of 15 mg per kg for lactating dairy and breeding cattle, and to deoxynivalenol at 5 mg per kg in grains making up no more than 40 percent of the diet. Keep suppliers' delivery notes; staple them to the month's records.

Storage discipline sits alongside records. Keep feed dry at below 12 to 14 percent moisture, rotate stock so the oldest is used first, and inspect visually for discoloured grain, mould growth and off odours before every mix. A record that says a batch was mixed from a delivery you had already rejected on sight is worth more than a laboratory report received three weeks later.

Worked example of what records buy you. A dairy notices milk down 8 percent over ten days. The batch sheets show the concentrate was mixed correctly. The purchase records show a new maize bran supplier started nine days earlier. The forage analysis file shows no change. That is a one-hour diagnosis instead of a month of guesswork, and the corrective action is to test the new bran and revert to the old supplier while waiting.

Concrete African context. A 30-cow dairy in Mukono District, Uganda, keeps a single hardback exercise book in the feed store with three ruled sections: mixes, purchases and monthly stock counts. It costs about a dollar and takes five minutes a day. When the farm applied for a bank loan to expand, that book was the only production evidence it had, and it was accepted.

Write it down at the time, in the store, in one book. Records reconstructed from memory at month end are worth nothing.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Cottonseed cake costs 0.42 USD/kg as fed at 90% dry matter. What is the cost per kg of dry matter?

0.42 / 0.90 = 0.467 USD per kg of dry matter.

2. What is the maximum inclusion of distillers dried grains in cattle diets?

Up to 50% of diet dry matter; above that sulphur toxicity becomes a risk and the phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting.

3. Why can the cheapest feed per bag be the most expensive protein?

Wet feeds such as brewers grains carry a lot of water; on a dry matter basis their cost per kg of crude protein can exceed that of a dearer-looking cake.

4. How much cottonseed meal may a breeding bull receive daily?

Bulls are limited to 3 to 5 lb per day of cottonseed meal, and it should be withdrawn two to three months before the breeding season because of gossypol.

5. What is the approximate dry matter content of dry hay used in ration calculations?

Dry hay is treated as roughly 88% dry matter, against 28 to 35% for silage.

6. What crude protein does a pre-weaning calf at 100 to 150 kg require?

Pre-weaning calves need 16 to 18% crude protein of diet dry matter for optimal gain, falling to 14 to 16% post-weaning.

7. A 400 kg steer eats hay at 60% NDF. How much dry matter can he consume daily?

DMI = 120 / 60 = 2.0% of body weight; 400 x 0.02 = 8.0 kg.

8. What is the crude protein target for 600 kg finishing cattle?

Finishing cattle at 600 kg gaining 2.0 to 3.5 lb/day need 8.5 to 9.5% crude protein of diet dry matter.

9. A ration will not balance for energy. What should you check first?

Intake capacity, calculated as 120 / NDF%, caps the ration; if the forage is too fibrous, no ingredient change can deliver the required energy.

10. How much TDN does a 500 lb steer gaining 1.5 lb/day need daily?

The published requirement is 8.0 lb of TDN per day at 59.5% of diet dry matter.

11. Using cottonseed cake at 40% CP and maize bran at 10% CP, what proportion of cake gives a 16% mix?

Cake parts = 16 - 10 = 6; bran parts = 40 - 16 = 24; total 30; cake = 6/30 = 20%.

12. In Pearson's Square, where does the target value go?

The target goes in the middle, the two ingredient values on the left corners, and the diagonal subtractions give the parts on the right.

13. Why must an energy check follow every protein balance?

Pearson's Square balances one nutrient only; a mix can meet the crude protein target and still fail to deliver the required TDN.

14. A concentrate must supply 0.82 kg of crude protein in 2.5 kg. What protein percentage is needed?

0.82 / 2.5 = 0.328, which is 32.8% crude protein in the concentrate portion.

15. What is the absolute minimum diet crude protein for any class of cattle?

7.5% of diet dry matter is the lowest level used in NRC recommendations and the floor below which rumen function is compromised.

16. How much dicalcium phosphate supplies 7 g of phosphorus at 18% P?

7 / 0.18 = 38.9 g of dicalcium phosphate.

17. Which trace mineral has the narrowest margin between requirement and tolerable level?

Copper requirement is 10 ppm against a maximum tolerable level of 40 ppm, only a fourfold margin, against 30 versus 500 ppm for zinc.

18. Why must the trace premix be blended into a carrier first?

Minerals are fed in grams against a ration measured in kilograms, so a carrier such as maize bran is needed to distribute them evenly.

19. High-sulphate borehole water matters because sulphur above 0.20% of the diet does what?

Dietary sulphur above 0.20% binds copper as sulphide, reducing availability even when copper supplementation looks adequate.

20. What calcium to phosphorus ratio should a finished premix deliver in the total diet?

The published optimum is 2.0 to 2.5 to 1 calcium to phosphorus in the total diet.

21. Maize bran costs 0.24 USD/kg at 88% DM and 78% TDN. What is the cost per kg of TDN?

0.24 / 0.88 = 0.273 USD per kg DM; 0.273 / 0.78 = 0.350 USD per kg of TDN.

22. What should be written before any price is considered?

The nutritional and safety constraint list defines what solutions are permissible; price only chooses among the ones that already qualify.

23. Why is molasses a poor choice as a primary energy source?

On a cost per kg of TDN basis molasses is expensive; it earns its place as a palatability and fermentation aid, not as bulk energy.

24. How often should a least-cost ration be recalculated?

Prices and compositions move, so a least-cost ration is a repeated monthly exercise rather than a fixed formula.

25. What is the risk with an unusually cheap bag of maize bran or cottonseed cake?

Maize bran diluted with ground cob and cake diluted with hulls are common; a laboratory crude protein test on a suspicious delivery is cheap insurance.

26. An unmarked tin holds 1.25 kg instead of the intended 1.0 kg. Across 20 cows for 300 days, how much extra feed is used?

20 x 300 x 0.25 kg = 1,500 kg of unbudgeted extra feed.

27. What shrink should you expect on stored dry feed?

Published guidance is to account for about 3% loss on dry feed and about 15% on high-moisture feeds.

28. Why must purchase records include supplier and lot details?

If an aflatoxin result appears in milk, purchase records identifying the supplier and lot are the only way to trace and act on the source.

29. How often should mix consistency be checked?

Published quality control guidance for total mixed rations is to test mix consistency weekly by calibrating the mixer.

30. Milk drops 8% and batch sheets show correct mixing. What record is checked next?

A recent supplier or ingredient change is the most likely explanation once the mix itself has been verified as correct.

Module 7 capstone

Build and Cost Your Own Ration Book. Step 1: list every feed ingredient available within reasonable transport distance of your farm - maize bran, wheat bran, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, brewers grains, molasses, maize stover, urea-treated straw, hay, silage, limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, magnesium oxide and a trace premix - and get a real delivered price in USD per kg for each. Step 2: record the dry matter, crude protein and either ADF or a supplier TDN figure for each, using a laboratory analysis where you can afford one and clearly marking every figure you had to estimate from published typical values. Step 3: convert each ingredient to cost per kg of dry matter, cost per kg of crude protein and cost per kg of TDN, and rank them. Step 4: choose four target animals - a growing steer, a late-gestation cow, a lactating cow and a breeding bull - and write down each one's published crude protein and energy requirement. Step 5: formulate a ration for each using Pearson's Square for the protein balance and a TDN check for the energy balance, respecting the byproduct inclusion limits: brewers grains no more than 30 to 40 percent of diet dry matter, whole cottonseed no more than 25 percent and no more than 0.5 percent of body weight daily, cottonseed meal for bulls limited to 3 to 5 lb per day, and concentrate no more than 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight. Step 6: build one mineral premix that brings the whole herd to a 2.0 to 2.5 to 1 calcium to phosphorus ratio and check every trace element against its maximum tolerable level. Step 7: write each finished ration on its own page with ingredient quantities, cost per head per day and the date, and keep the pages in a single ration book that you update whenever a price or a forage analysis changes.
