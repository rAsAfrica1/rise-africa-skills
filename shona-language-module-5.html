1. Common Foods and Ingredients

In Shona, common foods and ingredients are the building blocks of daily meals. Start with staples: sadza (thick maize meal porridge), muriwo (vegetables like covo, rape, or spinach), nyama (meat, often beef or goat), and hove (fish, especially dried kapenta). Learn these words: chibage (maize), mupunga (rice), bhinzi (beans), mbatatisi (potatoes), and miriwo yemusango (wild greens). For fruits: muchero (fruit), manzairazi (banana), maapuro (apple), and mazambiringa (grapes).

When shopping at a local market like Mbare in Harare or a village market, you'll hear: 'Ndinoda kutenga sadza' (I want to buy sadza). Prices are often in USD or ZiG. A 2kg bag of maize meal costs about $2. A bunch of covo is $0.50. A kilogram of beef is $5. Learn to ask: 'Imarii?' (How much?) and 'Ndipeiwo...' (Give me please...).

Cooking starts with ingredients. Common flavor bases: hanyanisi (onion), tii yemusango (wild tea), and mhiripiri (chili). Oil is mafuta. Salt is munyu. Water is mvura. You'll often hear 'Wedzera munyu' (Add salt) or 'Cheka hanyanisi' (Cut onion).

Practice by labeling your kitchen items in Shona. Say 'Ndinodya sadza nemuriwo' (I eat sadza with vegetables). At a market, point and ask 'Chii ichi?' (What is this?). The vendor might say 'Ichi chibage' (This is maize). Repeat back to remember.

A typical meal includes sadza, a protein, and a vegetable. For breakfast, many eat bota (thin porridge) with milk and sugar. Lunch might be leftover sadza with nyama. Dinner is the main meal. Knowing these words helps you cook, shop, and share meals with Shona speakers.

2. Ordering Food in a Restaurant

Ordering food in a Shona-speaking restaurant is straightforward if you know key phrases. Start with 'Ndinoda kudya' (I want to eat). To see a menu, ask 'Ndiratidzei menyu' (Show me the menu). In many local eateries, there is no menu; the waiter will tell you what's available: 'Tine sadza nenyama' (We have sadza and meat).

When ordering, use 'Ndipeiwo...' (Give me please...) followed by the dish. For example: 'Ndipeiwo sadza nenyama yehuku' (Give me sadza and chicken). To ask for a drink: 'Ndinoda kunwa' (I want to drink). Common drinks: mvura (water), tii (tea), kofi (coffee), and mahewu (fermented maize drink). Say 'Ndipeiwo mvura' (Give me water).

If you have dietary restrictions, say 'Handidye nyama' (I don't eat meat) or 'Handidye chikafu chine mukaka' (I don't eat dairy). For spicy food, ask 'Chine mhiripiri here?' (Does it have chili?). To ask for no chili: 'Usaise mhiripiri' (Don't put chili).

After the meal, ask for the bill: 'Ndipeiwo bhiri' (Give me the bill). The waiter may say 'Imarii?' (How much?). You can pay with cash or mobile money like EcoCash. Tipping is not mandatory but appreciated; 10% is fine. Say 'Ndatenda' (Thank you) when leaving.

Practice with a friend: one plays waiter, the other customer. Use real prices: a plate of sadza nenyama costs $3-$5 in Harare. A soft drink is $1. Remember to say 'Ndine nzara' (I am hungry) and 'Ndirikuda kudya' (I am about to eat). These phrases make dining out smooth.

3. Meals of the Day

Shona meals follow a daily rhythm. Breakfast (kudya kwemangwanani) is light. Many eat bota (thin maize porridge) with milk and sugar, or chingwa (bread) with tii. Some eat leftover sadza. You might say 'Ndinodya bota mangwanani' (I eat porridge in the morning).

Lunch (kudya kwemasikati) is often a packed meal for workers or students. It could be sadza nemuriwo (sadza with vegetables) or rice nebhiza (rice and beans). In towns, people buy from street vendors: a plate of sadza nenyama for $2. Ask 'Mangwanani akanaka here?' (Did you have a good morning?) as a greeting before eating.

Dinner (kudya kwemanheru) is the main meal, eaten after sunset. Families gather for sadza, nyama (meat), and muriwo. The father or eldest may be served first. Say 'Ndinodya sadza nenyama manheru' (I eat sadza and meat in the evening). Before eating, some say 'Ndinokutenda' (I thank you) or 'Chikafu chakanaka' (Good food).

Snacks (zvokudya zvidiki) include mhunga (millet), nzungu (groundnuts), and mazhanje (wild fruit). Children often eat mapa (puffed maize) after school. At a tuckshop, ask 'Ndipeiwo mapa' (Give me mapa).

Meal times vary: breakfast 6-8 AM, lunch 12-2 PM, dinner 7-9 PM. In rural areas, dinner may be later. Learn to ask 'Wadya here?' (Have you eaten?) as a caring greeting. Respond 'Ndadya' (I have eaten) or 'Handisati ndadya' (I haven't eaten yet). These phrases show cultural respect.

4. Cooking Verbs and Instructions

Cooking in Shona uses action verbs. Key verbs: kubika (to cook), kucheka (to cut), kusasa (to fry), kubika nechero (to boil), kubika muchoto (to roast), kusanganisa (to mix), and kudurura (to pour). Learn commands: 'Cheka hanyanisi' (Cut the onion), 'Sasa nyama' (Fry the meat), 'Bika sadza' (Cook sadza), 'Sanganisa zvinhu' (Mix the ingredients).

To make sadza: boil water (mvura inopisa). Add maize meal (upfu) slowly while stirring with a mukombe (wooden spoon). Stir hard (kurunga) until thick. Cover and simmer for 10 minutes. Say 'Bika sadza kwemaminitsi gumi' (Cook sadza for 10 minutes). For muriwo: wash vegetables (suka muriwo), cut (cheka), then fry with onion and tomato (sasa nehanyanisi nematimati). Add salt (wedzera munyu).

For nyama: cut meat into pieces (cheka nyama). Boil with salt and onion until tender (bika kusvika yapfava). Then fry (sasa) or add to stew (gadzira). A common instruction: 'Bika nyama kwemaawa maviri' (Cook meat for two hours). Use a pot (poti) on a stove (chitofu) or open fire (mot o).

When following a recipe, listen for sequence words: kutanga (first), ipapo (then), mushure (after), uye (and), pakupedzisira (finally). Example: 'Kutanga, cheka hanyanisi. Ipapo, sasa nyama. Mushure, wedzera madzi.' (First, cut onion. Then, fry meat. After, add gravy.)

Practice by cooking a simple meal. Narrate each step in Shona: 'Ndiri kucheka muriwo. Ndiri kusasa hanyanisi. Ndiri kubika sadza.' This builds fluency. Ask a Shona speaker to correct your pronunciation. Common mistakes: mixing up kubika (cook) and kubikisa (cook for someone).

5. Expressing Likes and Dislikes

To express likes in Shona, use 'Ndinoda' (I like) or 'Ndinofarira' (I enjoy). For dislikes: 'Handidi' (I don't like) or 'Handifariri' (I don't enjoy). For example: 'Ndinoda sadza nenyama' (I like sadza and meat). 'Handidi muriwo' (I don't like vegetables). To ask someone's preference: 'Unoda chii?' (What do you like?) or 'Unofarira chikafu chipi?' (Which food do you enjoy?)

Use 'zvikuru' (very much) to intensify: 'Ndinoda sadza zvikuru' (I like sadza very much). To say 'a little', use 'zvishoma': 'Ndinoda muriwo zvishoma' (I like vegetables a little). For neutral: 'Ndinodawo' (I also like). To say 'I love', use 'Ndinoda zvakanyanya' (I like extremely).

When someone offers food, accept with 'Ndatenda, ndinoda' (Thank you, I like it). Decline politely: 'Ndatenda, asi handidi' (Thank you, but I don't like it). If you are allergic, say 'Ndinorwara kana ndadya...' (I get sick if I eat...). For example: 'Ndinorwara kana ndadya nzungu' (I get sick if I eat groundnuts).

Common food preferences: 'Ndinoda huku' (I like chicken). 'Handidi hove' (I don't like fish). 'Ndinofarira mahewu' (I enjoy mahewu). 'Handifariri tii' (I don't enjoy tea). Practice by making a list of foods you like and dislike in Shona. Share with a partner: 'Ndinoda X, handidi Y.'

Cultural note: In Shona culture, it's polite to accept a little of what is offered, even if you don't like it. Say 'Ndinoda zvishoma' (I like a little) to avoid offending. If you truly can't eat, explain why. Use 'Ndinotenda' (I am grateful) to show respect. These phrases help you navigate meals with confidence.

6. Traditional Shona Dishes

Traditional Shona dishes are hearty and based on local ingredients. The national staple is sadza, made from maize meal (upfu). It is eaten with muriwo (vegetables), nyama (meat), or hove (fish). Another staple is bota, a thin porridge often eaten for breakfast. For a main meal, sadza is served with a relish called 'muriwo unedovi' (vegetables with peanut butter).

Popular dishes: 'Muriwo nedovi' (vegetables with peanut butter) covo or rape cooked with groundnut paste. 'Nyama yemombe' (beef stew) slow-cooked with tomatoes and onions. 'Huku yemusango' (free-range chicken) boiled then fried, often served at celebrations. 'Mazondo' (cow hooves) a delicacy, boiled until tender. 'Muboora' (pumpkin leaves) cooked with peanut butter. 'Chikafu chemumusha' (village food) includes these.

For special occasions, 'nyama yechin' (roast meat) is grilled over open fire. 'Sadza rezviyo' (finger millet sadza) is healthier and has a nutty taste. 'Mahewu' is a fermented maize drink, refreshing and slightly sour. 'Dovi' (peanut butter) is used in many dishes, adding richness.

Cooking methods: boiling (kubika), frying (kusasa), roasting (kugocha), and steaming (kupisa). Traditional cooking uses a three-legged pot (hari) over firewood. Modern kitchens use stoves. To make 'muriwo nedovi': boil vegetables, then mix with dovi and salt. To make 'mazondo': boil for 3-4 hours with salt and onion until soft.

Learn to say: 'Ndinoda sadza nemuriwo nedovi' (I like sadza with vegetables and peanut butter). 'Ndapedza kudya' (I have finished eating). 'Chikafu ichi chinonaka' (This food is delicious). Try cooking one dish this week. Visit a local market, buy ingredients, and follow a recipe in Shona. Share with friends and ask for feedback: 'Chinonaka here?' (Is it delicious?).

Practical Reference: Food and Dining in Shona for Agribusiness & Hospitality

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module Food and Dining within the Shona Language course. It is designed for agribusiness professionals, food traders, hospitality operators, and market vendors who interact daily with Shona-speaking farmers, customers, and suppliers. You will find vocabulary tables, a pronunciation diagram, a safety warning on food handling, a quick reference checklist, and links to further reading. All content is oriented toward commercial application: buying produce at Mbare Musika, negotiating prices with smallholder farmers, serving customers in restaurants, and describing dishes to tourists or urban buyers.

4. Cooking Verbs and Instructions

Whether you are training kitchen staff, writing a recipe for a food product, or explaining processing steps to a cooperative, these verbs are essential. Shona cooking instructions typically use the imperative form for direct commands and the habitual for general practice.

  • Kubika to cook. Bika sadza. (Cook sadza.)
  • Kudya to eat. Dya chikafu. (Eat the food.)
  • Kunwa to drink. Nwa mvura. (Drink water.)
  • Kucheka to cut. Cheka nyama. (Cut the meat.)
  • Kukanya to stir. Kanya sadza. (Stir the sadza.)
  • Kubata to hold. Bata poto. (Hold the pot.)
  • Kuisa to put. Isa munyu. (Put salt.)
  • Kuwedzera to add. Wedzera mvura. (Add water.)
  • Kubvisa to remove. Bvisa pamoto. (Remove from fire.)
  • Kunatsa to clean. Natsa ndiro. (Clean the plate.)

Source: Verb list adapted from Duramazwi reChiShona (Chimhundu, 1996) and the SADC harmonised orthography for Zimbabwean languages.

Safety Warning: Food Handling and Language Misunderstanding

In commercial food operations, miscommunication about cooking verbs can lead to food safety risks. For example, kubika (to cook) and kubikisa (to cause to cook / overcook) are distinct. Confusing kuisa munyu (add salt) with kuisa mushonga (add medicine/chemical) can be dangerous. Always confirm instructions verbally and in writing when dealing with pesticides, preservatives, or cleaning agents. The FAO and WHO recommend the "five keys to safer food" keep clean, separate raw and cooked, cook thoroughly, keep food at safe temperatures, and use safe water and raw materials. In Shona: Chengeta chikafu zvakanaka. (Keep food well.)

5. Expressing Likes and Dislikes

Customer preference data is gold for product development. When you ask Shona-speaking consumers about taste, use these structures:

  • Ndinoda ... I like ... Ndinoda sadza nenyama.
  • Handidi ... I do not like ... Handidi muriwo une munyu wakawanda. (I do not like vegetables with too much salt.)
  • Ndinofarira ... I prefer / enjoy ... Ndinofarira hove. (I enjoy fish.)
  • Ini ndinoda ... As for me, I like ... (emphasis)
  • Zvinonaka It is delicious. Chikafu ichi zvinonaka.
  • Zvinovava It is bitter / salty. Muriwo uyu zvinovava.
  • Zvinonhuwirira It smells good.

In market research, pair these with scale questions: Unoda here? (Do you like it?) and Ungatenge here? (Would you buy it?). This links preference directly to purchase intent.

6. Traditional Shona Dishes for Commercial Menus

Traditional dishes are a growing segment in Zimbabwe's urban food scene and export markets. The following are the most commercially relevant:

  • Sadza thick maize meal porridge, the national staple. Served with muriwo (greens), nyama (meat), or hove (fish).
  • Muriwo unedovi greens cooked with peanut butter. High protein, popular in urban restaurants.
  • Mazondo slow-cooked cow hooves. A delicacy with strong demand in beer gardens and events.
  • Muboora pumpkin leaves, often dried and sold in markets. Good shelf life for export.
  • Nyemba cowpeas / black-eyed peas. Important legume for nutrition and soil health.
  • Maheu traditional non-alcoholic fermented maize drink. Growing in formal retail.
  • Dovi peanut butter, used as a base for many dishes and a standalone product.

Source: Dishes documented in Traditional Zimbabwean Cookery (Zimbabwe Women's Bureau, 1985) and updated with market data from the Zimbabwe National Statistics Agency (ZIMSTAT) food consumption surveys.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Greet before business. In Shona culture, start with Mangwanani (good morning) or Masikati (good afternoon) before discussing food or prices.
  2. Use the right tone. Practice the H-L and L-L-H patterns in Figure 1 to avoid misquoting prices or ingredients.
  3. Confirm cooking instructions twice. Especially for salt, sugar, and chemicals. Ask Wanzwisisa here? (Do you understand?).
  4. Know your staple terms. Sadza, muriwo, nyama, hove, mukaka, chibage these cover 80% of market conversations.
  5. Link preference to purchase. Ask Unoda here? then Ungatenge here? to validate demand.
  6. Respect food safety language. Never confuse munyu (salt) with mushonga (medicine/chemical).
  7. Offer mobile money options. Use Ndinogona kubhadhara ne mobile money here? to close sales efficiently.
  8. Document traditional dishes. Keep a bilingual menu or product sheet with Shona and English descriptions for export and tourism markets.
