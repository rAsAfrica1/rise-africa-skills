Lesson 12.1~12 min

Business Planning and Market Research

In this lesson
  • Collect real local market data instead of estimating demand
  • Calculate a break-even volume from your own fixed and variable costs
  • Choose one product and one customer to launch with

Most small pet feed businesses fail on arithmetic, not on nutrition. The formulation is usually adequate. The volume needed to cover the rent is what nobody worked out.

Start with counting rather than estimating. There is no reliable published dog and cat population figure for most African cities, and our verified reference set contains none, so do not build a plan on a number you cannot source. Count instead. Walk the pet shops, agrovets and supermarkets within your delivery radius and write down every dog and cat food on the shelf: brand, origin, pack size, retail price, and price per kilogram. Then sit outside a busy veterinary clinic for two mornings and ask owners what they feed, what it costs them a month and what they cannot get. Fifty owners and twenty shelf products is a small survey, but it is your survey and it is about your town.

That exercise produces the only two numbers that matter at the start: what the market currently pays per kilogram, and what it cannot buy at any price. The gaps are where a small producer wins, because a gap is a market an importer is not serving. Common gaps in African cities include cat food of any kind, small pack sizes for owners who buy weekly rather than monthly, growth diets, and anything at all sold with an honest guaranteed analysis.

Now the break-even arithmetic. Suppose your fixed costs each month are rent USD 150, electricity USD 60, one part-time worker USD 200, and licence, insurance and other annual costs amortised at USD 40, giving USD 450 a month. Suppose ingredients, premix, packaging and delivery come to USD 1.10 per kilogram of finished feed, and you intend to sell to shops at USD 1.85 per kilogram. Contribution per kilogram is 1.85 minus 1.10 = USD 0.75. Break-even volume is fixed costs divided by contribution: 450 divided by 0.75 = 600 kilograms a month. That is your survival line. In 20 kg equivalents it is 30 bags a month, or about seven bags a week, which is a number you can test against your shelf survey before you spend anything.

Run the sensitivity too, because a single price is a fantasy. If shops push you to USD 1.65 per kilogram, contribution falls to USD 0.55 and break-even rises to 450 divided by 0.55 = 818 kilograms a month, a 36 percent increase in the volume you must sell. If an ingredient price rises and your cost goes to USD 1.25, contribution is USD 0.60 and break-even is 750 kilograms. Write those three lines on one page. That page is your business plan's spine.

Decide what you are making before you cost anything further, because the specification drives everything downstream. Dog or cat, adult maintenance or growth and reproduction, dry or wet. The AAFCO adult maintenance minima are 18.0 percent crude protein and 5.5 percent crude fat on a dry matter basis for dogs, and 26.0 percent protein and 9.0 percent fat for cats. Growth and reproduction raises those to 22.5 and 8.5 for dogs and 30.0 and 9.0 for cats. Cat food is harder and more expensive, because cats also require taurine at 0.10 percent of dry matter in extruded food, preformed vitamin A at 3332 IU per kilogram for adults, arachidonic acid at 0.02 percent and niacin at 60 mg per kilogram, which is more than four times the dog minimum of 13.6. But cat food is also the gap nobody is filling in many markets. Know which trade you are making.

A producer in Nairobi, Kenya spent three weekends on exactly this survey before buying anything. She found 22 dog foods on shelves in her radius and three cat foods, all imported, all in 2 kg packs or larger, and priced between USD 2.40 and USD 6.10 per kilogram. Owners told her repeatedly that they bought weekly and in small amounts. She launched with a 1 kg pack of adult dog food at USD 2.20 per kilogram, which nobody else was offering, and hit her 600 kilogram break-even in month five. The survey cost her three weekends and about USD 18 in transport.

Lesson 12.2~12 min

Regulatory Compliance and Labelling

In this lesson
  • Identify your own national regulator and obtain its actual labelling requirements
  • Explain why AAFCO rules are a useful model but not law in African countries
  • Draft a label that states only what you can prove

Everything taught in this course about nutrient profiles comes from AAFCO, the Association of American Feed Control Officials. It is worth being blunt about what that means. AAFCO is a United States model. Its nutrient profiles are the most widely used technical reference in the world and they are a sound basis for formulation anywhere, but AAFCO has no legal force in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, South Africa or anywhere else on the continent. Do not print an AAFCO statement on a bag because a course told you to, and do not assume that meeting AAFCO makes you compliant at home.

African pet food and animal feed rules vary a great deal by country, and they change. Depending on where you are, dog and cat food may be regulated as animal feed, as an agricultural input, under a standards body, under a veterinary authority, or scarcely at all. Bodies you may end up dealing with include the Kenya Bureau of Standards, the Standards Organisation of Nigeria and NAFDAC, the Ghana Standards Authority, the Tanzania Bureau of Standards, and in South Africa the registrar acting under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act. Which one applies to you, what a registration costs, what testing they demand and what wording they require on the pack are questions only your own national regulator can answer. Telephone them, visit them, and write down what they say with the date and the name of the officer. That single conversation is the most valuable hour in this module and it is free.

What travels internationally is the logic of a label rather than its exact wording. In most systems a pet food pack is expected to carry, in some form: the product name and the species and life stage it is for; the net weight; a guaranteed analysis, typically minimum crude protein, minimum crude fat, maximum crude fibre and maximum moisture; a full ingredient list in descending order by weight; feeding directions; your business name and physical address; and a batch code with a date. Confirm every one of those against your own regulator rather than against this list.

The part that carries real risk is the adequacy claim. If you state that a food is complete and balanced for a life stage, you are asserting that it meets a published profile. If you use AAFCO as your reference, then the dog adult maintenance minima of 18.0 percent protein, 5.5 percent fat, 0.5 percent calcium and 0.4 percent phosphorus on a dry matter basis are what you must actually meet, and the cat figures are 26.0 percent protein, 9.0 percent fat, 0.6 percent calcium and 0.5 percent phosphorus. Claim growth and reproduction and the dog figures become 22.5, 8.5, 1.2 and 1.0. Claim growth including large size dogs, defined by AAFCO as those weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, and you take on a calcium maximum of 1.8 percent of dry matter, against 2.5 percent for other life stages. That one sentence has closed more product lines than any other in pet food.

Watch the ceilings, because they differ between systems and a product legal in one place is illegal in another. AAFCO sets the dog vitamin D maximum at 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter, cut from 5000 in 2016. The EU legal limit, tabulated by FEDIAF, is 227 IU per 100 g of dry matter, that is 2270 IU per kilogram, materially below the AAFCO figure. If you ever intend to export, formulate to the tighter of the two.

Cost it honestly. A proximate analysis panel covering moisture, crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre and ash at a commercial or university laboratory typically runs somewhere in the region of USD 60 to USD 120 per sample in African cities, and mineral or amino acid work costs more. Treat those as approximate ranges to verify locally, not as quoted prices. Registration fees vary from nominal to several hundred dollars. Budget for at least one full analysis per formulation per year plus one for any recipe change, and put the receipts in the same file as your batch records.

A producer in Accra, Ghana printed 5000 bags carrying a United States style adequacy statement copied from an imported brand. When she finally spoke to the Ghana Standards Authority she learned the wording her own market expected was different, and the bags were unusable. The reprint cost her USD 640. The telephone call that would have prevented it cost nothing.

Lesson 12.3~12 min

Sourcing Ingredients and Suppliers

In this lesson
  • Select ingredients against published digestibility and inclusion evidence
  • Write a supplier specification that excludes known hazards
  • Cost a real blend from quoted local prices

Ingredient buying is where a pet feed business makes or loses its margin, and where most of the animal risk sits. Two rules govern it. Buy against a written specification, and buy against evidence rather than marketing.

Start with what the evidence actually supports. A Beagle trial of poultry by-products found that bone protein meal could be included up to 12 percent and feather meal up to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores. Digestibility remained acceptable at higher levels, but faecal quality fell at 24 percent bone protein meal and 20 percent feather meal, and stool quality is what your customer sees in the yard. A systematic review found poultry offal meal increases nutrient and energy digestibility and reduces faecal dry matter output compared with soy sources, while explicitly refusing to set a maximum inclusion because the study methods were too heterogeneous. Soybean meal reduced dry matter digestibility in most studies reviewed and reduced gross energy digestibility across most trials, though it raised crude protein digestibility in several. No published maximum inclusion exists for soybean meal, maize or rice. Do not invent one, and be sceptical of anyone who quotes you one.

On starch sources, a six-carbohydrate comparison in extruded dog diets ranked brewer's rice and cassava flour highest for dry matter, organic matter and gross energy digestibility, followed by maize and sorghum, with peas and lentils lowest. Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent across all six diets, which means properly extruded cereal starch is essentially fully digestible in dogs and the claim that dogs cannot digest grain is not supported. For an African producer this is good news, because cassava and maize are locally available and locally priced. Beet pulp at 10 percent inclusion raised dry matter digestibility to 81.3 percent against 77.2 percent for cellulose, but softened stools, so treat it as a moderately fermentable fibre to use carefully.

On alternative proteins, black soldier fly larvae meal is the only insect product to have completed the AAFCO ingredient definition process, tentatively approved in August 2021 for adult dog food only. It is not approved for cats and not for growing dogs, the definition requires drying to no more than 10 percent moisture, and no maximum inclusion is specified. Insect meal is typically around 55 percent protein with average digestibility around 80 percent. Verify the current status before you rely on it, because these approvals move.

Cats constrain your sourcing absolutely. Taurine, preformed vitamin A and arachidonic acid are absent or unavailable from plant ingredients, so a cat formulation must carry animal-derived material and usually supplemental taurine as well. That is not a preference, it is the formulation.

Your supplier specification should exclude hazards by name. Dried onion and garlic are the real formulation risk in this list, because they arrive hidden in flavourings, seasonings and broths. Cats are the most susceptible species, with signs reported from as little as 5 g per kg of raw onion, and garlic is three to five times more toxic than onion. Write into every purchase order that palatants, broths and flavourings must be free of allium in any form, and get it confirmed in writing. Xylitol, chocolate, grapes and raisins and macadamia are mainly contamination and treat risks rather than formulation risks, but a shared mixing floor makes them yours.

Now cost a blend. Suppose in your market maize meal is USD 0.42 per kg, poultry offal meal USD 1.05, cassava flour USD 0.38, soybean meal USD 0.70, poultry fat USD 1.30, and a vitamin and mineral premix USD 4.50. A recipe of 30 percent maize, 25 percent poultry offal meal, 20 percent cassava, 15 percent soybean meal, 8 percent poultry fat and 2 percent premix costs, per kilogram: 0.30 x 0.42 = 0.126, plus 0.25 x 1.05 = 0.263, plus 0.20 x 0.38 = 0.076, plus 0.15 x 0.70 = 0.105, plus 0.08 x 1.30 = 0.104, plus 0.02 x 4.50 = 0.090. Total USD 0.764 per kilogram of ingredients. Add packaging, energy, labour and delivery and you can see how the USD 1.10 variable cost in Lesson 1 is built.

A producer in Kampala, Uganda buying poultry offal meal from two suppliers had every delivery weighed and sampled, and sent one sample per quarter for proximate analysis. One supplier's crude protein varied by more than eight percentage points between deliveries. He kept the other supplier, paid USD 0.06 per kilogram more, and stopped having to reformulate every month.

Formulation, Testing and Refinement

In this lesson
  • Calculate the metabolisable energy of your own formulation step by step
  • Convert an as-fed analysis to a dry matter basis and check it against the profile
  • Plan a testing schedule you can actually afford

A recipe is not a formulation until you know its energy density and its dry matter analysis, because every claim you make and every feeding guide you print depends on those two numbers.

Work through the modified Atwater route on a real example. Suppose your finished dry food analyses at 25 percent crude protein, 12 percent crude fat, 3.5 percent crude fibre, 9 percent moisture, and the nitrogen-free extract plus crude fibre together come to 48 percent, all on an as-fed basis per 100 grams.

  1. Gross energy per 100 g is 5.7 x protein, plus 9.4 x fat, plus 4.1 x the sum of nitrogen-free extract and crude fibre. That is 5.7 x 25 = 142.5, plus 9.4 x 12 = 112.8, plus 4.1 x 48 = 196.8, giving 452.1 kcal per 100 g.
  2. Energy digestibility for dogs is 91.2 minus 1.43 times crude fibre times 100 divided by 100 minus percent moisture. That is 1.43 x 3.5 x 100 = 500.5, divided by 91 = 5.5, so 91.2 minus 5.5 = 85.7 percent.
  3. Digestible energy is 452.1 x 0.857 = 387.4 kcal per 100 g.
  4. Metabolisable energy for dogs is digestible energy minus 1.04 times protein: 387.4 minus 26.0 = 361.4 kcal per 100 g, that is 3614 kcal per kilogram.

For cats the two equations differ: digestibility is 87.9 minus 0.88 times crude fibre times 100 divided by 100 minus moisture, and metabolisable energy is digestible energy minus 0.77 times protein. Use the right pair for the species or your feeding guide will be wrong.

Check the result for sanity. Published dry foods run 2800 to 4800 kcal per kilogram as fed, so 3614 sits comfortably inside the range. Now convert the analysis to dry matter, because the AAFCO profiles are on a dry matter basis and an as-fed figure will mislead you. At 9 percent moisture, dry matter is 91 percent. Protein on a dry matter basis is 25 divided by 0.91 = 27.5 percent. Fat is 12 divided by 0.91 = 13.2 percent. So this food clears the dog adult minimum of 18.0 percent protein, clears the dog growth and reproduction minimum of 22.5 percent, clears the cat adult minimum of 26.0 percent, but fails the cat growth and reproduction minimum of 30.0 percent. One arithmetic step decides which claims you may print.

Do the same for calcium and phosphorus, and check the ratio. Dog adult needs 0.5 and 0.4 percent dry matter, growth needs 1.2 and 1.0, and the ratio must sit between 1:1 and 2:1, with a dog phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent. If you are claiming growth including large size dogs, calcium must not exceed 1.8 percent of dry matter.

Testing is where small producers cut corners and should not. A workable minimum schedule is: one full proximate analysis when a formulation is first made, one after any recipe or supplier change, and one per year thereafter; a taurine assay for any cat food, since the minimum is 0.10 percent of dry matter for extruded food and 0.20 percent for canned; and a retained sample of every batch, sealed, labelled with the batch code and date, and kept in a cool dark place for the shelf life of the product plus a margin. Retained samples cost you almost nothing and are the only way to answer a complaint with evidence rather than opinion. At roughly USD 60 to USD 120 per proximate panel as an approximate local range, four panels a year is a real but bearable cost against a business turning over 600 kilograms a month.

Refinement is done on observation, not on theory. Feed the product to real animals with the owner's consent, and record body weight monthly, body condition, stool score and palatability. Stool quality is the first thing to move when fibre or a rendered meal is too high, which is exactly what the poultry by-product and beet pulp trials showed.

One safety point that belongs in every formulation review. Vitamin D is the nutrient you are most likely to poison an animal with by accident, and real cases come from premix and formulation error rather than from raw ingredients. Check your premix inclusion rate arithmetic twice, keep the supplier's certificate of analysis, and never scale a premix by eye.

A small producer in Bulawayo, Zimbabwe kept a 200 g sealed retained sample from every batch in a labelled box. When a customer complained of loose stools across a whole month, he tested the two retained samples covering that period, found his fibre had drifted upward after a maize supplier change, and corrected the recipe. The complaint cost him two laboratory panels instead of a customer.

Lesson 12.5~12 min

Packaging, Branding and Marketing

In this lesson
  • Cost packaging accurately per kilogram of product sold
  • Build a feeding guide from published energy arithmetic
  • Market without making claims the evidence does not support

Packaging is a nutritional decision before it is a marketing one. Fat oxidises, vitamins degrade with light and heat, and a bag that lets moisture in will grow mould in a humid coastal climate long before the product is eaten. A woven polypropylene sack with a printed laminated outer and an inner liner is the common compromise for small African producers, and small pack sizes matter where customers buy weekly.

Cost it per kilogram, because packaging is where margin quietly disappears. Suppose a printed 5 kg bag costs USD 0.28 delivered, a label USD 0.04, and a heat-seal and code USD 0.01. That is USD 0.33 per bag, which is 0.33 divided by 5 = USD 0.066 per kilogram of product. Now add the one-off print plates at USD 250, amortised over a first run of 20,000 bags: 250 divided by 20,000 = USD 0.0125 per bag, or USD 0.0025 per kilogram. Packaging is therefore roughly USD 0.069 per kilogram, about 9 percent of the USD 0.764 ingredient cost calculated in Lesson 3. If you sell the same food in 1 kg bags, the bag cost per kilogram roughly quadruples, and your small-pack price must reflect that rather than absorb it.

The feeding guide on the pack is the most-read text you will ever print, and it must come from arithmetic rather than from a competitor. Use the energy density you calculated: 3614 kcal per kilogram from the previous lesson. A 20 kg neutered adult dog needs 70 x 20^0.75 x 1.6, that is 662 x 1.6 = 1059 kcal a day, which is 1059 divided by 3614 = 0.293 kg, so 293 grams a day. A 10 kg neutered dog needs 394 x 1.6 = 630 kcal, that is 174 grams. A 4 kg neutered cat needs 198 x 1.2 = 238 kcal, that is 66 grams. Print the table with a clear line saying that these are starting amounts to be adjusted to body condition, because they are derived figures, not published feeding standards.

Now the claims discipline, which is what separates a business that survives a scandal from one that does not. The single most instructive case is the FDA investigation into diet-associated dilated cardiomyopathy. Between January 2014 and 30 April 2019 the FDA received 524 reports, 515 canine and 9 feline, including 119 dog deaths, and named 16 brands by report frequency. On 23 December 2022 the FDA stated it did not intend to release further public updates until there was meaningful new scientific information to share. No causal link between grain-free diets and DCM has been demonstrated, and the FDA itself says adverse event numbers by themselves do not establish causation, calling it a complex scientific issue that may involve multiple factors. A later 18-month prospective study in healthy adult dogs found that different carbohydrate sources supported overall health and cardiac function.

So the honest marketing position is narrow and easy to remember. Do not market grain-free as a health claim. Do not market containing grain as protective either. Do not state or imply that the FDA has cleared or condemned grain-free diets, because neither is true. If you formulate with high pulse inclusion, the defensible things to do are to analyse the finished diet, to ensure generous methionine plus cystine as taurine precursors, and to consider taurine supplementation as cheap insurance even though dogs have no established taurine requirement and none appears in the AAFCO dog profile.

What you can market instead is far more useful in an African market: a real guaranteed analysis, a local address, a batch code, a price per kilogram the customer can compare, a pack size they can afford this week, and a producer who answers the telephone. Those are claims you can prove every day.

A producer in Lilongwe, Malawi launched with a printed feeding table calculated from her own energy figure and a line on the back reading, in plain words, what the food was for and what it was not for. Her retailer reported that shop staff used the table to sell the product because it was the only bag on the shelf that told them how much to feed a 10 kg dog. She spent nothing on advertising in the first year.

Scaling and Long-Term Success

In this lesson
  • Calculate how unit cost falls as volume rises and where the next equipment step is
  • Build a recall-ready record system before you need it
  • Plan working capital and staffing for the next volume step

Growth in a feed business is a series of steps, not a smooth line. Costs per kilogram fall between steps and jump at each one, and the businesses that fail are usually the ones that took a step without the working capital to survive the jump.

See the effect first. With fixed costs of USD 450 a month, at the 600 kilogram break-even those fixed costs are 450 divided by 600 = USD 0.75 per kilogram. At 1500 kilograms a month they are 450 divided by 1500 = USD 0.30. At 3000 kilograms they are USD 0.15. With variable cost at USD 1.10 per kilogram, your total cost per kilogram falls from USD 1.85 to USD 1.40 to USD 1.25 across those three volumes. At a selling price of USD 1.85 you go from break-even to USD 0.45 a kilogram profit to USD 0.60 a kilogram. On 3000 kilograms that is USD 1800 a month of gross profit, which is what pays for the next step.

Now the capacity arithmetic. Suppose a 100 kg mixer and your current drying or extrusion capacity allow six batches a day, which is 600 kilograms a day, and you work 22 days a month. Theoretical capacity is 13,200 kilograms a month, but realistic output after cleaning, changeovers, downtime and quality holds is often 60 to 70 percent of theory, so plan on 8000 to 9200 kilograms. The point is not the specific figure, it is that you should calculate your ceiling before you promise a retailer a volume. A missed delivery to a chain buyer costs more than the order.

Working capital is the thing that kills growing producers. If a retailer pays you in 45 days and your ingredient supplier wants cash on delivery, then every kilogram you sell locks up USD 1.10 for a month and a half. At 3000 kilograms a month that is roughly 3000 x 1.10 x 1.5 = USD 4950 of cash tied up permanently. Growing from 600 to 3000 kilograms a month is therefore not just a production problem, it is a USD 4000 financing problem, and it must appear in your plan before the first big order, not after it.

Records are the second thing that kills growing producers, and this is where a small business should copy large ones rather than dismiss them. Vitamin D formulation errors caused multiple dry food recalls in 2018 across brands from a single contract manufacturer, Hill's issued canned recalls in January, March and May 2019, and warning letters went to Sunshine Mills and to Hill's in 2019. These were companies with laboratories, quality managers and standard procedures, and they still got it wrong. What made recall possible at all was traceability. Your version of that costs almost nothing: a batch code on every bag; a batch record sheet listing date, ingredients, supplier lot numbers, premix batch, quantities, operator and finished weight; a sealed retained sample per batch; and a customer register showing which batch went where. If a problem appears, you can recall one batch instead of everything you have ever sold, and you can prove what was in it.

People are the third. The step from a one-person operation to two or three staff usually happens around the 1500 to 3000 kilogram level, and it is the point at which written procedures stop being bureaucracy and start being the only way the product stays the same. Write the mixing procedure down. Write the cleaning procedure down. Weigh, do not estimate, and never scale a premix by eye, because premix error is exactly the failure mode behind the vitamin D recalls above.

Finally, hold the technical line as you grow. The AAFCO minima do not relax with volume: dog adult 18.0 percent protein and 5.5 percent fat, dog growth 22.5 and 8.5, cat adult 26.0 and 9.0, cat growth 30.0 and 9.0, all on a dry matter basis, with cat taurine at 0.10 percent extruded and 0.20 percent canned. The commercial pressure to shave a formulation arrives precisely when volume rises and margins are being negotiated. The producers who last are the ones whose analysis in year five matches their analysis in year one.

A producer in Mwanza, Tanzania who had grown to about 4000 kilograms a month introduced numbered batch sheets and a customer register after a single complaint she could not trace. Six months later a premix delivery arrived with a different concentration than her usual lot. Because she recorded premix lot numbers per batch, she identified the eleven batches affected, held them, retested and released nine. The paperwork cost her a few minutes per batch and saved the business.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. With USD 450 monthly fixed costs and USD 0.75 contribution per kilogram, what is break-even volume?

450 divided by 0.75 equals 600 kilograms a month. At a lower price of USD 1.65 contribution falls to USD 0.55 and break-even rises to 818 kg.

2. Why should you count shelf products and interview owners rather than estimate market size?

Our verified reference set contains no African pet population figures and none is reliably published for most cities. A counted local survey is data you can defend.

3. Which nutrient minimum is more than four times higher for cats than for dogs?

Cats require 60 mg niacin per kg DM against the dog minimum of 13.6 mg per kg, because cats cannot synthesise substantial niacin from tryptophan.

4. A shop negotiates your price down from USD 1.85 to USD 1.65 per kilogram. What happens?

Contribution per kilogram drops from 0.75 to 0.55, so break-even moves from 600 kg to 818 kg a month. Price concessions must always be converted into volume.

5. What is the strongest opportunity signal in a shelf survey?

A gap is a market nobody is serving. Absent cat food, absent small packs and absent growth diets are common gaps a small local producer can fill.

6. What legal status do AAFCO nutrient profiles have in African countries?

AAFCO is the Association of American Feed Control Officials, a United States model. It is a sound technical reference but has no legal force in African countries, where requirements vary and change.

7. What is the first thing to do before printing labels?

Requirements differ by country and change over time. Copying an imported label is how producers end up scrapping printed stock, as the Accra example shows.

8. Claiming growth including large size dogs brings which constraint?

AAFCO limits calcium to 1.8 percent DM for formulas that may be fed to puppies of 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, against 2.5 percent for other life stages.

9. Which vitamin D ceiling is tighter?

The EU legal maximum tabulated by FEDIAF is 227 IU per 100 g DM, that is 2270 IU per kg, below the AAFCO dog maximum of 3000 IU per kg.

10. What is a realistic budget item for a small producer's compliance file?

Budget one full analysis per formulation per year plus one for any recipe change, verify actual prices locally, and file the receipts with your batch records.

11. Up to what inclusion did feather meal leave faecal scores unaffected in the Beagle trial?

The trial concluded bone protein meal could go to 12 percent and feather meal to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores. Quality fell at 24 and 20 percent respectively.

12. Which starch sources ranked highest for digestibility in extruded dog diets?

Brewer's rice and cassava flour led on dry matter, organic matter and gross energy digestibility, followed by maize and sorghum, with peas and lentils lowest.

13. What is the published maximum inclusion for soybean meal in dog food?

No maximum inclusion consensus exists for soybean meal, maize or rice. The review refused to set one because study methods were too heterogeneous.

14. Which hazard is most likely to enter a formulation through an ingredient rather than a treat?

Powders and dehydrated flakes are the concentrated allium hazard and arrive inside palatants and broths. The others are mainly contamination or treat risks.

15. What is the AAFCO status of black soldier fly larvae meal?

It was tentatively approved in August 2021 for adult dog food, not for cats and not for growing dogs, with a requirement to dry to no more than 10 percent moisture. Verify current status before relying on it.

16. Using the modified Atwater route, what is the gross energy of a food with 25 percent protein, 12 percent fat and 48 percent nitrogen-free extract plus fibre?

5.7 x 25 = 142.5, 9.4 x 12 = 112.8 and 4.1 x 48 = 196.8, which sum to 452.1 kcal per 100 g. 387.4 is digestible energy and 361.4 is metabolisable energy.

17. A food is 25 percent crude protein as fed at 9 percent moisture. What is that on a dry matter basis?

Dry matter is 91 percent, so 25 divided by 0.91 = 27.5 percent. That clears dog growth at 22.5 and cat adult at 26.0 but fails cat growth and reproduction at 30.0.

18. Which pair of equations applies to cats?

Cats use 87.9 minus 0.88 times crude fibre for digestibility and subtract 0.77 times protein for metabolisable energy. Using the dog pair for a cat food gives a wrong feeding guide.

19. Which nutrient is the most common cause of accidental poisoning through formulation error?

Vitamin D toxicity in dogs and cats has repeatedly arisen from premix and formulation error rather than from raw ingredients, causing multiple commercial recalls.

20. What is the purpose of a retained sample?

A sealed, coded and dated sample of every batch lets you test what actually shipped. In the Bulawayo example it identified a fibre drift after a supplier change.

21. What is the packaging cost per kilogram if a 5 kg pack costs USD 0.33 in bag, label and sealing?

0.33 divided by 5 kg equals USD 0.066 per kilogram, plus about USD 0.0025 per kilogram for amortised print plates.

22. How many grams a day should a pack recommend for a 20 kg neutered dog on a 3614 kcal per kg food?

RER is 662 kcal, and 662 x 1.6 = 1059 kcal, divided by 3614 kcal per kg gives 0.293 kg. 1059 is the kcal figure, not grams.

23. What has the FDA established about grain-free diets and DCM?

The FDA received 524 reports to 30 April 2019 including 119 dog deaths, but states adverse event numbers alone do not establish causation. It paused updates on 23 December 2022 pending meaningful new science.

24. If you formulate with high pulse inclusion, what is a defensible action?

Those are the defensible steps. Dogs have no AAFCO taurine minimum, but supplementation is cheap insurance while the mechanism remains unresolved.

25. Which marketing claims can a small African producer prove every day?

These are verifiable facts about your own product and business, unlike health claims that the published evidence does not support.

26. What happens to fixed cost per kilogram when monthly volume rises from 600 kg to 3000 kg at USD 450 fixed costs?

450 divided by 600 is USD 0.75 and 450 divided by 3000 is USD 0.15. Spreading fixed costs over more kilograms is the economic case for scaling.

27. A retailer pays in 45 days and your suppliers want cash. At 3000 kg a month and USD 1.10 variable cost, roughly how much cash is tied up?

3000 x 1.10 x 1.5 months equals about USD 4950 permanently locked up. Growth must be financed before the first large order, not after it.

28. Why should a small producer keep supplier lot numbers on every batch sheet?

Traceability is what turns a total recall into an eleven-batch hold, as in the Mwanza example. Vitamin D premix errors forced recalls at large firms in 2018 and 2019.

29. Why plan on 60 to 70 percent of theoretical capacity?

Six 100 kg batches a day over 22 days is 13,200 kg in theory, but realistic output is nearer 8000 to 9200 kg. Calculate your ceiling before promising a retailer volume.

30. What happens to the nutrient minima as your business grows?

Dog adult 18.0 and 5.5 percent, dog growth 22.5 and 8.5, cat adult 26.0 and 9.0, cat growth 30.0 and 9.0 on a dry matter basis apply regardless of scale, along with cat taurine at 0.10 or 0.20 percent.

Module 12 capstone

Write the first version of a real business plan for a pet feed operation in your own town, built entirely on data you collect this month rather than on figures from this course. Step 1: count the market. Visit at least three veterinary clinics, three pet shops or agrovet stores and one supermarket, and record for every dog and cat food on the shelf the brand, pack size, country of origin, retail price and price per kilogram. Photograph every label. Step 2: interview at least fifteen dog or cat owners and record what they feed, how much they spend a month, what they wish they could buy and what they would pay. Step 3: telephone or visit your national feed or standards regulator and write down, in their words, what is required to register and label a dog or cat food in your country, what it costs and how long it takes. Do not assume the AAFCO rules taught here apply. Step 4: get written quotations per kilogram for every ingredient in one formulation, including a vitamin and mineral premix, and build a costed recipe summing to 100 percent. Step 5: calculate energy density using the modified Atwater route and check your dry matter protein, fat, calcium and phosphorus against the AAFCO minima for the species and life stage you intend to claim. Step 6: build a break-even table with your real fixed costs per month, your real variable cost per kilogram and your intended price, and state the kilograms per month at which you stop losing money. Step 7: write one page on what you will not claim on the pack and why, naming grain-free and DCM specifically. Finish with the three numbers you are least sure of and how you will verify them.
