1. Solvent Selection and Properties

Solvent extraction uses a liquid solvent to dissolve oil from oilseeds, leaving behind a dry meal. The most common solvent is food-grade hexane, a petroleum distillate. In Africa, hexane is imported and expensive, so you must use it carefully. Key properties: hexane boils at 6869C, which means it evaporates easily and can be recovered. It is highly flammable (flash point -22C) and its vapours are heavier than air, so they pool in low areas. It is also a health hazard if inhaled. Other solvents like ethanol or isopropanol can be used, but they are less efficient and require more energy to recover. For small-scale operations in Africa, hexane is still the standard. You need solvent with low boiling point, low latent heat, low toxicity, and high oil solubility. Commercial hexane is a mixture of isomers with a boiling range of 6070C. Always buy food-grade hexane (not industrial grade) to avoid toxic impurities. Store it in sealed steel drums away from sparks, in a cool, ventilated shed. Never use petrol, kerosene, or other fuels as solventsthey are toxic and leave harmful residues. Check the label for purity: at least 95% n-hexane. When you receive a drum, test its boiling point with a simple thermometer and distillation setup if possible. If it boils above 80C, it contains heavy contaminants that will be hard to remove from oil. Keep records of solvent purchases and usage. In many African countries, hexane is taxed as a chemical, so budget for it. A typical small plant uses 25 litres of hexane per tonne of seed, but losses can be higher if equipment leaks. Proper selection and handling save money and lives.

2. Principles of Solvent Extraction

Solvent extraction works on the principle of dissolving oil in a solvent. Oilseeds first go through preparation: cleaning, cracking, dehulling, and flaking. Flaking is criticalflakes should be 0.20.3 mm thick. Thin flakes rupture oil cells, allowing solvent to penetrate. The prepared flakes are then contacted with hexane in a countercurrent flow. In simple terms, fresh solvent meets the most exhausted flakes, while concentrated oil-rich solvent (called miscella) meets fresh flakes. This countercurrent design uses less solvent and extracts more oil. The main mechanisms are washing and diffusion. Washing removes oil from the surface; diffusion pulls oil from inside the flakes. Temperature matters: extraction is done at 5060C, just below hexanes boiling point, to reduce viscosity and improve flow. The ratio of solvent to flakes is typically 1:1 to 3:1 by weight. Contact time is 3060 minutes in batch systems, or 2030 minutes in continuous systems. After extraction, you get two streams: oil-rich miscella (2030% oil) and wet meal (2535% solvent). The miscella is distilled to evaporate hexane, leaving crude oil. The wet meal is desolventized to remove hexane, leaving meal for animal feed. Extraction efficiency should be 9598% oil recovery. To test efficiency, take a sample of spent flakes and check residual oilit should be less than 1%. If higher, adjust flake thickness, temperature, or solvent ratio. In Africa, many small plants use batch extractors because they are cheaper and easier to maintain. But continuous extractors save solvent and labour. Always monitor for solvent lossesif you smell hexane, you are losing money and risking health. Use a simple flow meter or weigh your solvent tank daily. Keep extraction records: seed weight, solvent used, oil produced, and residual oil. This data helps you improve.

3. Batch and Continuous Extraction Systems

Batch extraction is the simplest system for small-scale African producers. You load flaked seed into a vessel, add hexane, stir, and drain the miscella. Then repeat with fresh solvent 23 times. A common batch extractor is a vertical cylindrical tank with a false bottom and a drain valve. You can build one from stainless steel or mild steel lined with epoxy. Capacity: 100500 kg of flakes per batch. Cycle time: 12 hours. Labour: 23 workers. Solvent use: 35 litres per kg of oil extracted. Batch systems are cheap (USD 2,0005,000) but labour-intensive and lose more solvent. Continuous extraction uses a series of connected extractors (e.g., a shallow-bed or deep-bed extractor) where flakes move continuously on a conveyor or through a rotary drum. Solvent flows countercurrently. Continuous systems handle 10100 tonnes per day, recover 98% oil, and use only 12 litres of solvent per kg of oil. They cost USD 50,000500,000, so they suit larger cooperatives or commercial plants. In Africa, countries like Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa have continuous plants for soybeans and sunflower. For a beginner, start with batch extraction. You can later upgrade to a continuous system by adding more extractor vessels in series. Key components for either system: a solvent tank, extractor, miscella tank, distillation unit, and condenser. For batch, you also need a stirrer and a pump. Always use explosion-proof motors and wiring. In continuous systems, the most common design is the Rotocel extractor (a rotating basket) or the Crown extractor (deep bed). You can find used equipment from Asia or Europe, but check for corrosion and worn seals. In Africa, local fabrication is possible: a welding shop can make a batch extractor from stainless steel. Ensure all welds are smooth and leak-free. Test with water before using hexane. Safety: never open an extractor while it contains hexane vapours. Purge with nitrogen or steam before maintenance.

4. Desolventizing and Toasting of Meal

After extraction, the wet meal contains 2535% hexane. You must remove this solvent to make the meal safe for animal feed and to recover the hexane. This process is called desolventizing. The simplest method is heating the meal in a jacketed vessel with steam. Hexane evaporates at 68C, so you heat the meal to 7585C for 3060 minutes. But direct steam can also be injected to strip solvent. This is called desolventizer-toaster (DT) operation. In a DT, the meal moves through several trays. Steam heats the trays and also contacts the meal. The steam carries away hexane vapours to a condenser. After desolventizing, the meal still contains some water from steam. You then dry it to 1012% moisture and cool it. Toasting is a critical step for meals like soybean, which contain anti-nutritional factors (trypsin inhibitors). Toasting at 100110C for 2030 minutes destroys these factors. For groundnut or sunflower meal, toasting is less critical but still improves digestibility. In Africa, many small plants skip toasting, but that reduces meal value. You can build a simple DT from a vertical stack of trays with steam coils. Or use a rotary dryer with steam injection. The key is to monitor temperature and time. Use a thermometer in the meal outlet. If meal smells like hexane, you need more desolventizing. Test residual hexane by putting a sample in a sealed jar and smelling after 10 minutes. If you smell solvent, increase steam or time. The recovered hexane vapours go to a condenser and then to a solvent-water separator. The water layer is drained, and hexane is recycled. Typical desolventizing losses are 0.51.5 kg per tonne of meal. Keep records. In Africa, meal is a valuable product for poultry and cattle. Proper desolventizing and toasting can double its market price. Never feed meal with more than 0.1% residual hexaneit is toxic to animals. Also, ensure the meal is not burnt; burnt meal is dark and has low protein digestibility. Use low-pressure steam (35 bar) and gentle agitation.

5. Solvent Recovery and Recycling

Solvent recovery is the heart of profitable solvent extraction. Hexane is expensive (USD 1.52.5 per litre in Africa). If you lose 5 litres per tonne of seed, that is USD 7.512.5 per tonnea huge cost. So you must recover and recycle at least 99% of the solvent. Recovery happens in three places: from the miscella (oil-solvent mixture), from the wet meal (desolventizing), and from vents and leaks. The main recovery method is distillation. Miscella contains 2030% oil and 7080% hexane. You heat it in a distillation column or a simple pot still. Hexane boils at 68C, so you heat to 8090C. The hexane vapour goes to a condenser (water-cooled) and becomes liquid hexane again. The oil left behind is crude oil, which still contains 100500 ppm hexane. You can strip remaining hexane by steam injection under vacuum or by a second distillation. For small-scale plants, a batch distillation unit works: a kettle with a steam jacket, a condenser, and a receiving tank. You can build one for USD 1,0003,000. In continuous plants, you use a rising film evaporator or a falling film evaporator. After distillation, the recovered hexane may contain water. You separate it in a decanterhexane floats on water. Drain the water, and reuse the hexane. Check its purity: if it boils above 70C, it has oil or water contamination. You can re-distill it. Also, recover hexane from the desolventizer vapours and from any vents by using a mineral oil scrubber or a chilled condenser. Keep your system sealed. Use gaskets, flanges, and welded pipes. Leaks are the biggest cause of solvent loss. Do a simple leak test: pressurize the system with air to 0.5 bar and spray soapy water on jointsbubbles show leaks. Fix them immediately. In Africa, some plants lose 1020 litres of hexane per tonne due to poor recovery. With good recovery, you can reduce that to 12 litres. That saves money and protects workers. Train your staff to check for hexane smells and to log solvent usage daily.

6. Safety and Environmental Controls in Solvent Extraction

Hexane is dangerous. It is highly flammable, and its vapours can explode if ignited. It is also a neurotoxininhaling it causes dizziness, headaches, and long-term nerve damage. In Africa, many small plants ignore safety, leading to fires and poisonings. You must follow strict controls. First, ventilation: work areas must have cross-ventilation and exhaust fans. Hexane vapours are heavier than air, so install exhausts at floor level. Use explosion-proof motors, switches, and lights. No open flames, no smoking, no sparks. Keep a dry powder fire extinguisher and a sand bucket nearby. Second, personal protective equipment (PPE): workers must wear chemical goggles, nitrile gloves, and respirators with organic vapour cartridges. Never use cotton glovesthey soak up hexane. Third, spill control: keep absorbent materials (sand, sawdust) and a spill kit. If hexane spills, shut off ignition sources, ventilate, and absorb. Do not wash it into drainsit is toxic to aquatic life. Fourth, health monitoring: rotate workers to limit exposure. The safe exposure limit is 500 ppm for 8 hours (OSHA), but aim for below 50 ppm. Use a simple gas detector or colourimetric tubes to check. Fifth, waste management: never dump hexane or oil sludge into rivers or on land. Collect waste solvent in sealed drums and send to a licensed recycler or incinerator. In many African countries, environmental agencies require an EIA (Environmental Impact Assessment) for solvent plants. You must also manage the water from decantersit contains traces of hexane. Treat it by air stripping or activated carbon before disposal. Sixth, emergency plan: post emergency numbers, train workers in first aid, and conduct fire drills. If someone inhales hexane, move them to fresh air and seek medical help. If hexane contacts skin, wash with soap and water. Never induce vomiting if swallowedseek medical attention. Finally, keep records of incidents and near-misses. A safe plant is a profitable plant. Invest in safetyit costs less than a fire or a lawsuit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Solvent Extraction Technology in African Oil Milling

For African oil millers moving beyond manual presses and small expellers, solvent extraction is the step that turns oilseed cake into real money. A tonne of expeller cake still holds 612% residual oil; a properly run solvent plant can pull that down below 1%, recovering an extra 50110 litres of crude oil per tonne while producing a higher-protein meal for the feed trade. This lesson is a working reference for operators, plant managers, and investors in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Tanzania, Zambia, and South Africa who need to run a solvent plant safely, profitably, and within environmental limits.

Solvent Selection for African Conditions

Commercial hexane remains the industry standard, but African operators must weigh cost, availability, and safety. Inland mills in Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Uganda often pay a premium for imported hexane, so some use locally refined petroleum fractions with wider boiling ranges. The trade-off is higher solvent loss and more energy in the distillation train.

Batch vs Continuous: What Fits Your Scale

Batch extractors (typically 110 tonnes per batch) suit small and medium mills where capital is tight and labour is affordable. Continuous systems (1001,000 tonnes per day) dominate large operations in Nigeria, South Africa, and Egypt. The decision is business-driven: a 5 t/day batch plant may cost USD 80,000150,000 installed, while a 100 t/day continuous plant runs USD 25 million. Payback depends on the spread between cake price and crude oil price in 2024, that spread in West Africa was roughly USD 400700 per tonne of oil recovered.

Desolventizing, Toasting, and Solvent Recovery

After extraction, the meal carries 2535% solvent. The desolventizer-toaster (DT) uses direct steam to strip solvent, then indirect steam to toast the meal raising protein digestibility and destroying anti-nutritional factors like trypsin inhibitors in soy. For African soy processors, proper toasting is non-negotiable: under-toasted meal fails feed mill quality tests, while over-toasted meal loses lysine and market value.

Solvent recovery is where profit leaks. A well-run plant loses 13 kg solvent per tonne of seed; a poorly maintained one loses 815 kg. At USD 1,200/tonne hexane, that difference is USD 618 per tonne of seed processed enough to swing a mill from profit to loss. Key recovery points: condenser efficiency, condensate separation, mineral oil absorption, and steam stripping of the final oil.

SAFETY WARNING HEXANE IS EXPLOSIVE

Hexane vapour ignites at concentrations as low as 1.1% in air. All electrical equipment in the extraction building must be explosion-proof (ATEX or IECEx rated). No smoking, no open flames, no non-rated phones or torches. Static bonding and grounding on all vessels, pipes, and tanker unloading points is mandatory. In African plants, the most common causes of accidents are: (1) ungrounded solvent drums, (2) unrated lighting or motors, (3) inadequate ventilation in the extractor pit, and (4) untrained night-shift operators. Install continuous LEL (lower explosive limit) monitors with audible alarms, and enforce a permit-to-work system for hot work. A single drum of hexane can destroy a plant and kill workers treat it accordingly.

Environmental Controls and African Regulatory Reality

Solvent-laden air and water are the main environmental issues. Condensers must achieve >95% recovery; vent gas should pass through a mineral-oil absorber or activated carbon bed before release. Wastewater from the DT and barometric condensers must be treated hexane in effluent kills aquatic life and violates national discharge limits in Nigeria (NESREA), Kenya (NEMA), and South Africa (DWAF). Many African mills now use closed-loop cooling and zero-discharge designs to avoid fines and community complaints.

Also consider the meal market: solvent-extracted meal is a valuable feed ingredient, but only if residual solvent is below 500 ppm (typically <100 ppm in good plants). Buyers in the poultry and aquaculture sectors increasingly test for it. Your DT performance is therefore both a safety and a commercial issue.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Solvent quality: Verify boiling range 6369C and water content <0.1% before accepting any hexane delivery.
  2. Explosion-proof equipment: Confirm all motors, lights, switches, and instruments in the extraction zone carry ATEX/IECEx certification.
  3. Grounding and bonding: Test continuity on all vessels, pipes, and drum unloading points weekly; log results.
  4. LEL monitoring: Calibrate continuous gas detectors monthly; keep portable meters at the extractor and DT.
  5. Solvent loss tracking: Record daily solvent in vs. out; investigate any loss above 3 kg per tonne of seed.
  6. DT temperature: Monitor direct and indirect steam temperatures; target meal exit temp 105115C for soy, 100110C for groundnut.
  7. Effluent checks: Test wastewater for hexane monthly; ensure it meets NESREA/NEMA/DWAF limits before discharge.
  8. Operator training: Run quarterly refresher sessions on solvent handling, emergency shutdown, and fire response.
