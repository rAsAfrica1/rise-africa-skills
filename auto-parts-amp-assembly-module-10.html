1. Reading Tyre Sizes and Choosing the Right Tyres

Look at the sidewall of any tyre and you will see a code like 195/65R15 91H. The first number, 195, is the tyre width in millimetres. The second number, 65, is the aspect ratio: the height of the sidewall is 65% of the width. The R means radial construction. The 15 is the rim diameter in inches. The 91 is the load index, and H is the speed rating. For example, a 91 load index means each tyre can carry 615 kg. If your car weighs 1,500 kg, four tyres at 91 give you 2,460 kg total capacity, which is safe. Never fit a tyre with a lower load index than the manufacturer recommends.

In Africa, road conditions vary from smooth tarmac to rough gravel. For mixed use, choose a tyre with a slightly higher aspect ratio, like 70 or 75, because the taller sidewall absorbs potholes better and protects your rims. A lower profile tyre (like 55) looks sporty but is easily damaged on bad roads. Also check the treadwear rating: a number like 400 means the tyre lasts longer than one with 200. Traction rating A is best for wet roads; temperature rating A resists overheating.

When buying used tyres, check the DOT code: the last four digits show the week and year of manufacture. For example, 2319 means the tyre was made in the 23rd week of 2019. Tyres older than six years should be avoided, even if the tread looks good, because rubber hardens and cracks. Also inspect for uneven wear, bulges, or exposed cords. A matching set of four tyres of the same size and brand is ideal, but at minimum, replace tyres in pairs on the same axle.

Finally, always inflate to the pressure listed on the driver's door jamb or fuel cap, not the maximum on the tyre sidewall. For a typical sedan, that is around 30-35 PSI. Under-inflation causes overheating and rapid wear; over-inflation reduces grip. Check pressures weekly when tyres are cold.

2. Changing a Tyre Safely with a Jack and Spanner

Before you start, park on a flat, hard surface away from traffic. Turn on hazard lights and apply the handbrake. If you have wheel chocks, place them on the wheel diagonally opposite the one you are changing. For a manual car, engage first gear; for an automatic, put it in Park. Loosen the wheel nuts slightly with the spanner while the tyre is still on the ground. Do not remove them yet.

Locate the jacking point under the car, usually a reinforced metal seam near each wheel. Your owner's manual shows exact spots. Place the jack head under that point and turn the handle clockwise until the tyre just lifts off the ground. Never put any part of your body under the car when it is only supported by a jack. If you have a jack stand, place it under a strong chassis point as a backup.

Now remove the wheel nuts completely and pull the wheel straight off. If it is stuck, kick the tyre edge or use a rubber mallet. Mount the spare wheel, aligning the bolt holes. Screw on the nuts by hand until snug. Lower the jack until the tyre touches the ground but still bears no weight. Then tighten the nuts in a star pattern: tighten one, then the one opposite, and so on. This ensures the wheel sits flat. Finally, lower the car fully and tighten the nuts firmly with the spanner. Check tightness again after 50 km.

Common mistakes: using a scissor jack on soft ground (it sinks), over-tightening nuts with a foot (strips threads), or forgetting to re-check torque. A torque wrench set to 110 Nm is ideal for most cars. If you have no torque wrench, tighten firmly by hand but do not jump on the spanner. Always replace the spare with a properly inflated one, and repair the punctured tyre as soon as possible.

3. Balancing Wheels and Understanding Wheel Weights

Wheel balancing stops vibrations that occur at certain speeds, usually 80-120 km/h. An unbalanced wheel causes the steering wheel to shake, uneven tyre wear, and stress on bearings. Balancing is done on a special machine that spins the wheel and measures heavy spots. The machine tells the operator where to attach small lead or zinc weights on the rim. These weights come in grams, typically 5g to 50g, and are either clip-on (for steel rims) or stick-on (for alloy rims).

When a tyre is mounted on a rim, the heavy spot of the tyre (marked with a yellow dot) should be aligned with the light spot of the rim (often the valve stem). This reduces the amount of weight needed. After balancing, the machine spins the wheel again to confirm it is within 5 grams of perfect. If not, the operator adds or moves weights. A properly balanced wheel needs no more than 60g total weight. If it needs more, the tyre or rim may be defective.

In Africa, many roadside tyre shops have balancing machines, but not all are calibrated. Ask the operator to show you the machine reading before and after. If you see a pile of old weights on the rim, insist they remove all old weights first. Also, after off-road driving, mud can stick to the rim and throw off balance. Wash your wheels regularly. If you feel vibration after hitting a pothole, a wheel weight may have fallen off. Re-balance that wheel.

Dynamic balancing (spin balancing) is better than static (bubble balancing) because it corrects both up-down and side-to-side imbalance. Always balance all four wheels when you buy new tyres. For a small extra cost, it saves your suspension and gives a smoother ride.

4. Performing Basic Wheel Alignment Checks

Wheel alignment ensures your wheels point in the correct direction. The three main angles are camber (vertical tilt), caster (steering axis tilt), and toe (horizontal direction). Toe is the most critical for tyre wear. If your front wheels point slightly inward (toe-in) or outward (toe-out), the tyres scrub sideways and wear quickly. You can check toe at home with a simple string method. Park on level ground with wheels straight. Tie a long string to a fixed point at the rear of the car, stretch it along the side to the front, and measure the distance from the string to the front and rear of the front tyre. The difference should be small, typically 0-3 mm toe-in.

A more accurate DIY check uses a measuring tape. Measure the distance between the inner edges of the front tyres at the front and at the rear of the tyres. For most cars, the front measurement should be 1-3 mm less than the rear (toe-in). If the difference is more than 5 mm, you need alignment. Also check the steering wheel: if it is off-centre when driving straight, alignment is off. Another sign is uneven tyre wear: feathered edges on the inside or outside of the tread.

Many alignment problems come from worn suspension parts: tie rod ends, ball joints, or control arm bushings. Before paying for alignment, have a mechanic check these. If they are loose, alignment will not hold. In Africa, potholes and rough roads knock alignment out frequently. Get it checked every 10,000 km or after a hard impact. A professional alignment with a computerised machine costs about 20-50 USD in most cities. It is worth it to save tyres that cost 100 USD each.

If you cannot afford professional alignment, at least set toe roughly using the string method. But camber and caster require shims or adjustment bolts, so see a specialist. Always test drive after alignment on a straight road; the car should not pull to one side.

5. Repairing Tubeless Tyre Punctures

Tubeless tyres can be repaired if the puncture is in the tread area, not the sidewall. A simple repair uses a plug kit, available at any auto parts shop for a few dollars. First, locate the puncture by spraying soapy water on the tyre and looking for bubbles. Mark the spot. Remove the object (nail, screw) with pliers. If the object is still in, do not remove it until you are ready to plug.

Use a reaming tool to clean and roughen the hole. Insert the reamer into the hole and twist it to remove dirt and make the hole slightly larger. Then take a plug strip (a sticky rubber string) and thread it through the eye of the insertion tool. Push the tool into the hole until about 2 cm of the plug remains outside. Pull the tool out quickly, leaving the plug in the hole. Cut off the excess plug flush with the tread. Inflate the tyre to the correct pressure and check for leaks with soapy water.

This plug repair is a temporary fix. For a permanent repair, take the tyre to a shop where they will remove the tyre from the rim and apply a patch from the inside. This is called a patch-plug combination. It costs more but lasts the life of the tyre. Never repair a puncture in the sidewall or shoulder; the tyre must be replaced. Also, if the tyre has been driven flat, the inside may be damaged; have it inspected.

In Africa, many roadside repairs use only plugs. That is fine for emergencies, but if you plan to drive at high speed or long distance, get an internal patch. Keep a plug kit and a small compressor in your car. Learn to do it yourself: it takes 10 minutes and can save you a long walk.

6. Sourcing Affordable Tyres and Rims

New tyres are expensive, but you can find good deals if you know where to look. Start with local tyre shops that sell used tyres, often called 'second-hand' or 'part-worn'. They may have tyres with 50-70% tread left for half the price. Check the DOT code for age and inspect for damage. Avoid tyres with uneven wear or patches. Also, ask for 'take-offs' from car dealers or fleet operators who replace tyres in sets. These are often high-quality brands at low prices.

Online marketplaces like Jumia, OLX, or Facebook Marketplace can have good deals, but you must inspect in person. Never pay before seeing the tyre. For rims, steel rims are cheaper and easier to repair; alloy rims look better but bend more easily on potholes. If you need a rim, check scrapyards for matching models. A used steel rim might cost 10-20 USD, while a new alloy can be 100 USD or more.

Consider retreaded tyres for off-road or slow-speed use. Retreading adds a new tread to a used casing. They are cheaper but not recommended for high-speed driving. In many African countries, retreads are common for trucks and buses. For a car, only use retreads if you drive mainly on rough roads at low speeds. Always buy in pairs or sets of four for even wear.

When negotiating, ask for a discount for cash or for buying multiple tyres. Check the manufacturing date: tyres older than six years are unsafe even if unused. Also, ensure the load index and speed rating match your car's requirements. Finally, factor in the cost of fitting, balancing, and new valve stems. A cheap tyre that costs extra to mount may not be a bargain. Build a relationship with a trusted tyre dealer who will give you honest advice.

1. Decoding a Tyre Sidewall â The Commercial Foundation

Every quotation you give, every warranty claim you honour, and every customer complaint you resolve begins with correctly reading the sidewall. A size like 205/55 R16 91V means: section width 205 mm; aspect ratio 55% (sidewall height = 55% of 205 mm = 112.75 mm); radial construction; rim diameter 16 inches; load index 91 (615 kg per tyre); speed rating V (up to 240 km/h). Misreading any digit leads to wrong stock, unsafe fitment, and lost margin.

3. Matching Tyres to the Road You Actually Drive On

Do not sell a European summer tyre into a market where the customer's last 30 km was laterite. Practical selection rules for African workshops:

  • Sealed highways / city fleets: standard radial, symmetric tread. Prioritise rolling resistance for fuel savings â a real selling point for boda-boda and taxi fleets.
  • Mixed sealed/unsealed: all-terrain or reinforced-sidewall passenger tyres. Sidewall cuts are the number-one cause of premature failure in East and West Africa.
  • Gravel, murram, farm tracks: LT-rated (Light Truck) with higher load index and deeper tread. Accept higher noise and lower fuel economy.
  • Hot-climate fleets: check the UTQG temperature rating; an "A" rating resists heat build-up better than "B" or "C".
  • Retreads and part-worns: legal in most African jurisdictions for commercial vehicles, but never mix a retread with a new tyre on the same axle, and never fit part-worns on the front of a passenger car.

5. Wheel Balancing â The Margin You Are Leaving on the Table

Dynamic balancing is a high-margin, low-cost add-on. A balancer pays for itself in weeks. Balance every wheel you fit, and re-balance after any puncture repair that involves removing the tyre from the rim. Wheel weights come in two families: clip-on (lead or zinc, for steel and alloy rims with a lip) and adhesive (for alloy wheels without a lip). Note that lead weights are being phased out in many jurisdictions â stock zinc or steel alternatives.

Typical imbalance symptoms you can use as a sales diagnostic: steering-wheel shimmy at 80â100 km/h (front wheels out of balance); seat or floor vibration at the same speed (rear wheels); cupped or scalloped tread wear (chronic imbalance or worn dampers).

6. Basic Wheel Alignment Checks â The Diagnostic That Sells Tyres

You do not need a 3D aligner to make money. A tape measure, a plumb line, a spirit level, and a straight edge will let you check toe and camber to a useful tolerance, and to identify when a customer needs a full alignment bay. Check and record:

  • Toe: measure the distance between the front and rear of the front tyres at axle height. Difference of more than 3 mm indicates toe-in or toe-out.
  • Camber: with a spirit-level gauge against the wheel, more than 1Â° deviation from vertical is a flag.
  • Caster: requires a turn-plate or a bubble gauge; usually a job for a full aligner.
  • Steering-wheel centring after any alignment.
  • Tyre wear pattern: inner-edge wear = excessive negative camber or toe-out; outer-edge = positive camber or toe-in; feathering = toe problem.

Always check tyre pressures, ride height, and suspension play before alignment. Aligning a car with worn ball joints or tie-rod ends is a waste of the customer's money and your reputation.

7. Tubeless Puncture Repair â Doing It So It Holds

Tubeless repairs are the bread-and-butter job of every African tyre shop. The only repair method acceptable for a permanent, roadworthy fix is a combined plug-and-patch applied from inside the tyre. External "string" plugs are a temporary roadside get-you-home measure only and should be labelled as such to the customer. Never repair:

  • Sidewall or shoulder punctures.
  • Punctures larger than 6 mm.
  • Tyre with run-flat damage, bead damage, or exposed cords.
  • More than two repairs per tyre, or two repairs in the same quadrant.
  • Any tyre that has been run flat and shows internal heat damage.

Procedure: deflate, demount, inspect inside with a light, mark the injury, buff the inner liner to a matte finish, apply vulcanising cement, allow to flash off, fit the combined plug-patch from inside, stitch down with a roller, refit, inflate, check for leaks at the repair and around the bead, then re-balance the wheel.

8. Sourcing Affordable Tyres and Rims â The Business Layer

Margins in African tyre retail come from three levers: volume (fleet contracts), mix (upselling balancing, alignment, TPMS service and nitrogen), and sourcing (buying at the right point in the import cycle). Practical guidance:

  • Container consolidations: partner with two or three other shops to import a mixed container from China, India, Thailand or Indonesia. This cuts per-unit landed cost dramatically versus buying from a local distributor.
  • Watch the exchange rate: in most African markets the tyre price tracks the USD or EUR within weeks. Buy forward when the local currency is strong.
  • Verify DOT / E-mark: reject tyres older than 3 years from date of manufacture (DOT week/year code). Old stock is the most common way African retailers lose repeat customers.
