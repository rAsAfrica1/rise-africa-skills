1. Understanding Malnutrition in Children

Before you cook anything, you must know what you are treating. Malnutrition is not one condition — it is several, and the wrong food for the wrong type can waste time, money and lives.

Module 1 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~45 minutes reading · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 1.1 — The three types of malnutrition: stunting, wasting and underweight

Malnutrition is not one condition. It is several, and they look different in the same child. Understanding which one you are facing changes everything about how you respond.

Stunting — low height for age

Stunting is what happens when a child is undernourished for months or years during the time they should be growing. It is chronic. Once stunting has occurred, it cannot be reversed. A stunted child will be permanently shorter than their genetic potential and will have a smaller, less-connected brain than they would have had with proper nutrition.

Roughly 149 million children under five are stunted worldwide. About 40% of them live in sub-Saharan Africa. Stunting is the single most common form of child malnutrition — and because it is invisible day-to-day (a short child simply looks short), it is also the most neglected.

Wasting — low weight for height

Wasting is what happens when a child has recently been severely undernourished, ill, or both. It is acute, meaning it develops fast — sometimes in a matter of weeks. Unlike stunting, wasting is reversible if treated quickly.

The World Health Organization classifies a child as moderately wasted if their weight-for-height is more than 2 standard deviations below the median, and severely wasted if it is more than 3 standard deviations below. In plain language: a child whose ribs are visible, whose arms are thin, whose skin is loose, is likely wasted.

Severe wasting is a medical emergency. If a child cannot eat, has oedema (swollen feet and legs), or is lethargic, they need a clinic immediately. This course teaches you to make food — not to replace medical care.

Underweight — low weight for age

Underweight combines aspects of both stunting and wasting. A child may be short, light, or both. It is the easiest to measure because all you need is a scale and the child's age. But it is the least precise for telling you what is actually wrong.

Why the distinction matters

Because the response is different. Give the wrong response, and you waste resources.

ConditionWhat it meansResponse
StuntingChronic undernutrition during growthLong-term improved diet, sanitation, repeated infection control
WastingRecent severe food shortage or illnessImmediate high-energy, high-protein food — this is where your porridge fits
UnderweightEither or bothDepends on underlying cause — measure height too
Your porridge is designed for wasting. If you are feeding a stunted child, the same porridge will help prevent further damage — but it will not make them taller. Be honest with the family about what to expect.
Practical exercise 1.1: If you have access to a child who may be malnourished, measure three things: weight (kg), height (cm), and age (months). Write them down. You now have data you can track over time.

Lesson 1.2 — The 1,000-day window: why nutrition from conception to age 2 matters more than any other period

The "first 1,000 days" is a phrase used by every major health organisation in the world. It refers to the period from the moment of conception (day 1) to the child's second birthday (day 1,000).

What happens in those 1,000 days shapes the rest of the child's life.

The brain

By age two, a child's brain has reached about 80% of its adult size and structure. That growth does not happen on its own — it needs specific nutrients: protein, iron, zinc, iodine, omega-3 fats, and B vitamins. Miss those in this window, and the brain simply cannot build what it should. IQ is measurably lower. School performance is worse. Adult earning capacity is reduced by up to 20% per year of stunting.

The body

The same window determines height, immune system development, and even how the body handles sugar and fat for the rest of life. Children malnourished in the first 1,000 days are at higher risk of obesity, diabetes and heart disease as adults — even if they eat well later.

The gut

Chronic undernutrition damages the lining of the small intestine — a condition called environmental enteric dysfunction. Damaged guts absorb fewer nutrients, which makes malnutrition worse, which damages the gut more. Breaking this cycle in the first 1,000 days is critical.

After age two, you can still improve a child's life. You can fix wasting, prevent further stunting, and support brain development. But you cannot recover what was lost. This is why prevention matters more than treatment — and why your porridge should be given to children as young as 6 months if they are weaning.
Practical exercise 1.2: Write down the ages of the children you plan to feed. Circle any under 24 months. These are your priority — feed them first, feed them most, and track their weight every month.

Lesson 1.3 — Signs and symptoms: what to look for in hair, skin, eyes, belly and energy levels

You are not a doctor. But you can learn to see what a doctor sees. Here are the reliable signs, from head down.

Hair

  • Thin, sparse, brittle — pulls out easily, does not grow back quickly
  • Colour change — patches of lighter or reddish hair, sometimes called the "flag sign"
  • Straight, dull hair — in a child with African hair, straight sections are a strong signal

Skin

  • Dry, flaky, or cracked — especially on legs and arms
  • Dark patches with sharp edges — often on the back, chest, thighs
  • Very pale palms — sign of anaemia
  • Slow wound healing — small scratches that linger for weeks

Eyes

  • Dull, sunken look — the child looks tired even after sleeping
  • Dull white patches on the whites of the eyes — this is a vitamin A deficiency sign (Bitot's spots). Urgent.
  • Night blindness — child cannot see in low light; early sign of vitamin A deficiency

Belly and body

  • Swollen feet and legs (oedema) — press a thumb into the top of the foot for 10 seconds. If it leaves a dent, that's oedema. Sign of kwashiorkor — urgent.
  • Distended belly with visible ribs — classic "skin and bones with a round belly" look
  • Muscle wasting in arms and legs — measuring the mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is the simplest field test. Below 11.5 cm in a child 6–59 months = severe acute malnutrition.

Energy and behaviour

  • Lethargy — child does not want to play, does not respond to food
  • Irritability — constant crying, hard to comfort
  • Loss of appetite — for a malnourished child, no appetite is a serious sign; it usually means infection is present too
  • Repeated infections — malaria, diarrhoea, chest infections that don't clear
The MUAC tape is your single best tool. A plastic tape costing pennies measures the middle of the left upper arm. Green = healthy, yellow = moderate, red = severe. If you only learn one measurement, learn this one.
Practical exercise 1.3: Do a head-to-toe check on the child in front of you right now. Does any single sign from this lesson apply? If more than two apply, that is moderate or severe malnutrition and needs a clinic referral — your porridge is a support, not a treatment, at that stage.

Lesson 1.4 — Causes: not just lack of food — infection, poor water, and traditional feeding practices

Everyone assumes malnutrition means "not enough food." It almost never is that simple. UNICEF's framework splits the causes into three levels.

Immediate causes

  • Inadequate dietary intake — not enough calories, or not enough variety, or both
  • Disease — diarrhoea, malaria, HIV, TB, worms, chest infections

Underlying causes

  • Household food insecurity — food exists in the market but the family cannot afford it
  • Poor care practices — early weaning, no exclusive breastfeeding, wrong food for age
  • Unhealthy environment — no clean water, no toilet, no handwashing
  • Poor health services — no access to vaccines, no growth monitoring

Basic causes

  • Poverty
  • Low maternal education — the single strongest predictor of child stunting in most studies
  • Conflict, displacement, drought

The infection–malnutrition cycle

Diarrhoea causes malnutrition. Malnutrition makes diarrhoea worse and more frequent. A child can get stuck in this loop for years without anyone noticing, because each episode seems minor.

The cycle works like this:

  1. Child eats contaminated food or water → diarrhoea
  2. Diarrhoea empties the gut → child loses nutrients
  3. Weak gut absorbs less → child becomes more malnourished
  4. Malnourished child has weaker immunity → next infection is worse
  5. Repeat, until severe wasting or death
Your porridge breaks this cycle in two ways. First, it provides nutrients the child could not otherwise absorb. Second, if you make it safely (boiled water, clean utensils), you stop re-infecting the child at every meal. That is why hygiene in module 7 is not optional.
Practical exercise 1.4: For the child you are feeding, ask: how many times did they have diarrhoea in the last month? If the answer is more than three, the porridge alone will not fix this. You must also address water, sanitation, and possibly worms and malaria.

Lesson 1.5 — Why porridge? The case for a low-cost, locally produced, high-energy food

Of all the foods you could give a malnourished child, why porridge? Four reasons — each one practical.

1. It is energy dense — when you make it right

Plain thin maize porridge has about 40 calories per 100 ml. A malnourished child needs 150–220 calories per kg of body weight per day. So a 10 kg child needs 1,500–2,200 calories daily. If porridge is their main food, it must be fortified to do that job — that is what this course teaches.

2. It is easy to digest

A damaged gut cannot handle solid food. Porridge is smooth, warm, and semi-liquid — the gut absorbs it more easily than rice, bread or beans.

3. It is locally produced

Maize, soy and milk powder are available in almost every African market. You do not need imports. You do not need foreign exchange. You do not need a factory. You can make it in a home kitchen.

4. It is culturally accepted

Mothers already feed their children porridge. You are not changing behaviour, only improving the recipe. That single fact makes a huge difference in whether the food actually gets eaten.

What porridge is not: It is not a replacement for breast milk in babies under 6 months. It is not a replacement for clinic treatment in severe cases. It is not a magic food. It is one tool among several — a very good tool, but not the only one.
Practical exercise 1.5: Look at what you currently feed a child in your care. Calculate roughly how many calories they get per day from porridge alone. If it is under 500, they need much more — and this course will show you how to add the missing 1,000+.

Lesson 1.6 — Your role: cook, processor or health worker — and the limits of what you can do alone

You have three possible roles in this work. Pick the one that matches your situation.

Role 1 — Household cook

You make porridge for your own children or the children of your extended family. Your priority: correct recipe, correct hygiene, correct frequency. You do not need to sell. You do not need packaging. You need to feed the child every single day without missing one.

Role 2 — Small processor

You make the fortified flour in bulk and sell it to families, clinics, shops or NGOs. Your priorities: consistent recipe, safe processing, shelf-stable packaging, honest labelling, and pricing that lets you stay in business. This is a business — and it needs a business plan (module 12).

Role 3 — Health worker / community educator

You teach others to make and use the porridge. Your priority: simple, memorable demonstrations, follow-up, and knowing when to refer a child to a clinic. You will not cook for every child — you will teach 100 families to cook for their own.

What you cannot do alone

Be honest with yourself and with the families you serve.

  • You cannot replace a clinic. Severe acute malnutrition with complications requires medical treatment.
  • You cannot fix poverty. If a family cannot afford the ingredients, the porridge will not appear.
  • You cannot fix water and sanitation. A child will keep getting sick if the water is unsafe.
  • You cannot do it alone in your community. Sustainable change needs health workers, teachers, churches, and local government on the same page.
The most successful fortified-porridge operations are almost always a partnership: one person makes the flour, one person distributes it, one clinic tracks the children, and one school teaches the mothers. If you try to do all four jobs yourself, you will burn out by month three.
Practical exercise 1.6: Write down which role fits you right now. Then write down two people in your community who could play the other roles. You will need them within 90 days — start the conversations this week.
