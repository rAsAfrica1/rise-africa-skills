1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Alluvial gold mining is the recovery of gold from sand, gravel, and soil that has been eroded from primary rock deposits and transported by water. In Africa, this is the most common form of artisanal mining because it requires less capital and technical skill than hard rock mining. Rivers like the Offin in Ghana, the Niger in Mali, and the Zambezi in Zimbabwe have supported alluvial mining for centuries. Gold particles are heavy (specific gravity 19.3) and settle in river bends, behind boulders, and in gravel layers. You can find fine flakes, small nuggets, or dust. The key is to find where the river slows down and drops its heavy load. This module teaches you how to locate, extract, and process alluvial gold safely and efficiently. You will learn to read a river, use simple tools like pans, sluices, and shovels, and avoid common mistakes that lose gold. Alluvial mining can be done by one person or a small team. It is low-cost but requires patience and observation. In many African countries, alluvial mining is legal with a permit, but you must respect environmental rules and community water rights. This module will give you the practical skills to start, but always check local laws. Remember: gold is where you find it, but you must know where to look.

2. Tools and Materials

To mine alluvial gold, you need a few basic tools that are affordable and available in most African markets. The most important is a gold pan. A 14-inch plastic or metal pan with riffles works well. You can buy one for about $10-15, or make one from a car tire inner tube cut into a shallow bowl. Next, a shovel and a pickaxe for digging gravel. A bucket or two for carrying material. A sluice box is a game-changer for processing more gravel. You can build one from wood or buy a small one for $50-100. It uses a series of riffles and a water flow to catch gold. You also need a classifier or sieve to remove large rocks. A 1/2-inch mesh screen is ideal. For safety, bring gloves, boots, and a first-aid kit. A small vial or film canister for storing your gold. A magnet is useful to remove black sand (magnetite) from your concentrate. For river mining, you may need a small pump or just use the river current. A crevice tool or spoon helps clean cracks in bedrock. Always carry drinking water and food. In Africa, many miners use a 'batea' (wooden bowl) or a 'kuru' (calabash) as a pan. These are cheap and effective. Don't forget a headlamp if working at dawn or dusk. Keep your tools clean and in good repair. A broken shovel or torn sluice mat costs you gold. Finally, a notebook to record where you find gold and how much. This helps you return to good spots.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Find a good spot. Look for inside bends of rivers, behind large rocks, and where the river widens and slows. Gold settles in these low-energy zones. Also check gravel bars and exposed bedrock. Step 2: Test panning. Take a panful of gravel from different spots. Pan it down to see if there is gold. If you see colors (fine gold), dig there. Step 3: Dig and classify. Use your shovel to dig gravel from the pay streak, which is usually a layer of gravel on bedrock or clay. Put it through your classifier to remove rocks larger than 1/2 inch. Step 4: Pan or sluice. If using a pan, fill it half with gravel, add water, and swirl. Remove large stones, then tilt and shake to let gold settle. Repeat until only black sand and gold remain. If using a sluice, set it in the river with a gentle slope. Feed classified gravel slowly. The riffles catch gold. Step 5: Clean up. Every few hours, clean the sluice by removing the mat and washing it into a bucket. Pan the concentrate to get your gold. Step 6: Store your gold. Dry it and put it in a vial. Weigh it if you have a scale. Step 7: Reclaim the site. Fill your holes and don't leave mercury or trash. In Africa, many miners work in teams: one digs, one carries, one pans. This is efficient. Work upstream to downstream. Always check your tailings (waste) for gold you might have missed. A common method is to use a 'long tom' or 'sluice box' with a gentle flow. Remember: slow and steady wins. Rushing loses gold.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Panning too fast. If you swirl too quickly, gold washes out. Fix: Use a gentle circular motion and let the gold settle. Mistake 2: Not classifying. Large rocks hide gold and make panning hard. Fix: Always screen your gravel to 1/2 inch or smaller. Mistake 3: Using too much water flow in a sluice. Fast water flushes gold out. Fix: Adjust the slope and water speed so the flow is just enough to move gravel, not gold. Mistake 4: Digging in the wrong place. Many miners dig anywhere. Fix: Test pan first. Gold is patchy. Find the pay streak. Mistake 5: Losing fine gold. Fine gold floats or washes away. Fix: Use a sluice with fine riffles or a pan with deep riffles. Add a drop of dish soap to break surface tension. Mistake 6: Not checking tailings. You might be throwing away gold. Fix: Re-pan your tailings once a week. Mistake 7: Working alone in dangerous areas. Fix: Work in pairs or groups. Mistake 8: Ignoring safety. Fix: Wear gloves, boots, and don't work under overhanging banks. Mistake 9: Using mercury without retort. Mercury is toxic. Fix: Avoid mercury; use gravity methods. If you must, use a retort and never in water. Mistake 10: Not cleaning equipment. Fix: Clean your sluice and pan after each session. Gold sticks to dirt. Mistake 11: Giving up too soon. Fix: Gold mining requires persistence. Test many spots. In Africa, many miners fail because they don't test. A simple fix is to spend one day just panning test holes. That saves weeks of wasted digging.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. Alluvial mining can be dangerous. Rivers can rise quickly. Never work in a river during heavy rain or upstream of a dam. Always check weather. Work with a buddy. Wear boots with good grip, gloves, and a hat. Use sunscreen. Stay hydrated. If you dig in a bank, watch for collapse. Never undercut a bank. Keep your tools sharp and in good condition. A dull shovel causes accidents. If you use a sluice, set it on stable ground. Don't stand in fast water above your knees. If you use mercury, it is highly toxic. Inhaling vapors causes brain damage. Never use mercury near water or food. Use a retort and always in a ventilated area. Better yet, avoid mercury entirely. Gravity methods work fine for alluvial gold. Best practice: Fill your holes. Don't leave open pits. They are dangerous for people and animals. Don't cut trees unnecessarily. Respect local water sources. In many African communities, water is shared. Don't muddy the water downstream. Use settling ponds. Don't use chemicals like cyanide. It kills fish and people. If you find a large nugget, don't tell everyone. Theft is common. Store your gold securely. Work legally. Get a permit from the local mining authority. Pay taxes. This protects you. Finally, take breaks. Mining is hard work. Fatigue causes mistakes. Eat well. Sleep well. And always have a first-aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and rehydration salts. In remote areas, know the nearest clinic. Safety is not optional. It is part of the job.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. Find a local river or stream known for gold. If you don't have one, use a sandy area with a known gold history. You will need: a gold pan, shovel, bucket, classifier, and a vial. Step 1: Choose a test spot. Look for a gravel bar on the inside bend. Dig a hole 1 foot deep. Step 2: Take a panful of gravel from the bottom of the hole (on bedrock if possible). Step 3: Classify the gravel through your screen. Step 4: Pan it. Fill your pan with water, remove large rocks, then swirl gently. Tilt the pan and let the gold settle. Repeat until you see black sand. Look for gold colors. Step 5: Record your result. Did you find gold? How many flakes? Step 6: Move 10 feet upstream and repeat. Do this 5 times. Step 7: If you find gold, dig a larger hole and process more gravel. If not, move to another spot. Step 8: Practice using a sluice. Set it in the river at a slight angle. Feed classified gravel slowly. After 1 hour, clean the sluice and pan the concentrate. Step 9: Weigh your gold if you have a scale. Even 0.1 gram is a success. Step 10: Clean up. Fill your holes. Take your trash. For a team exercise, split into groups: diggers, carriers, panners. Time yourselves. See who finds the most gold in 2 hours. This builds skill and speed. Remember: the goal is to learn, not to get rich in one day. Repeat this exercise weekly. Keep a log of your best spots. In Africa, many successful miners started with just this simple test. Good luck!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Alluvial and River Mining

This practical reference brings together the working numbers, field rules of thumb, and decision aids that a small-scale alluvial gold operator in Africa needs on site. It is written for the artisan or small team running a sluice, pan, or river dredge, and for the buyer, trainer, or cooperative officer supporting them. The focus is on recoverable value per cubic metre of gravel, the cost of the inputs that create that value, and the safety and legal controls that keep the operation running.

Alluvial gold is placer gold: free gold that has been eroded from primary reef deposits and re-deposited in stream beds, floodplains, terraces, and paleochannels. It is typically fine, flaky, and often coated, which makes recovery harder than coarse nugget gold. In most African artisanal settings, alluvial mining is the lowest-capital entry point into gold production, but the recovery efficiency of an untrained crew is often below 40 percent of the gold actually present in the gravel. Closing that gap is the single largest margin improvement available to a small operation.

â  SAFETY WARNING â MERCURY AND RIVER WORK

Do not use mercury to amalgamate alluvial gold in a river or on a sluice. Mercury is highly toxic, and its use contaminates water, fish, and the community downstream. It is illegal or tightly restricted in most African countries, including under the Minamata Convention on Mercury, which most African states have ratified. If you encounter mercury on a site, stop work, do not touch it, and report it. Separately, never work alone in or beside a river: flash floods, undercut banks, and suction dredge entrapment kill artisanal miners every year. Always use a spotter, a rope, and a buoyancy aid, and never enter water above knee depth without a second person on the bank.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test before you dig. Pan 5â10 sample holes across the gravel body before committing a crew. Confirm grade is above your break-even (see Table 2).
  2. Classify the feed. Remove oversize rocks and break up clay. Clay balls roll over riffles and carry fine gold out in the tailings.
  3. Set the slope. Keep the sluice at 1 in 12 to 1 in 20. Too steep and gold washes out; too flat and gravel builds up.
  4. Control water depth. Maintain 25â50 mm of water over the riffles. A visible boil behind each riffle means the flow is right.
  5. Feed steadily. A consistent, thin feed recovers more gold than dumping buckets in surges.
  6. Clean up on schedule. Clean the carpet and riffles every 2â4 hours of running, or at the end of each shift. Do not let concentrate build up.
  7. Secure your concentrate. Weigh, label, and lock up concentrate. Record the date, location, and volume processed.
  8. Re-process tailings. Run tailings once more through a finer setup. Recovered fine gold here is often pure profit.
  9. Comply and record. Keep your licence, water-use permit, and community agreement on site, and log daily production and sales.

Business note: Alluvial mining rewards discipline more than capital. The operators who make money are the ones who test grade before digging, control slope and water, clean up on time, and keep written records of every cubic metre and every gram. Treat the sluice as a production line, not a lucky dip, and the margin will follow.
