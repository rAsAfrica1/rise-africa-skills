🐄 Forage Quality Assessment and Optimization

How to judge, test, conserve and improve the forage that makes up most of every cattle diet, and how to turn a lab report into a buying decision.

Lesson 10.1~12 min

Forage Quality Parameters

In this lesson
  • Interpret ADF, NDF, crude protein and lignin on a forage analysis report
  • Calculate digestible dry matter, intake capacity and relative feed value from a lab result
  • Compare two forage offers on quality-adjusted price rather than on price per bale

A forage analysis looks intimidating until you learn that two numbers carry most of the meaning. ADF tells you how digestible the forage is. NDF tells you how much of it the animal can physically eat. Everything else is supporting detail.

Acid detergent fibre, ADF, represents cellulose plus lignin plus insoluble ash. It is the primary predictor of digestibility, and higher is worse. The published prediction equation is DDM% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), where DDM is digestible dry matter. The same equation is used to estimate TDN. Neutral detergent fibre, NDF, is total plant fibre, cellulose plus hemicellulose plus lignin. It is the primary predictor of voluntary intake, and again higher is worse. The published equation is DMI as a percentage of body weight = 120 / NDF%. Crude protein is simply nitrogen multiplied by 6.25. Lignin is the villain behind both: each 1 percent increase in ADF-bound lignin cuts digestibility by roughly 3 to 4 percentage units, and forages above about 12 percent lignin as a share of ADF are poor feeds regardless of what else the report says.

Relative feed value pulls the two main numbers together: RFV = DDM% x DMI% x 0.775. It is a single index for ranking hay lots. Relative forage quality, RFQ, is the modern dairy version and additionally accounts for NDF digestibility; it generally runs 5 to 15 percent higher than RFV for the same forage. In vitro dry matter digestibility, IVDMD, is a direct laboratory measurement rather than a prediction, and typically ranges from about 45 percent for poor hay to over 75 percent for excellent fresh forage.

Worked example, comparing two hay offers. Lot A is tested at 31 percent ADF and 52 percent NDF. Lot B is tested at 42 percent ADF and 66 percent NDF.

Lot A: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 31) = 88.9 - 24.1 = 64.8 percent. DMI = 120 / 52 = 2.31 percent of body weight. RFV = 64.8 x 2.31 x 0.775 = 116. Lot B: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 42) = 88.9 - 32.7 = 56.2 percent. DMI = 120 / 66 = 1.82 percent of body weight. RFV = 56.2 x 1.82 x 0.775 = 79.

Now apply that to a real animal. A 400 kg cow on Lot A can eat 400 x 0.0231 = 9.2 kg dry matter a day, of which 64.8 percent, or 6.0 kg, is digestible. On Lot B she can eat only 400 x 0.0182 = 7.3 kg, of which 56.2 percent, or 4.1 kg, is digestible. Lot B delivers barely two thirds of the digestible dry matter of Lot A. If Lot A costs 100 USD per tonne, Lot B is only worth 100 x (4.1 / 6.0) = 68 USD per tonne to you. If the seller of Lot B wants 90 USD, walking away is the correct commercial decision even though it is the cheaper bale.

That last calculation is the entire point of forage testing. A test that costs 20 to 40 USD routinely changes a purchasing decision worth hundreds. NIRS testing, which predicts ADF, NDF, CP, IVDMD and digestibility from light reflectance, is faster and cheaper than wet chemistry, with accuracy of roughly plus or minus 2 to 5 percent. Its major limitation is that its prediction equations are calibration-dependent, so accuracy varies by regional forage type and season. Ask the lab whether their equations are calibrated for tropical grasses before trusting a result on Napier or Brachiaria.

Concrete African context. A cattle trader near Arusha, Tanzania, buys standing hay from irrigated Rhodes grass blocks. Sellers price per acre regardless of cutting date. Two adjacent blocks looked identical, but one had been left standing an extra three weeks. Forage ADF rises roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week of additional maturity, so three extra weeks is potentially 6 to 12 ADF points, which by the DDM equation is 4.7 to 9.3 digestibility points lost. At the top of that range the later block is worth roughly 15 percent less per tonne. Once the trader started asking one question, when was this last cut or grazed, before naming a price, he stopped overpaying.

Working reference points for judging a report:

  1. ADF below about 31 percent indicates high quality; above 40 percent the forage is a maintenance feed at best.
  2. NDF below about 40 percent gives high intake; above 65 percent intake will be severely limited.
  3. Crude protein below 7.5 percent of diet DM means the rumen is nitrogen-limited and forage intake will collapse.
  4. Lignin above about 12 percent of ADF signals a poor feed regardless of the other figures.

Lesson 10.2~12 min

Forage Conservation: Hay, Silage, Haylage

In this lesson
  • Select the right conservation method for your rainfall pattern, labour supply and capital
  • Calculate real dry matter delivered after conservation losses
  • Judge fermentation quality by smell, colour and dry matter without laboratory equipment

The dry-season feed gap is the defining problem of African cattle production. Grass is abundant and cheap for three or four months and absent for five. Conservation is how you move feed across time, and the choice between hay, silage and haylage is mostly a question of your weather, your labour and your cash.

Hay is forage dried to roughly 88 percent dry matter. It is stable, storable, tradeable and needs no equipment beyond a cutting tool and a place to stack it. Its weakness is that it requires a reliable dry window of several days at cutting time, which in a bimodal rainfall zone is exactly what you often do not have. Rain on cut hay leaches soluble nutrients and encourages mould. Protein content depends heavily on cutting stage, and vitamin content falls with storage: beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage, so year-one hay retains roughly 50 to 67 percent of its original vitamin A value and year-two hay only 25 to 50 percent. Brown hay is severely depleted, which is why all cattle on old or weathered hay should receive vitamin A supplementation.

Silage is forage fermented under airtight conditions at roughly 28 to 35 percent dry matter. It preserves more of the original nutrients than hay because it does not depend on field drying, so it suits wet climates and it captures maize and sorghum crops at their peak. Its weakness is that it is heavy, it cannot travel far economically, and it punishes carelessness. Fermentation requires genuinely anaerobic conditions and takes 4 to 6 weeks to stabilise.

Haylage sits between the two at roughly 40 to 60 percent dry matter, wilted then sealed. It is lighter than silage, easier to feed out, and does not need as long a drying window as hay.

Fermentation is the biology that makes silage work. Lactic acid bacteria consume plant sugars and produce lactic and acetic acid, which drop the pH low enough to stop spoilage organisms. Good fermentation smells slightly sweet and sharp, like sour milk or vinegar. Bad fermentation, dominated by clostridia, produces butyric acid and smells rancid, like vomit; it comes from ensiling material that is too wet or too low in sugar. Ammonia smell indicates protein has been broken down and the feeding value is reduced.

Judging silage without a lab, in order of reliability:

  1. Smell. Sweet-sharp is good. Rancid butyric or strong ammonia is bad.
  2. Colour. Light green to olive-brown is good. Dark brown or black indicates overheating; slimy dark material indicates clostridial spoilage.
  3. Dry matter by the squeeze test. Squeeze a handful hard for 30 seconds. If liquid runs freely, it is too wet, below the 28 percent target. If it springs apart with no moisture on your palm, it is too dry to pack. If it holds shape and your palm is damp, you are near the 28 to 35 percent target.
  4. Mould. Visible mould means discard that layer, and it flags a mycotoxin risk in the rest.

Worked example on losses. You cut 10 tonnes of fresh Napier at 22 percent dry matter, so 2.2 tonnes of dry matter. Storage losses on high-moisture feeds run around 15 percent, against about 3 percent for dry feeds. Ensiled, you recover roughly 2.2 x 0.85 = 1.87 tonnes DM. If instead you could dry it successfully to hay, you would recover roughly 2.2 x 0.97 = 2.13 tonnes DM, but only if the weather holds; a rained-on hay crop can lose far more than 15 percent and may lose the lot. In a wet climate the reliable 85 percent beats the theoretical 97 percent.

Now cost it. A 400 kg cow needs roughly 9 kg dry matter a day. Over a 150-day dry season that is 1,350 kg, or 1.35 tonnes of dry matter per cow. Your 1.87 tonnes of silage dry matter carries about 1.4 cows through the dry season. Five cows therefore need roughly 6.75 tonnes of conserved dry matter, which at 22 percent DM means cutting and ensiling about 30 tonnes of fresh material. That is the number most farmers never calculate, and it is why silage pits are routinely built at a third of the size needed.

Concrete African context. Tube silage, made in 60 to 100 kg polythene tubes rather than a pit, has spread across Kenyan and Ugandan smallholder dairy because it needs no concrete, no tractor and no minimum scale. Cost is roughly 1 to 2 USD per tube for the polythene plus labour, and a farmer with three cows can make 40 to 60 tubes over a few weekends. The critical discipline is chop length of 2 to 3 cm, hard compaction, and a completely airtight seal; a single rat hole ruins a tube.

Lesson 10.3~12 min

Legumes vs Grasses in Rations

In this lesson
  • Compare the nutritional profile of legume and grass forages and explain the differences
  • Calculate the protein and calcium contribution of adding legume to a grass-based ration
  • Plan a legume integration that reduces purchased protein cost

Grasses and legumes do different jobs, and a ration built on one alone is usually leaving money on the table. Grasses are the bulk and the energy: Napier, Rhodes, Brachiaria, star grass, maize stover. Legumes are the protein and much of the calcium: lucerne (alfalfa), lablab, Desmodium, calliandra, leucaena, cowpea haulms, groundnut haulms.

The consistent differences are these. Legumes carry more protein than grasses at the same stage, and they are high in rumen-degradable protein, which is exactly what a nitrogen-limited rumen needs. Legumes are markedly higher in calcium. Legumes also tend to have lower NDF at a comparable stage, which means higher intake, and their digestibility declines more slowly with maturity than grasses because the leaf-to-stem ratio holds up better. Grasses, in exchange, yield more tonnes of dry matter per hectare, are easier to establish, and tolerate harder grazing.

On the protein fraction, legume forages behave differently once conserved. Alfalfa haylage runs about 40 to 45 percent RUP as a share of crude protein, so a well-made legume haylage supplies useful bypass protein as well as degradable protein. Fresh legume forage is mostly degradable.

Worked example. A 400 kg cow is eating 9 kg dry matter a day of Napier at 8 percent crude protein and 68 percent NDF. Current protein supply = 9 x 0.08 = 0.72 kg, and diet crude protein is 8 percent. Her intake capacity from that NDF is 120 / 68 = 1.76 percent of body weight, so 7.1 kg, meaning she is already at or above her physical limit at 9 kg and is probably not really eating that much.

Now replace 3 kg of the Napier dry matter with Desmodium or lablab hay at, say, 17 percent crude protein and 48 percent NDF. New diet: 6 kg Napier plus 3 kg legume. Protein = (6 x 0.08) + (3 x 0.17) = 0.48 + 0.51 = 0.99 kg, from 9 kg dry matter, so 11.0 percent crude protein, up from 8 percent. Diet NDF = ((6 x 68) + (3 x 48)) / 9 = (408 + 144) / 9 = 61.3 percent, so intake capacity rises to 120 / 61.3 = 1.96 percent of body weight, or 7.8 kg. Both the protein concentration and the physical intake capacity improved from the same swap.

Cost it. To supply that same extra 0.27 kg of crude protein from cottonseed cake at 38 percent CP you would need 0.27 / 0.38 = 0.71 kg of cake. At 0.40 USD/kg that is 0.28 USD per cow per day, about 104 USD per cow per year, or 520 USD a year across five cows. Established Desmodium or lablab supplies it from your own land. That is the arithmetic that should drive every decision about whether to plant a fodder legume.

There are cautions. Legume pasture grazed wet carries a bloat risk, covered in Module 11. Some tree legumes carry anti-nutritional compounds and should be fed as a portion of the diet rather than alone; leucaena in particular is normally kept to a modest share of total dry matter. And a fodder legume takes a season to establish, so the decision has to be made a year before the benefit arrives.

Concrete African context. The push-pull and fodder shrub programmes across western Kenya and eastern Uganda have promoted calliandra and Desmodium alongside Napier for exactly this reason. Extension guidance commonly frames it as a simple substitution: a few kilograms of fresh calliandra leaf can displace a comparable amount of purchased dairy meal in a smallholder ration. Farmers who plant a hedge along their boundary get a protein source that costs nothing per day once established, needs no bag, cannot be adulterated by a trader, and does not fluctuate with the maize price.

Practical plan for integration:

  1. Start with 20 to 30 percent of forage dry matter from legume, not more, and observe intake and dung.
  2. Plant fodder legumes on land you are not using: boundaries, terrace edges, under-utilised corners.
  3. Conserve legume as hay or haylage where possible, since the dry season is when its protein is worth most.
  4. Track your purchased protein spend before and after; that number is the proof the legume is working.

Lesson 10.4~12 min

Pasture Management and Nutritional Value

In this lesson
  • Match grazing timing to the growth stage where forage quality peaks
  • Apply a rotational grazing plan that maintains quality without buying inputs
  • Recognise the pasture conditions that create nitrate and grass tetany risk

Pasture is the cheapest feed a cattle producer will ever have, and its quality is controlled almost entirely by timing. A pasture at the vegetative stage is a high-quality feed. The same pasture six weeks later, headed and flowering, is a maintenance ration. Nothing was bought or sold in between; the value simply evaporated because nobody moved the cattle.

The quality curve is predictable. Vegetative growth has the highest digestibility and protein and the lowest fibre. As the plant moves through boot stage, heading and flowering, stem increases, lignin increases, protein falls and fibre rises. The published rule is that ADF increases roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week of additional maturity. Run that through the digestibility equation: three weeks of delay at 3 points a week is 9 ADF points, and 9 x 0.779 = 7.0 digestibility points lost. On a forage at 62 percent DDM, that is a fall to 55 percent, more than a tenth of the feeding value gone.

Rotational grazing is the tool that manages this. The principle is to graze a paddock hard for a short period, then move on and let it recover, so that cattle are always eating regrowth rather than mature stem. Practically, on a small farm this means dividing available grazing into as few as four to eight paddocks with cheap fencing or a herder's discipline, grazing each for a few days, and returning only after the grass has recovered to grazing height. The benefits published for rotational systems include reduced erosion, improved forage quality and the potential for soil carbon gain.

Seasonality matters and differs by grass type. Cool-season grasses peak in spring and autumn; warm-season grasses peak in summer. Across most of tropical Africa the dominant grasses are warm-season, so quality peaks in and just after the rains and falls away hard as the dry season sets in. Legume inclusion, such as clover or Desmodium in the sward, improves both protein and palatability and extends the useful window.

Two risks arise from pasture specifically, both covered in more detail elsewhere but flagged here because they are grazing-management decisions.

First, nitrate. Plants accumulate nitrate under drought, reduced light and heavy nitrogen fertilisation. Below 0.5 percent nitrate in dry matter, that is under 5,000 ppm, forage is safe. From 0.5 to 1.5 percent it needs caution, and the published management is to feed a minimum of 2 lb of energy grain per head daily alongside. From 1.5 to 3.0 percent it is unsafe for grazing, and above 3.0 percent it must not be fed at all. Drought-stressed oats, maize and sorghum, and heavily fertilised pasture, are the high-risk cases. The first rain after a drought is the classic danger point, because stressed plants take up nitrate faster than they can convert it.

Second, grass tetany risk, which is a spring and lush-growth phenomenon. Rapid growth with high potassium, nitrogen and calcium interferes with magnesium absorption. The published pasture management guidance is to delay grazing until the sward is at least 6 inches high and to reduce nitrogen fertiliser applications in autumn and spring. Forage magnesium below 0.15 percent of dry matter, a potassium to magnesium ratio above 10 to 1, or a potassium to calcium-plus-magnesium ratio above 2.2, all signal high risk.

Worked example. A farm has 8 hectares of Brachiaria grazed continuously by 20 head. The manager splits it into 6 paddocks of roughly 1.3 hectares and grazes each for 4 days, giving each paddock 20 days of rest. Instead of cattle grazing regrowth the day it appears, the grass reaches proper grazing height. If this holds average sward ADF at 33 instead of 40 percent, DDM moves from 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 57.7 percent to 88.9 - (0.779 x 33) = 63.2 percent, a gain of 5.5 digestibility points. Across 20 head each eating 8 kg dry matter a day, that is 20 x 8 x 0.055 = 8.8 kg of extra digestible dry matter per day for the herd, delivered by fencing and a calendar.

Concrete African context. In the pastoral systems of northern Kenya and southern Ethiopia, traditional grazing reserves held back for the dry season, sometimes called kalo or seasonal enclosures, are rotational grazing under a different name and have operated for generations. Where these institutions have weakened, forage quality and dry-season survival have both fallen. The lesson is that the technology here is not fencing wire; it is an agreement about when animals may enter.

Lesson 10.5~12 min

Forage-Based Diet Formulation

In this lesson
  • Match a forage to an animal's published nutrient requirement and identify the shortfall
  • Calculate the exact quantity of supplement needed to close an energy or protein gap
  • Decide whether buying better forage beats buying more concentrate

Formulating a forage-based diet is four steps: find the requirement, measure what the forage delivers, calculate the gap, and buy the cheapest thing that closes it. Most farms skip straight to step four and buy a bag.

Step one, the requirement. Published figures give you anchors. A 500 lb (227 kg) steer gaining 1.5 lb per day needs 1.33 lb of crude protein per day, which is 10.5 percent of diet dry matter, and 8.0 lb of TDN per day, which is 59.5 percent of diet dry matter, plus calcium at 0.35 to 0.45 percent and phosphorus at 0.20 to 0.25 percent of diet DM. A 1,200 lb lactating beef cow giving 10 lb of milk a day needs 2.2 to 2.5 lb of crude protein and 12.8 lb of TDN daily, with calcium 0.35 to 0.45 percent, phosphorus 0.25 to 0.30 percent, magnesium at least 0.20 percent and 34,000 IU of vitamin A. At 20 lb of milk a day at 3.8 percent fat the TDN requirement rises to 17.5 lb, and crude protein to 3.0 to 3.4 lb. A 400 kg growing animal gaining 1.0 lb/day needs 0.88 lb crude protein, at 1.5 lb/day needs 1.33 lb, and at 2.0 lb/day needs 1.60 lb. Finishing cattle around 600 kg gaining 2.0 to 3.5 lb/day need only 8.5 to 9.5 percent crude protein of diet dry matter, which is why protein supplements are wasted late in a finishing period.

Step two, what the forage delivers. This is Lesson 1 applied: TDN from ADF, intake from NDF, protein from the report or a table.

Worked example, a full formulation. Take a 227 kg (500 lb) steer targeted at 1.5 lb per day gain. Requirement: 1.33 lb (0.60 kg) crude protein and 8.0 lb (3.63 kg) TDN per day.

Available forage is a grass hay at 40 percent ADF, 65 percent NDF, 7 percent crude protein. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 57.7 percent. Intake capacity = 120 / 65 = 1.85 percent of body weight, so 227 x 0.0185 = 4.2 kg dry matter per day.

What the hay delivers: TDN = 4.2 x 0.577 = 2.42 kg. Crude protein = 4.2 x 0.07 = 0.29 kg. Shortfall: TDN 3.63 - 2.42 = 1.21 kg. Crude protein 0.60 - 0.29 = 0.31 kg.

Close the gap. Maize bran at roughly 70 percent TDN and 11 percent crude protein: 1.21 / 0.70 = 1.73 kg would cover the energy but supplies only 1.73 x 0.11 = 0.19 kg protein, leaving 0.12 kg short. Add cottonseed cake at about 38 percent CP: 0.12 / 0.38 = 0.32 kg. So the supplement is roughly 1.7 kg maize bran plus 0.3 kg cottonseed cake. Note that adding 2 kg of supplement will displace some hay intake, so in practice you would feed slightly less hay and re-check.

Cost it. At maize bran 0.22 USD/kg and cottonseed cake 0.40 USD/kg: (1.7 x 0.22) + (0.3 x 0.40) = 0.374 + 0.12 = 0.49 USD per head per day. Over a 120-day growing period that is 59 USD per animal.

Now the alternative. Suppose you could instead buy a leafy hay at 31 percent ADF and 52 percent NDF and 10 percent crude protein. TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 31) = 64.8 percent. Intake = 120 / 52 = 2.31 percent, so 5.24 kg. Delivered TDN = 5.24 x 0.648 = 3.40 kg, and protein = 5.24 x 0.10 = 0.52 kg. Now the shortfall is only 0.23 kg TDN and 0.08 kg protein, closed with roughly 0.35 kg of maize bran and 0.2 kg of cake, about 0.16 USD a day. You could afford to pay substantially more per tonne for the better hay and still come out ahead. This is the calculation that turns a farmer into a buyer.

Concrete African context. Feedlot operators around Adama, Ethiopia, fattening Boran and Arsi bulls for the Middle East export market, live or die on this arithmetic because both hay and concentrate prices swing sharply between seasons. The operators who survive keep a wall chart of the cost per kg of TDN and per kg of crude protein from every locally available feed, updated monthly, and reformulate rather than staying loyal to one ration.

Rules:

  1. Never buy a supplement before you have calculated the gap.
  2. Recalculate whenever any input price moves more than about 10 percent.
  3. Good forage often beats cheap concentrate, and quality hay can support more production than poor hay plus concentrate.
  4. Late-finishing cattle need less protein than you think; do not keep paying for cake at 8.5 to 9.5 percent requirement.

Lesson 10.6~13 min

Silage Quality and Safety

In this lesson
  • Identify spoiled silage and decide what to discard and what to feed
  • State the regulatory limits for the main mycotoxins in cattle feed and milk
  • Manage prussic acid and nitrate risk in sorghum and drought-stressed forage

Conserved forage that has gone wrong is worse than no forage at all, because it costs money, damages animals and contaminates milk. This lesson covers the three ways stored forage turns dangerous: bad fermentation, mould toxins and plant toxins.

Bad fermentation first. Good silage is light green to olive-brown, smells sweet and slightly acidic, sits at 28 to 35 percent dry matter, and has minimal visible mould. Poor silage is dark, smells of ammonia or rancid butter, and has visible mould. Ammonia smell means protein has been degraded and feeding value is down. Butyric smell means clostridial fermentation from material ensiled too wet. Visible mould at the top of a pit means air got in; discard those layers rather than mixing them through, because the mould that grew there may have produced toxins that no smell test will find.

Mycotoxins are the serious risk, and dairy limits are stricter than beef limits because toxins reach human food through milk. The published figures:

  • Aflatoxin: 20 micrograms per kg (ppb) in feed for both dairy and beef. Between 2.0 and 6.2 percent of intake transfers into milk, and the milk safety limit is 0.5 micrograms per kg, set to protect human infants. Risk factors are heat plus moisture, particularly in maize, groundnuts and cottonseed meal. Visible mould is not always present. Signs are feed refusal, milk drop, diarrhoea and immune suppression.
  • Fumonisin: FDA guidance of 15 mg/kg for lactating dairy and breeding cattle. Milk transfer is minimal and over 80 percent is excreted in faeces. Ruminants tolerate it better than pigs or poultry because the rumen degrades it.
  • Deoxynivalenol (DON, vomitoxin): FDA advisory of 5 mg/kg in grains where grain is no more than 40 percent of the diet. Rumen microbes convert it to the less toxic DOM-1. Cattle are more resistant than swine, but feed refusal appears at high levels.
  • Zearalenone: above 10 mg/kg causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers, and above 20 mg/kg affects fertility in lactating cows. Over 90 percent is converted in the rumen to zearalenol, and the alpha form is about three times more oestrogenic than the parent compound. Signs are anoestrus and vaginal prolapse.
  • Ergot alkaloids: roughly 2 mg/kg of ergotine in the diet causes clinical signs, seen as dark hard sclerotia in contaminated grain, with vasoconstriction and lameness.

Worked example on aflatoxin economics. A dairy farmer with 10 cows producing 100 litres a day sells at 0.40 USD per litre, so 40 USD a day. A maize batch is suspected of aflatoxin. A mycotoxin screen at a regional lab costs, in round terms, in the range of 30 to 60 USD. If the batch is contaminated and the milk is rejected by the cooperative for even three days, that is 120 USD of lost sales plus the reputational cost of a positive test on record. The test pays for itself the first time it is right.

Plant toxins next. Prussic acid, or hydrogen cyanide, comes from cyanogenic glycosides in sorghum species, sudangrass, johnsongrass, oats and wheat, and from wild cherry. The lethal dose is around 4.4 mg HCN per kg of body weight per hour. Risk ranks from johnsongrass as highest, then sorghum hybrids, then sorghum-sudan hybrids, then sudangrass as lowest. The dangerous conditions are young plants, frost-damaged plants and rapidly regrowing plants; leaves are more hazardous than stems; fresh forage is worse than ensiled. The published management is unambiguous: do not feed green sorghum as fresh forage, because the safety margin is extremely narrow. Ensile it, and HCN dissipates after 3 to 4 weeks of ensiling. Or hay-cure it, which leaves very low residual HCN if well cured. Or wait for regrowth to reach 18 to 24 inches before grazing. Spring emergence is the highest risk window, at 1 to 2 weeks.

Nitrate is the other plant toxin, and it is the one that kills cattle in African drought years. Rumen bacteria convert nitrate to nitrite, which binds haemoglobin and blocks oxygen transport. At 30 to 40 percent methaemoglobin animals show lethargy, brown mucous membranes and muscle tremors; at 80 to 90 percent they die. Thresholds are below 0.5 percent of dry matter safe, 0.5 to 1.5 percent caution with at least 2 lb of energy grain per head daily, 1.5 to 3.0 percent unsafe, and above 3.0 percent highly toxic. Water matters too: the standard is 10 mg/L of nitrate-nitrogen, equivalent to 44 mg/L of nitrate. Drought-stressed oats, maize and sorghum, and pastures fertilised above roughly 100 lb of nitrogen per acre, are the high-risk cases.

Concrete African context. In Zimbabwe and northern South Africa, feeding drought-stressed maize and sorghum stover to cattle after a failed rainy season is standard practice and is a recurring cause of sudden stock losses. The two rules that prevent most of it cost nothing: never turn hungry cattle onto a drought-stressed cereal field, feed them hay first; and cut suspect material and ensile it for at least four weeks rather than grazing it green.

Storage discipline: keep feed below 12 to 14 percent moisture, rotate stock so nothing sits for years, inspect visually for discoloured grain and off-odours, and verify that your grain supplier has an aflatoxin testing protocol.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A hay tests at 36% ADF. What is its estimated digestible dry matter?

DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 36) = 88.9 - 28.0 = 60.9%.

2. A forage tests at 60% NDF. What is the estimated dry matter intake capacity?

DMI = 120 / 60 = 2.0% of body weight.

3. Which forage measurement is the primary predictor of voluntary intake?

NDF represents total plant fibre and is the primary predictor of how much an animal can physically consume; ADF predicts digestibility.

4. Roughly how much does forage ADF increase per additional week of maturity?

Mature forage ADF increases about 2 to 4 percentage units per week, which is why cutting date dominates hay value.

5. What is the main limitation of NIRS forage testing?

NIRS is fast and cheap with roughly 2-5% accuracy, but its equations must be calibrated for the forage types being tested.

6. What is the target dry matter range for good silage?

Good silage is 28-35% DM. Wetter material tends toward butyric clostridial fermentation; drier material will not compact properly.

7. A silage pit smells strongly rancid, like vomit. What does this indicate?

Butyric acid from clostridial fermentation is the classic sign of ensiling material that was too wet or too low in sugar.

8. Typical storage losses for high-moisture feeds are about:

Published planning figures are about 3% loss for dry feeds and about 15% for high-moisture feeds.

9. Two-year-old brown hay is being fed. What supplementation is most clearly indicated?

Beta-carotene degrades 33-50% per year of storage, leaving year-two hay at only 25-50% of original vitamin A. Brown hay is severely depleted.

10. How long does silage need to ferment before it is stable enough to open?

Fermentation requires anaerobic conditions and 4-6 weeks to stabilise.

11. Compared with grasses at the same stage, legume forages are generally:

Legumes carry more protein and calcium and generally lower NDF at comparable maturity, which raises both protein supply and intake capacity.

12. A diet of 6 kg Napier at 8% CP plus 3 kg legume hay at 17% CP gives what diet crude protein?

(6 x 0.08) + (3 x 0.17) = 0.99 kg CP from 9 kg DM, which is 11.0%.

13. What proportion of crude protein in alfalfa haylage is rumen-undegradable?

Alfalfa haylage runs about 40-45% RUP of crude protein, making conserved legume a partial bypass protein source.

14. What is a sensible starting share of legume in forage dry matter?

20-30% of forage dry matter lifts protein meaningfully while limiting bloat risk and the effects of any anti-nutritional compounds.

15. Why does legume digestibility decline more slowly with maturity than grass?

Legumes retain a better leaf-to-stem ratio with age, so quality falls off less sharply than in grasses, which stem rapidly.

16. At which growth stage is pasture nutritional value highest?

Quality declines steadily from vegetative through boot, heading and flowering as stem and lignin increase and protein falls.

17. Forage nitrate is measured at 2.0% of dry matter. What is the correct action?

1.5-3.0% nitrate in DM is classed as unsafe and not recommended for grazing. Caution feeding with grain applies only in the 0.5-1.5% band.

18. A pasture is delayed three weeks before grazing, adding about 9 ADF points. Roughly how much digestibility is lost?

9 ADF points x 0.779 = 7.0 digestibility points lost, using DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF).

19. Which pasture condition most raises grass tetany risk?

Rapid growth with high K, N, P and Ca interferes with magnesium absorption, which is why lush spring pasture is the classic tetany setting.

20. What is the core principle of rotational grazing?

Short grazing periods followed by rest keep the sward in the high-quality vegetative stage and reduce erosion.

21. A 500 lb steer targeted at 1.5 lb/day gain requires how much TDN per day?

Published requirement is 8.0 lb TDN per day, which is 59.5% of diet dry matter, alongside 1.33 lb of crude protein.

22. Finishing cattle around 600 kg gaining 2-3.5 lb/day require what dietary crude protein?

Protein requirement falls as cattle approach finish, so 8.5-9.5% is sufficient and extra protein supplement is money wasted.

23. A hay at 40% ADF is fed to a 227 kg steer whose intake capacity is 4.2 kg DM. How much TDN does the hay deliver?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 57.7%. 4.2 kg x 0.577 = 2.42 kg TDN.

24. How much TDN does a 1,200 lb cow producing 20 lb of milk per day at 3.8% fat require?

The published figure is 17.5 lb TDN daily at 20 lb milk, up from 12.8 lb at 10 lb of milk.

25. What is the correct first step before buying any supplement?

You cannot buy the cheapest solution to a gap you have not measured. Requirement minus forage supply defines what and how much to buy.

26. What is the milk safety limit for aflatoxin?

The feed limit is 20 micrograms/kg but the milk limit is 0.5 micrograms/kg, set to protect human infants, with 2.0-6.2% of intake transferring.

27. A farmer wants to feed green regrowing sorghum to cattle. What is the correct advice?

Young and rapidly regrowing sorghum is the highest prussic acid risk and the safety margin on fresh feeding is extremely narrow. Ensiling for 3-4 weeks or curing as hay removes most HCN.

28. Zearalenone above what level causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers?

Above 10 mg/kg affects growing heifers and above 20 mg/kg affects lactating cow fertility.

29. Visible mould is found on the top layer of a silage pit. What is the correct action?

Mould that has grown in an aerobic top layer may carry mycotoxins that smell and appearance will not reveal, so those layers are discarded, not diluted.

30. At what proportion of methaemoglobin do cattle typically die from nitrate poisoning?

Symptoms such as lethargy and brown mucous membranes appear at 30-40% methaemoglobin; death occurs at 80-90%.

Module 10 capstone

Forage Value and Buying Decision Project using your own land and local market. Step 1: sample the three main forages you use or could buy (for example your own Napier or Rhodes hay, a neighbour's maize stover, and a commercially offered bale). Sample properly: take a core or a handful from at least 10 different bales or 10 points in the field, mix them in a bucket, and take a 500 g subsample per forage. Step 2: get ADF, NDF and crude protein on each, either from a local lab or from NIRS if available; if no lab is reachable, use the closest published values for that forage type and cutting stage and mark clearly that they are estimates. Step 3: for each forage calculate DDM using DDM% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), DMI capacity using DMI (% of body weight) = 120 / NDF%, and RFV using RFV = DDM% x DMI% x 0.775. Step 4: convert TDN to energy with NEL Mcal/kg = (0.0245 x TDN%) - 0.12 or NEg Mcal/kg = (0.0257 x TDN%) - 0.315 depending on whether the animals are dairy or growing beef. Step 5: get an actual delivered price in USD per bale or per tonne for each option, convert to USD per kg of dry matter (remember dry hay is roughly 88% DM and silage 28-35% DM), and calculate USD per Mcal and USD per kg of crude protein for each. Step 6: pick a target animal, for example a 400 kg cow, and calculate whether each forage alone can physically supply enough intake to meet requirement using the DMI equation. Step 7: write a one-page buying recommendation stating which forage you will buy, at what maximum price you will still buy it, the price above which you will walk away, and what you will grow or conserve differently next season to avoid needing to buy at all.
