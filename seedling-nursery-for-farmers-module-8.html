1. Nutrient Needs of Seedlings

Seedlings need three main nutrients: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Nitrogen makes leaves green and helps stems grow. Phosphorus builds strong roots. Potassium helps the plant resist disease and use water well. Young seedlings also need small amounts of calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients like zinc and boron. Without enough nutrients, seedlings become stunted, yellow, or weak. But too much can burn them.

At the seedling stage, roots are small and delicate. They cannot take up large amounts of fertilizer. A common mistake is to feed seedlings the same strength as mature plants. That will kill them. Instead, use a weak solution. For example, mix 1 teaspoon of NPK 20-20-20 in 5 liters of water. This gives a gentle feed. Apply it once a week after the seedlings have two true leaves. Before that, they live on seed reserves.

Different seedlings have different needs. Tomatoes and peppers are heavy feeders. They benefit from a balanced fertilizer. Leafy greens like kale and spinach need more nitrogen. Onions and carrots prefer less nitrogen and more phosphorus for root growth. Know your crop. In Africa, many farmers grow tomatoes, peppers, kale, and onions in nurseries. Adjust your feeding to match.

Watch your seedlings. If leaves are pale green or yellow, they need nitrogen. If stems are weak and plants fall over, they may need more potassium. If growth is slow and roots are poor, phosphorus may be lacking. But always check watering and sunlight first. Nutrients only work if other conditions are good. A simple soil test kit can tell you what your soil lacks. These kits are cheap and available at agro-dealer shops.

Remember, seedlings in a nursery will stay for 3 to 6 weeks. During that time, they need steady but mild nutrition. Start with a weak liquid feed, then gradually increase as they grow. Never fertilize dry soil. Water first, then feed. This prevents root burn. By matching nutrients to seedling needs, you grow strong, healthy transplants that will perform well in the field.

2. Organic Fertilizers: Compost Tea, Manure Tea

Organic fertilizers feed both the plant and the soil. They are made from natural materials like compost, manure, and plants. Two easy ones for nurseries are compost tea and manure tea. They are cheap, safe, and improve soil life. You can make them yourself.

To make compost tea: Put 1 part mature compost into 5 parts water in a bucket. Stir well. Let it sit for 2 to 3 days, stirring twice a day. Strain through a cloth. The liquid is compost tea. Dilute it 1:1 with water before using. Apply it to seedlings every 7 to 10 days. It gives a gentle boost of nutrients and beneficial microbes. You can also add a handful of sugar or molasses to feed the microbes, but that is optional.

Manure tea is similar. Use well-rotted manure from cows, goats, chickens, or rabbits. Never use fresh manureit can burn plants and carry diseases. Put 1 part manure in 10 parts water. Let it soak for 5 to 7 days. Strain and dilute 1:1 with water. Chicken manure is the strongest, so use less. Manure tea is rich in nitrogen. It is good for leafy greens. For tomatoes, mix it with compost tea for a balanced feed.

Apply teas to the soil around the seedling base, not on the leaves. If you get it on leaves, rinse with plain water. Use within a few hours of making. Organic teas can smell bad and attract flies. Keep buckets covered. In hot weather, make smaller batches more often. These teas are not as concentrated as chemical fertilizers, so you can use them more frequently without burning. But they still can burn if too strong. Always dilute.

In Africa, many farmers have access to manure and compost. Making tea turns these into a liquid feed that seedlings can absorb quickly. It is a sustainable way to nourish your nursery. You can also add wood ash to manure tea for potassium. Use 1 cup of ash per 20 liters of tea. This helps flowering and fruiting crops. Organic teas are not a complete fertilizer, but they are an excellent supplement. Combine them with good soil and proper watering for best results.

3. Inorganic Fertilizers: NPK and Micronutrients

Inorganic fertilizers are manufactured. They are concentrated and fast-acting. The most common is NPK, which stands for nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). The numbers on the bag, like 20-20-20, show the percentage of each nutrient. For seedlings, use a balanced NPK or one slightly higher in phosphorus, like 10-20-10, to promote roots. Avoid high-nitrogen fertilizers like 46-0-0 (urea) on young seedlingsthey can burn.

To use NPK: Dissolve 1 teaspoon of 20-20-20 in 5 liters of water. This makes a stock solution. For seedlings, dilute this stock further: 1 part stock to 3 parts water. Apply once a week. Or use 1/2 teaspoon per liter of water. Always water the seedlings first, then apply the fertilizer solution. Never apply to dry soil. After feeding, water lightly again to wash nutrients to the roots.

Micronutrients are needed in tiny amounts. They include iron, zinc, manganese, copper, boron, and molybdenum. A lack of micronutrients causes problems like yellow leaves between veins (iron), curled new leaves (boron), or stunted growth (zinc). You can buy micronutrient mixes at agro-dealers. Use them at the recommended rate, usually 1 gram per liter of water. Do not overdosemicronutrients can be toxic if too much is applied.

In Africa, many soils are deficient in zinc and boron. If your seedlings show deficiency signs, a micronutrient spray can help. But first, check your soil pH. If pH is too high or too low, nutrients get locked up. Most seedlings prefer pH 6.0 to 6.8. You can test with a simple pH kit. Add lime to raise pH or sulfur to lower it. But do this before planting, not during nursery.

Store inorganic fertilizers in a dry place, away from children and animals. Wear gloves when handling. Mix only what you need. Do not store mixed solution for more than a day. Inorganic fertilizers are powerful tools. Use them carefully. For seedlings, always err on the side of weaker strength. You can always add more, but you cannot take away burn. Combine with organic matter for best soil health.

4. Foliar Feeding Basics

Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertilizer directly onto plant leaves. Leaves can absorb nutrients quickly. This is useful when seedlings show a deficiency or when roots are damaged. It gives a fast boost. But it is not a replacement for soil feeding. Use it as a supplement.

To foliar feed, mix a water-soluble fertilizer like NPK 20-20-20 at half the recommended strength. For example, if the label says 1 teaspoon per liter, use 1/2 teaspoon per liter. Fill a clean spray bottle or knapsack sprayer. Spray in the early morning or late afternoon when it is cool. Avoid midday sundroplets can act like magnifying glasses and burn leaves. Spray the undersides of leaves too, because they have more stomata (pores) for absorption.

For micronutrient deficiencies, use a micronutrient mix. Dissolve according to label, usually 1 gram per liter. Add a few drops of liquid soap to help the spray stick to leaves. Spray every 7 to 10 days until symptoms improve. Do not spray more than once a week. Too much foliar feeding can burn leaf edges or cause salt buildup.

Foliar feeding is especially good for seedlings that are recovering from transplant shock, pest damage, or nutrient lockout due to cold or wet soil. It can also be used to apply calcium to prevent blossom end rot in tomatoes. Use calcium nitrate at 1 teaspoon per liter. Spray on leaves and fruit.

In Africa, many farmers use knapsack sprayers for foliar feeding. Make sure the sprayer is clean and has never held herbicides. Even a tiny residue can kill your seedlings. Rinse thoroughly with soap and water. Also, do not mix foliar fertilizer with pesticides unless you know they are compatible. Mixing can cause clogs or leaf burn.

After spraying, check plants the next day. If leaves look burned or curled, stop and rinse with plain water. Foliar feeding is a precise tool. Use it when needed, not as a routine. For most seedlings, soil feeding with organic or inorganic fertilizers is enough. Foliar feeding is a first-aid measure for quick fixes.

5. Avoiding Fertilizer Burn

Fertilizer burn happens when too much fertilizer is applied or when it is applied to dry roots. Symptoms include yellow or brown leaf edges, wilting, stunted growth, and white crust on soil. Seedlings are very sensitive. A small overdose can kill them. Prevention is better than cure.

First, always water seedlings before fertilizing. Moist soil dilutes the fertilizer and protects roots. Second, use the right strength. For seedlings, use half or quarter of the recommended rate for mature plants. For example, if a tomato plant in the field gets 1 tablespoon of NPK, a seedling gets 1/4 teaspoon. Third, never fertilize dry soil. Fourth, do not fertilize newly germinated seedlings. Wait until they have at least two true leaves.

If you use manure, make sure it is well-rotted. Fresh manure heats up and burns roots. Compost should be mature and crumbly, not hot. If you use inorganic fertilizers, measure carefully. Use a measuring spoon, not a random scoop. Over-application is common when farmers guess. A good rule: 1 teaspoon of 20-20-20 in 5 liters of water is safe for most seedlings. Apply this solution once a week.

If you see burn symptoms, act fast. Flush the soil with plain water to wash out excess salts. Do this two or three times. Then stop fertilizing for two weeks. Keep the seedlings in shade and water normally. They may recover. If leaves are badly burned, remove them. But do not remove more than one-third of the leaves at once.

In Africa, fertilizer burn is common when farmers use urea or DAP directly on seedlings. These are strong fertilizers. Urea has 46% nitrogen. It should never be used on seedlings unless heavily diluted. DAP (18-46-0) is also strong. Use only a pinch per seedling, mixed well into soil. Better yet, use a balanced liquid fertilizer. Remember, more fertilizer does not mean more growth. It often means dead plants. Always follow the label and start weak. You can always add more later.

6. Fertilization Schedule for Nursery Plants

A fertilization schedule keeps your seedlings healthy without overfeeding. The schedule depends on the crop and how long seedlings stay in the nursery. Most seedlings stay 3 to 6 weeks. Here is a general plan you can adjust.

Week 1: No fertilizer. Seedlings use seed reserves. Just water and light.

Week 2: When true leaves appear, start with a weak organic tea (compost or manure) diluted 1:1. Apply once. Or use 1/4 teaspoon of NPK 20-20-20 in 5 liters of water. Water first.

Week 3: Feed again with the same strength. If leaves are pale, use a slightly stronger mix: 1/2 teaspoon in 5 liters. For leafy greens, use manure tea. For tomatoes and peppers, use balanced NPK.

Week 4: Increase to 1 teaspoon in 5 liters. Add micronutrients if you see deficiency signs. Foliar feed with half-strength solution if needed. Continue watering regularly.

Week 5: Same as week 4. If seedlings are for sale or transplant soon, reduce nitrogen to harden them off. Too much nitrogen makes soft growth that wilts after transplant.

Week 6: Stop fertilizing 3 to 4 days before transplanting. Give only water. This helps seedlings adjust to field conditions.

For slow-growing seedlings like onions, extend the schedule. For fast growers like kale, you may harvest earlier. Always watch your plants. If they look dark green and vigorous, do not increase fertilizer. If they are pale, increase slightly. Keep a notebook. Write down what you applied and when. This helps you learn.

In Africa, many nurseries are in dry areas. In hot weather, fertilize more often but weaker. In cool weather, less often. If using organic teas, apply every 7 days. If using inorganic, every 10 to 14 days. Never fertilize on very wet soilwait for it to drain. Also, do not fertilize if plants are wilting from drought. Water first, then feed. A good schedule is flexible. It responds to the plants, not a fixed calendar. With practice, you will know what your seedlings need.

<h2>Practical Reference: Nutrition and Fertilization for African Seedling Nurseries</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core lessons of the Nutrition and Fertilization module into a single, field-ready guide. It is written for nursery operators who treat seedlings as a commercial product: every gram of fertilizer, every litre of compost tea, and every week of the production cycle has a cost and a return. The goal is uniform, vigorous, market-ready seedlings that transplant with minimal shock and a fertilization programme you can repeat, cost, and scale.</p>

<p>Across Africa, nursery businesses lose money in three predictable ways: over-fertilizing (burned, unsellable seedlings), under-fertilizing (pale, stunted seedlings that buyers reject), and inconsistent feeding (uneven batches that complicate pricing and delivery). This lesson gives you the numbers, schedules, and safety rules to avoid all three.</p>

<h3>1. What Seedlings Actually Need</h3>

<p>Seedlings are not small versions of mature plants. In the nursery phase, root volume is tiny and the growing medium holds limited nutrients, so demand is concentrated. The three macronutrients dominate:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Nitrogen (N)</strong> leaf and stem growth; the main driver of the green, stocky look buyers pay for.</li>
<li><strong>Phosphorus (P)</strong> root development and energy transfer; critical in the first two weeks after germination.</li>
<li><strong>Potassium (K)</strong> stem strength, disease resistance, and hardening before sale.</li>
</ul>

<p>Calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients (iron, zinc, manganese, boron) matter in smaller quantities but cause visible defects when missing yellow interveinal leaves (magnesium), stunted new growth (zinc), or tip burn (calcium). Most African nursery soils and composts are adequate in micronutrients; deficiencies usually appear in soilless media or after repeated cropping.</p>

<h3>4. Foliar Feeding Basics</h3>

<p>Foliar feeding delivers nutrients directly through leaves fast response when roots are damaged, when medium pH locks up nutrients, or when a deficiency appears mid-cycle. It supplements, never replaces, root feeding.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Best time:</strong> early morning or late afternoon; never in midday heat.</li>
<li><strong>Concentration:</strong> half the root-feeding rate; test on 10 seedlings first.</li>
<li><strong>Coverage:</strong> undersides of leaves, where stomata are concentrated.</li>
<li><strong>Additives:</strong> a few drops of mild soap improve wetting; avoid harsh detergents.</li>
<li><strong>Frequency:</strong> every 714 days, no more than twice a month.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Avoiding Fertilizer Burn</h3>

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING FERTILIZER BURN AND CHEMICAL HANDLING</strong>
<p>Over-concentrated or too-frequent fertilizer kills seedlings within 2448 hours. Symptoms: scorched leaf margins, white salt crust on the medium surface, wilting despite moist soil, and sudden collapse of young plants. If burn appears, flush the medium with clean water immediately and stop all feeding for 710 days.</p>
<p>Always wear gloves and eye protection when handling inorganic fertilizers and concentrated manure teas. Store fertilizers in labelled, sealed containers away from children, livestock, and food. Never mix fertilizers with bare hands. Wash hands and tools after use. Manure teas must be made from well-aged manure to reduce pathogen risk (E. coli, Salmonella).</p>
</div>

<p><strong>Prevention rules:</strong> never exceed label rates; always water the medium before feeding; never feed dry, stressed, or diseased seedlings; never feed in extreme heat; always dilute teas correctly; and keep a written record of every application so problems can be traced.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Test your water and medium before setting the feeding rate hard water raises pH and locks out nutrients.</li>
<li> Dilute every fertilizer, organic or inorganic, to seedling strength never full field strength.</li>
<li> Water the medium before feeding to protect young roots from salt burn.</li>
<li> Feed low and often: small weekly doses beat one large dose.</li>
<li> Switch from high-N to high-K in the final two weeks to harden seedlings.</li>
<li> Inspect leaves weekly for deficiency or burn symptoms and act within 48 hours.</li>
<li> Keep a written record of date, product, rate, and observed response for every batch.</li>
<li> Wear gloves and eye protection; store all fertilizers safely and label every container.</li>
</ul>
