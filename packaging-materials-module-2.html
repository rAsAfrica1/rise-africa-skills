1. Types of Paper and Paperboard

Paper and paperboard are everywhere in packaging. In Africa, common types include kraft paper, newsprint, and paperboard like SBS (solid bleached sulfate) and recycled chipboard. Kraft paper is strong and tear-resistant, often used for bags and wrapping. It comes in weights from 30 to 120 gsm (grams per square meter). Newsprint is cheap and used for inner wrapping, but it's weak and not water-resistant. Paperboard is thicker, usually above 200 gsm. SBS is white and food-safe, used for cosmetics and medicine boxes. Recycled chipboard is grey or brown, used for cereal boxes and shoe boxes. In many African markets, locally made paperboard from waste paper is common. For example, in Kenya, companies like Chandaria Industries recycle waste paper into paperboard for packaging. When choosing, consider strength, printability, and moisture resistance. For dry goods like tea leaves, a 300 gsm kraft paperboard works well. For frozen foods, you need polyethylene-coated board to resist moisture. Always check the caliper (thickness) in micrometers; typical folding boxboard is 300-500 microns. If you're starting a small packaging business, you can buy paperboard sheets from local suppliers or import from South Africa or Egypt. Test by folding a sample: if it cracks, it's not suitable for creasing. Remember, the right paper type saves money and protects your product.

2. Manufacturing Process of Paperboard

Paperboard is made from wood pulp or recycled paper fibers. The process starts with pulping: wood chips or waste paper are cooked with chemicals (like sodium hydroxide) or mechanically ground to separate fibers. In Africa, many small mills use waste paper, which is soaked and pulped in a hydrapulper. The pulp is then refined to improve fiber bonding. Next, the pulp slurry is poured onto a moving wire screen, where water drains and fibers form a mat. This mat is pressed between rollers to squeeze out more water, then dried over heated cylinders. For multi-layer board, different pulps are combined: top layer for printing, middle for bulk, bottom for strength. The dried board is then calendered (pressed between smooth rollers) to improve surface smoothness. Finally, it's cut into sheets or rolls. Key parameters: consistency (fiber to water ratio) is 0.5-1% at the wet end; drying temperature is 100-120Â°C. In Nigeria, local mills like Bel Papyrus produce paperboard from recycled fibers. If you're setting up a small operation, you can buy pre-pulped fiber or start with a simple Hollander beater. Quality control checks include basis weight (gsm), moisture content (should be 6-8%), and thickness. Moisture is critical: too dry, the board is brittle; too wet, it won't print well. Always store paperboard in a dry place, off the floor, to prevent warping. This process turns waste into valuable packaging, creating jobs and reducing imports.

3. Corrugated Board: Structure and Grades

Corrugated board is made of a fluted (wavy) layer sandwiched between flat linerboards. The flutes give strength and cushioning. Common flute types: A-flute (4.8 mm high, good cushioning), B-flute (3.2 mm, good crush resistance), C-flute (4.0 mm, a balance), E-flute (1.6 mm, thin, good for printing), and F-flute (0.8 mm, very thin). In Africa, B and C flutes are most common for shipping boxes. The structure can be single-wall (one flute), double-wall (two flutes), or triple-wall. Grades are based on linerboard weight: for example, 125 gsm liner with 120 gsm fluting gives a standard box. Bursting strength (measured in kPa) and edge crush test (ECT) determine quality. A typical single-wall box has an ECT of 4-6 kN/m. For heavy loads like bananas, use double-wall with ECT above 7 kN/m. Corrugated board is made on a corrugator: liner and medium are glued with starch adhesive. In Kenya, packaging companies like Bobmil Industries produce corrugated boxes for agriculture. When buying, ask for the flute type and ECT rating. To test, stand on a box: if it crushes easily, it's weak. Store corrugated boxes flat, away from moisture, as humidity reduces strength by up to 50%. For small businesses, you can buy corrugated sheets and assemble boxes manually. This is a low-cost way to package goods for local markets.

4. Paperboard Converting: Cutting, Creasing, and Folding

Converting paperboard into boxes involves cutting, creasing, and folding. Cutting is done with a die-cutter: a sharp steel rule bent into the shape of the box net. The die is pressed onto the board, cutting through. For small-scale, you can use a hand die or a laser cutter. Creasing creates a line where the board folds. A creasing rule (blunt edge) presses a groove into the board. The groove depth should be about 50% of board thickness. For a 400-micron board, crease width is 1.2 mm. If the crease is too shallow, the board cracks; too deep, it weakens. Folding is then done by hand or machine. For straight-line boxes, a folder-gluer applies glue and folds. In Africa, many small workshops use manual creasing tools and bone folders. Key steps: first, print the board (if needed). Then die-cut and crease in one pass. Remove waste (the skeleton). Fold along creases and glue the side seam. For lock-bottom boxes, no glue is needed; tabs interlock. Test the fold: it should be clean, no cracking. If cracking occurs, adjust crease width or use a softer board. For short runs, you can use a tabletop die-cutter from China, costing around $500. For larger runs, partner with a local converter. Always wear gloves when handling cut boards. Store finished boxes flat to prevent distortion. This process adds value to plain board, allowing you to sell custom boxes to bakeries, pharmacies, and retailers.

5. Printing and Coating on Paperboard

Printing on paperboard makes your package attractive and informative. Common methods: offset lithography (for high-quality, large runs), flexography (for corrugated, fast), and digital printing (for short runs, variable data). In Africa, flexo is popular for corrugated boxes because it's cheap and fast. Offset is used for folding cartons. Before printing, the board may be coated: clay coating gives a smooth, white surface for better ink adhesion. Water-based coatings protect against moisture and scuffing. For food packaging, use food-grade inks and coatings. Key parameters: ink viscosity (for flexo, 20-30 seconds in a Zahn cup), drying temperature (60-80Â°C). In Nigeria, printers like Printpak use offset for beverage cartons. If you're small-scale, start with digital printing: a desktop printer can print on pre-cut sheets, but you need a flatbed printer for thick board. Alternatively, screen printing works for simple designs. Always test print a sample: check for smudging, color accuracy, and adhesion. Use a tape test: press tape on the print, pull off; if ink comes off, it's not cured. Coatings can be applied online (during printing) or offline. For water resistance, apply a polyethylene or wax coating. But note: waxed board is not recyclable. For eco-friendly, use starch-based coatings. After printing, the board is die-cut and folded. Ensure the printed design aligns with the creases. This adds professionalism and can increase sales.

6. Testing and Quality Control for Paper Packaging

Testing ensures your paper packaging meets requirements. Key tests: basis weight (gsm) â weigh a 10x10 cm sample and multiply by 100. Thickness (caliper) â use a micrometer, measure at 5 points. Moisture content â use a moisture meter; should be 6-8%. For strength, burst test (Mullen) measures resistance to rupture; typical values: 200-400 kPa for folding boxboard. Edge crush test (ECT) for corrugated: cut a small sample, compress between plates; ECT in kN/m. For printing, check ink adhesion with tape test. For creasing, fold and inspect for cracks. In Africa, many small producers skip testing, leading to failures. Simple tools: a kitchen scale for basis weight, a caliper from a hardware store, and a homemade burst tester using a bicycle pump and pressure gauge. For corrugated, you can approximate ECT by stacking boxes and adding weight until failure. Record results in a logbook. If board fails, check storage: humidity above 60% weakens paper. Also, test after converting: boxes should withstand a drop from 1 meter with no damage. For food packaging, ensure no toxic inks. Always keep samples from each batch. If you buy from suppliers, ask for test certificates. In Kenya, KEBS has standards for paper packaging. Meeting them opens doors to supermarkets. Quality control saves money by preventing returns and recalls.

Why Paper and Paperboard Matter in African Agribusiness

Paper-based packaging dominates African food and agricultural export packaging for three commercial reasons: cost per unit is lower than rigid plastics or metal for dry goods; it is recyclable and increasingly demanded by EU and UK retail buyers under extended producer responsibility (EPR) rules; and it can be printed with high-quality branding at local converters in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Egypt, and Morocco. For exporters of coffee, tea, cocoa, nuts, and dried fruits, paperboard cartons and corrugated shippers are often the primary packaging format accepted by buyers.

However, paper and paperboard are moisture-sensitive. In humid tropical and coastal environments â Mombasa, Lagos, Dar es Salaam, Accra â board can lose compression strength by 30â50% within weeks if not protected. This is the single most common cause of collapsed pallets and rejected shipments in the region.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Dust, Chemicals, and Heat

Paper and paperboard converting generates fine cellulose dust that is combustible. In African converting plants with limited dust extraction, accumulated dust on rafters, motors, and conveyor frames has caused explosions. Ensure dust extraction is operational, no smoking or open flame near cutting and creasing stations, and that all electrical equipment is rated for the dust zone. Coating and printing solvents (especially in flexographic inks) must be stored in ventilated areas away from heat. Creasing and die-cutting presses cause severe hand injuries â never reach into a running press, and ensure all guards and two-hand controls are functional. When handling recycled board, wear dust masks; recycled fibre can carry mould spores in humid climates.

Converting and Printing: Practical Notes for African Converters

Cutting, creasing, and folding are the operations where most board failure occurs. For folding boxboard (FBB) and coated recycled board (CRB), the crease line must be parallel to the grain direction of the board, or the carton will crack on the fold. For corrugated board, score lines must be deep enough to allow folding without fracturing the liner but not so deep that they cut the fluting medium â a common defect in locally converted boxes.

Printing on paperboard in Africa is dominated by offset litho for folding cartons and flexography for corrugated. For food-contact applications, ensure inks are low-migration (LM) or use a barrier coating between ink and product. Water-based coatings are preferred over solvent-based for both cost and regulatory reasons in most African markets. If you are exporting to the EU, your converter must be able to demonstrate compliance with EU 10/2011 and, for recycled fibre, the relevant national food-contact rules.

Coating choices: PE extrusion gives excellent moisture barrier but complicates recycling; wax coatings are cheaper but can transfer to product at high temperatures (relevant for African ambient storage above 30Â°C); dispersion coatings are the emerging middle ground and are increasingly available from converters in South Africa and Egypt.

Testing and Quality Control on Receipt

You do not need a full laboratory to protect your business. A basic incoming QC regime for paperboard includes: visual inspection for moisture damage, delamination, and print defects; caliper measurement with a micrometer at three points per sheet; a simple Cobb test or water-drop test for moisture resistance; and a compression test on finished boxes using a basic compression tester or, at minimum, a stack test under load for 24 hours in ambient humidity. Record results by batch and supplier. In humid African conditions, re-test compression after 7 days of storage in your warehouse â not just on arrival.
