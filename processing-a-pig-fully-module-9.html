1. Cleaning and cooking tripe for local soups

Tripe is the stomach lining of the pig. It is cheap, nutritious, and makes rich soups. Start by rinsing the tripe in cold water to remove loose dirt. Then soak it in a basin with 2 tablespoons of salt and the juice of 2 lemons per liter of water for 30 minutes. This reduces smell. Scrub both sides with a stiff brush. If you have lime, rub it on and rinse. For tougher tripe, blanch it: put in a pot, cover with water, add 1 cup of vinegar, boil for 10 minutes, then drain and rinse again. Now cut into strips or squares about 2 cm wide. For local soup, you need: 1 kg cleaned tripe, 2 onions, 4 tomatoes, 2 tablespoons curry powder, salt, pepper, and 2 liters water. Boil tripe in water for 1.5 to 2 hours until tender. Add chopped onions, tomatoes, and spices. Simmer for 30 minutes. You can add vegetables like carrots or potatoes. Serve with rice, fufu, or ugali. To save time, use a pressure cooker: 45 minutes instead of 2 hours. Clean tripe sells for double the price of uncleaned. Package in 500g portions and freeze if you have electricity. In many markets, cooked tripe soup is sold in cups for quick income. Always wash hands and tools with soap after handling raw offal.

2. Preparing liver, kidneys, and heart for sale

Liver, kidneys, and heart are called 'red offal'. They spoil fast, so work quickly and keep everything cold. As soon as the pig is dressed, remove these organs. Rinse in cold water. For liver: trim off any green or dark spots (bile stains) and any tough membranes. Soak liver in milk or salt water for 30 minutes to reduce bitterness. Pat dry. Cut into 1 cm slices or cubes. For kidneys: remove the white core and surrounding fat. Soak in salted water for 1 hour, changing water twice. Slice. For heart: cut open, remove blood clots and valves. Soak in salted water for 30 minutes. Slice into strips. To sell fresh, pack in clean plastic bags or trays. Label with date. Keep on ice or in a fridge at 4Â°C. Sell within 2 days. For longer storage, freeze at -18Â°C for up to 3 months. You can also cook and sell as ready-to-eat: fry liver with onions and peppers; boil kidneys and heart for stew. In many African markets, liver is the most expensive offal, followed by kidney, then heart. Price according to local demand. A whole pig yields about 1.5 kg liver, 0.3 kg kidneys, and 0.5 kg heart. Clean and trim carefully to avoid waste. Use separate cutting boards for offal and other meat to prevent cross-contamination.

3. Making head cheese and brawn from the head

Head cheese (also called brawn) uses the pig's head, which is often wasted. It is a jellied meat loaf. You need: 1 pig head, 2 onions, 4 bay leaves, 1 tablespoon peppercorns, salt, vinegar, and water. First, clean the head thoroughly. Singe off any hair with a hot iron or over a flame. Scrub with salt and vinegar. Rinse. Cut the head into 4-6 pieces. Put in a large pot. Cover with water. Add onions, bay leaves, peppercorns, and 1 cup vinegar. Boil gently for 3-4 hours until meat falls off the bone. Remove bones, skin, and any gristle. Chop meat finely. Strain the broth and skim off fat. Measure broth: you need about 1 liter for every 2 kg chopped meat. Mix meat and broth. Add salt and pepper to taste. You can add chopped garlic or chili. Pack into clean molds or lined trays. Press down to remove air pockets. Refrigerate for 6-8 hours until set. Slice and serve cold, or fry slices. Head cheese keeps in fridge for 5 days, or freeze for 3 months. Sell in slices or blocks. In many areas, it is a popular snack or sandwich filling. Price per kg should be similar to cooked ham. Use the head of a healthy pig only. Clean all tools with hot soapy water.

4. Utilizing bones for broth and stock

Bones are not waste. They make rich broth and stock, which you can sell or use in your own cooking. After cutting meat, save all bones: legs, ribs, spine, and neck. For a good broth, roast bones first: spread on a baking tray, roast at 200Â°C for 30 minutes until browned. This adds flavor. Then put bones in a large pot. Cover with cold water. Add 2 tablespoons vinegar (helps draw out minerals). Add onions, carrots, celery, and herbs if available. Bring to a boil, then simmer for 4-12 hours. Skim off foam. The longer you simmer, the richer the stock. Strain through a cloth. Cool quickly. Remove fat from top (save for lard). You can reduce stock by boiling to concentrate. Pour into clean jars or plastic containers. Refrigerate for up to 5 days, or freeze for 6 months. Sell as fresh or frozen stock. In many cities, stock is sold in 1-liter bottles. You can also make bone meal: dry bones, crush into powder. Use as fertilizer or animal feed. A pig carcass yields about 10-15% bones by weight. So a 100 kg pig gives 10-15 kg bones. That is 20-30 liters of stock. Price stock at 50-70% of the price of meat per liter. Always label with date. Do not use bones from sick animals.

5. Processing fat into lard and cracklings

Pig fat is valuable. You can render it into lard (cooking fat) and cracklings (crispy bits). Collect fat from around kidneys, intestines, and under the skin. Cut into small pieces. Put in a heavy pot. Add 1/2 cup water per kg fat to prevent burning. Heat slowly. Stir often. Fat will melt and water will evaporate. After 1-2 hours, pieces turn golden brown and float. Strain through a fine sieve or cloth. The liquid is lard. The solid bits are cracklings. For clearer lard, strain again. Pour lard into clean jars. Cool. It keeps at room temperature for 3 months, or refrigerate for 1 year. Cracklings: sprinkle with salt, eat as snack, or sell in bags. You can also press cracklings to remove more fat. Lard is used for frying, baking, and soap making. Sell lard in 500g or 1kg containers. In many markets, lard sells for 70-80% of the price of cooking oil. Cracklings sell as a cheap snack. A 100 kg pig yields about 5-8 kg fat, which gives 4-6 kg lard and 1-2 kg cracklings. Use low heat to avoid burning. Never use fat from sick pigs. Store in a cool, dark place. Label with date. Clean all equipment with hot water and soap.

6. Selling hides for leather or local crafts

The pig hide (skin) can be sold for leather or crafts. After slaughter, remove the hide carefully. Use a sharp knife to separate skin from meat. Avoid cuts and holes. For fresh sale, wash the hide in cold water. Scrape off any meat and fat. Soak in salt water (1 kg salt per 10 liters water) for 24 hours. Drain and dry in shade. Or salt dry: rub salt on flesh side, stack, and leave for 3 days. Then dry. For leather, the hide must be tanned. Small-scale tanning: use vegetable tanning with bark (e.g., acacia, mangrove). Soak hide in water for 2 days. Then in tanning liquor (bark boiled in water, cooled) for 2-4 weeks. Stir daily. Remove, dry, and soften by rubbing with oil. For local crafts, you can sell dried hides to drum makers, bag makers, or shoe makers. In many African markets, a raw pig hide sells for 5-10% of the pig's live weight value. A 100 kg pig gives about 6-8 kg hide. Dried hide weight is about 30% of fresh. So 2 kg dried hide. Price per kg depends on quality. Avoid holes and cuts. Store in dry place. Use salt to prevent rotting. If you cannot tan, sell fresh or salted hide to a tannery. Always check local regulations on hide sales.
