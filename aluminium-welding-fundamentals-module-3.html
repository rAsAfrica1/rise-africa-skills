1. The Real Hazards

Aluminium welding is not the same as welding steel. The hazards are different, and if you treat aluminium like steel, you can get hurt. The biggest danger is the bright light. Aluminium reflects about 80% of the arc's ultraviolet (UV) rays. That means you get a double dose: direct UV and reflected UV bouncing off the shiny metal. Without proper protection, you can get 'arc eye' in minutesa painful burn of the cornea that feels like sand in your eyes. You also risk skin burns on your arms and neck from reflected UV.

Another real hazard is the fume. Aluminium welding produces fumes that contain aluminium oxide and, if you're using a filler alloy with magnesium or zinc, those metals too. Breathing these fumes can cause metal fume fevera flu-like illness with fever, chills, and body aches. Long-term exposure can lead to lung damage.

Heat is also a factor. Aluminium conducts heat away from the weld zone very quickly, so you often need higher amperage than for steel. That means the workpiece gets hot fast, and you can easily burn yourself on the hot metal. The arc itself is around 3,000Chot enough to ignite clothing.

Finally, there's the risk of electric shock. Aluminium welding often uses alternating current (AC) or high-frequency starting, which increases shock risk, especially in damp conditions. In many African workshops, floors are concrete, and sweat is commonboth make shock more likely.

To stay safe, you must use a proper welding helmet with a shade 10-13 lens (for aluminium, shade 11-13 is typical), wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and long sleeves. Never weld in shorts or open shoes. Work in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. Keep your area dry and check your cables for damage. Remember: the hazards are real, but they are controllable with the right gear and habits.

2. Fume and Metal Fume Fever

When you weld aluminium, the intense heat vaporises the metal and any coatings or oils on it. The vapour cools into tiny particles that float in the airthis is welding fume. If you can see a haze or smell a metallic odour, you are breathing fume. The main components of aluminium welding fume are aluminium oxide and, depending on the filler wire, magnesium, zinc, or copper.

Breathing these fumes can cause metal fume fever. Symptoms usually appear 4-12 hours after exposure: a metallic taste in your mouth, dry throat, cough, fever, chills, muscle aches, and sometimes nausea. It feels like a bad flu and usually passes in 24-48 hours. But repeated episodes can lead to permanent lung damage, and some metals like zinc and copper are more toxic than aluminium alone.

Prevention is straightforward: keep your head out of the fume. The best method is a fume extractor with a hood placed close to the weld. If you don't have one, weld outdoors or in a large open space with a cross-breeze. Never weld in a small, closed room. A simple fan blowing across your work area can help, but be careful not to blow shielding gas awayuse a fan that pulls air away from you, not across the weld.

If you must weld in a confined space, wear a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) with a P100 filter. A disposable N95 mask is not enough for welding fume. Also, clean the aluminium before welding: remove paint, oil, and grease with acetone or a stainless steel brush. That reduces fume at the source.

If you or a co-worker develops metal fume fever, move to fresh air, drink water, and rest. See a doctor if symptoms are severe or last more than two days. Never ignore repeated feversthey are a warning sign that your ventilation is inadequate.

3. Eye and Skin Protection

Your eyes and skin are your first line of defence in aluminium welding. The arc produces intense ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (IR) radiation that can burn your eyes and skin in seconds. For aluminium, you need a welding helmet with a shade 11 to 13 lens. Why? Because aluminium reflects up to 80% of UV rays, so you need a darker lens than for steel welding (where shade 10 is often enough). If you are welding at higher amperages (over 200 amps), use shade 13.

Auto-darkening helmets are convenient, but make sure they have a sensitivity adjustment and a grind mode. In dusty African workshops, keep the sensors clean. Always test your helmet before welding: strike an arc on a scrap piece and check that the lens darkens instantly. If it flickers or stays light, replace the battery or the helmet.

For skin, cover up. Wear a leather welding jacket or a heavy cotton long-sleeve shirt. Never weld with bare arms or a t-shirtUV will burn you like sunburn, but faster. Leather gloves are a must; choose gauntlet-style gloves that cover your wrists. Wear a leather apron to protect your torso and trousers from sparks and hot metal.

Don't forget your neck and ears. A welding beanie or a leather cap protects your head and ears from sparks and UV. Use earplugs if you are grinding or working in a noisy shop.

If you get arc eye (painful, watery eyes, feeling like sand), do not rub your eyes. Cover them with a cold, damp cloth and see a doctor. Rest in a dark room. Prevention is simple: always wear your helmet, and never look at the arc with naked eyes. Even a quick glance can cause damage.

Remember: in many African workshops, safety gear can be expensive, but a good helmet and gloves cost less than a hospital visit. Buy the best you can afford, and inspect it regularly for cracks or holes.

4. Gas Cylinder Safety

Aluminium welding often uses shielding gases like argon, helium, or a mix of both. These gases are stored in high-pressure cylinderstypically 200 bar (about 3,000 psi). If a cylinder is damaged or overheated, it can become a rocket, causing serious injury or death.

First, always secure cylinders upright with a chain or strap to a wall, trolley, or welding cart. Never leave a cylinder standing free. In African workshops, it's common to see cylinders rolling aroundthis is an accident waiting to happen.

Second, keep cylinders away from heat and sparks. The sun in many African countries can heat a cylinder to dangerous pressures. Store them in a shaded, ventilated area. Never weld near a cylinder; keep at least 3 metres (10 feet) between the cylinder and the welding arc.

Third, handle cylinders with care. Use a proper trolley to move them, not your shoulder. Never drop or drag a cylinder. Keep the valve protection cap on when moving or storing. When in use, open the valve slowly with a proper regulator. Check for leaks with soapy waternever with a flame.

Fourth, know your gas. Argon and helium are inert but can displace oxygen in a confined space, causing suffocation. If you are welding in a small room, ensure ventilation. Carbon dioxide (CO2) used in MIG welding can also displace oxygen.

Fifth, never use oil or grease on cylinder valves or regulators. Oil can react with high-pressure oxygen (if used) and cause an explosion.

Finally, if a cylinder leaks and you can't stop it, evacuate the area and call your gas supplier. Do not try to repair a valve yourself. Always store full and empty cylinders separately, and mark them clearly. In many African countries, gas suppliers offer trainingtake it. It's free and can save your life.

5. Electrical Safety

Aluminium welding uses electricityoften high currents (100-300 amps) and sometimes high-frequency starting. That combination can kill you if you are careless.

The main risk is electric shock. You become part of the circuit if you touch the electrode and the workpiece at the same time. Damp conditions, sweaty skin, and metal floors make it worse. In many African workshops, concrete floors are common, and during rainy season, humidity is high. Never weld in wet clothes or with wet gloves.

Before you start, inspect your equipment. Check the electrode holder, ground clamp, and cables for cracks, cuts, or exposed wires. Replace damaged cables immediatelydo not tape them. Make sure the ground clamp is firmly attached to clean metal. A poor ground can cause erratic arcs and increase shock risk.

Never weld on a wet floor. If you must, stand on a dry rubber mat or wooden board. Wear dry leather gloves and boots with rubber soles. Do not lean on the workpiece. Keep your body insulated from the metal you are welding.

Use a welding machine with a proper earth leakage circuit breaker (ELCB) or residual current device (RCD). In many African countries, these are available at hardware stores. If you don't have one, get oneit cuts power instantly if there is a fault.

When changing electrodes, turn off the machine or use a holder with a heat shield. Never change electrodes with bare hands.

If you get a shock, turn off the power immediately. Do not touch the person if they are still in contact with the live circuit. Use a dry wooden stick to separate them. Call for help.

Finally, keep your cables tidy. Coil them properly when not in use. Never run cables through water or over sharp edges. In a busy shop, cables can be a tripping hazardkeep them off the floor where possible.

6. Fire Prevention and Ventilation

Aluminium welding produces sparks and molten metal that can travel up to 10 metres (30 feet). In a typical African workshop, there may be cardboard, wood, paper, or oily rags nearby. A single spark can start a fire that spreads in seconds.

Before you weld, clear the area. Remove all flammable materials within a 10-metre radius. If you can't move them, cover them with a fire blanket or wet cloth. Never weld near petrol, paint, or solvents.

Have a fire extinguisher ready. A dry powder or CO2 extinguisher is best for electrical fires. Check the pressure gauge monthly. In many African markets, you can buy a 2 kg dry powder extinguisher for a reasonable price. Mount it on the wall near your welding area.

Never leave your welding area unattended while the arc is on. After you finish, stay for at least 30 minutes to watch for smouldering fires. Many workshop fires start after the welder has gone home.

Ventilation is equally important. Welding fume and shielding gas can build up in a closed space. Argon and helium are heavier than air and can pool in low areas, displacing oxygen. You need fresh air movement.

Open doors and windows. Use a fan to blow fume away from your breathing zone. If natural ventilation is not enough, use a fume extractor with a flexible arm. Place the hood 30-45 cm (12-18 inches) from the arc.

In small workshops, a simple setup works: a box fan in a window pulling air out, and another fan pushing air in from the opposite side. This creates a cross-breeze. But be careful not to blow the shielding gas awaykeep the fans directed away from the weld.

If you weld in a confined space, use forced ventilation and a respirator. Never rely on a dust mask alone.

Finally, train everyone in the shop on fire response. Know where the extinguisher is and how to use it. Practice the PASS method: Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep. A small fire can be put out in seconds if you act fast.

<h2>Practical Reference: Aluminium Welding Safety in African Workshops</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core safety knowledge from the module into a format you can print, laminate, and pin to your workshop wall. It is written for the reality of African aluminium fabrication businesses from small kiosks in Kumasi and Nairobi to medium-sized fabrication yards in Lagos, Johannesburg, and Cairo. The goal is simple: keep your welders alive, keep your workshop standing, and keep your business running. Every safety measure here also protects your bottom line. A fire, a hospital bill, or a lost welder costs far more than the time and money needed to prevent them.</p>

<p>Aluminium welding presents specific hazards that differ from steel welding. Aluminium conducts heat away from the weld zone rapidly, requiring higher heat input and often leading to more intense fume generation. The filler alloys and base metals may contain magnesium, zinc, copper, or silicon. Fluxes and cleaning chemicals introduce chlorides and fluorides. In many African workshops, ventilation is limited, welding is done in shared spaces, and gas cylinders are stored near walkways. This reference addresses those conditions directly.</p>

<h3>Why Aluminium Fume Deserves Special Attention</h3>

<p>Aluminium welding fume is a complex mixture. The base metal and filler wire vaporise in the arc, then condense into fine particles. These particles may include aluminium oxide, magnesium oxide, zinc oxide, copper oxide, and fluorides from flux. Particle sizes are typically below 1 micron, which means they penetrate deep into the lungs and are not easily cleared. In poorly ventilated African workshops often with open doors but no cross-breeze, or with fans that simply recirculate contaminated air exposure can reach levels many times the recommended limits within a single shift.</p>

<p>The health effects are cumulative. Short-term exposure can cause metal fume fever, a flu-like illness that appears hours after welding and resolves within a day or two. Long-term exposure is linked to bronchitis, reduced lung function, and in the case of certain alloying elements, more serious neurological or respiratory conditions. The business case for control is straightforward: a welder who is sick misses work, produces poor-quality welds, and eventually leaves. Replacing a skilled aluminium welder is expensive in every African market.</p>

<h3>Ventilation: The First Line of Defence</h3>

<p>In most African aluminium workshops, general dilution ventilation opening doors and windows is not enough. The fume is generated close to the welder's face, and even a moderate breeze can be blocked by the workpiece, a partition, or the welder's own body. Local exhaust ventilation (LEV) is the preferred control. A hood positioned 2030 cm from the arc, connected to a fan and filter, can capture 8095% of fume before it reaches the breathing zone. Where electricity is unreliable, a portable battery-powered fume extractor with a HEPA filter is a practical investment. Where no extractor is available, a simple cross-draft arrangement a fan behind the welder pushing air across the work and out through an opposite opening is better than nothing, provided the fan does not blow fume toward other workers.</p>

<p>Respiratory protection is the last line of defence, not the first. If ventilation is inadequate, a welder should wear at least a P2/P100 half-mask respirator, fit-tested and worn for the entire welding period. In hot African climates, comfort matters a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) with a lightweight helmet is more likely to be worn consistently than a tight half-mask. Beards reduce the seal of tight-fitting respirators; if your welders have beards, PAPR or loose-fitting powered respirators are the practical choice.</p>

<h3 style="margin-top:0;color:#922b21;"> RED SAFETY WARNING: Never Weld on Closed Aluminium Containers or Tanks</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;color:#7b241c;">Aluminium tanks, drums, cylinders, and vessels that have held fuel, oil, solvents, or unknown substances can explode when heated even if they appear empty and have been open for weeks. Residual vapours and reactive residues ignite or generate pressure inside the enclosed space. In 2019, a workshop fire in Nairobi's Industrial Area was traced to welding on a used aluminium fuel tank. Before welding any container: (1) confirm it has never held flammable material, or (2) have it professionally cleaned, purged, and gas-tested by a competent person. Never use water alone as a purge it does not remove all vapour. When in doubt, refuse the job. No contract is worth a life.</p>

<h3>Eye and Skin Protection</h3>

<p>Aluminium welding emits intense ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (IR) radiation. The arc is brighter and richer in UV than many steel welding arcs because of the higher currents and the reflective nature of aluminium. Without proper protection, the welder risks arc eye (photokeratitis), skin burns, and long-term skin cancer. A welding helmet with a shade of at least 1012 for MIG/TIG aluminium is essential. Auto-darkening helmets are convenient but must be rated for the process and kept clean and functional. Sunglasses are not a substitute. Gloves should be leather or heat-resistant, and sleeves should cover the arms fully. In hot climates, welders often roll up sleeves this is a direct route to UV burns and should be corrected through training and supervision.</p>

<h3>Gas Cylinder Safety</h3>

<p>Aluminium welding commonly uses argon or argon-helium mixtures for MIG and TIG. These cylinders are under high pressure (typically 200 bar / 2,900 psi). In African workshops, cylinders are often stored loose, chained to a wall, or left in the sun. Each of these is a hazard. Cylinders should be stored upright, secured with a chain or strap, away from heat sources, and with valve protection caps in place when not in use. Argon is an asphyxiant in a confined space, it displaces oxygen and can cause rapid unconsciousness. Never store cylinders in a pit, basement, or enclosed room without ventilation. When moving cylinders, use a trolley never roll or drag them. Check regulators and hoses for leaks with soapy water, never a flame.</p>

<h3>Electrical Safety</h3>

<p>Welding machines draw high currents and are often connected to unreliable or overloaded electrical systems. In many African workshops, extension leads are undersized, plugs are damaged, and earthing is poor or absent. This creates a serious risk of electric shock, especially in wet conditions. All welding equipment should be properly earthed. Cables should be inspected daily for cuts, exposed conductors, and damaged insulation. Welding should never be done in wet areas or while standing in water. If the workshop floor is damp, use a dry wooden platform or rubber mat. Residual current devices (RCDs) or earth leakage circuit breakers should be installed on the welding circuit they are relatively inexpensive and can save a life.</p>

<h3>Fire Prevention</h3>

<p>Aluminium welding produces sparks and hot spatter that can travel several metres. In workshops that store solvents, paints, thinners, or packaging materials, the fire risk is high. A clear zone of at least 10 metres around the welding area should be free of combustibles. A fire extinguisher dry powder or CO should be within reach and inspected monthly. In aluminium workshops, never use water on a metal fire involving aluminium powder or fines; use a Class D extinguisher or dry sand. Fire blankets are useful for smothering small fires and for protecting nearby equipment. Train every worker on the location and use of extinguishers, and practise a fire drill at least twice a year.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Ventilation first:</strong> Position LEV hood within 30 cm of the arc; if no LEV, use cross-draft and a P2/P100 respirator.</li>
<li><strong>Respirator fit:</strong> Fit-test tight-fitting masks; use PAPR if bearded or working long shifts in heat.</li>
<li><strong>Eye protection:</strong> Welding helmet with shade 1012 for aluminium MIG/TIG; safety glasses underneath for chipping and grinding.</li>
<li><strong>Skin coverage:</strong> Leather gloves, long sleeves, apron, and closed shoes no exposed skin in the arc's path.</li>
<li><strong>Cylinder discipline:</strong> Upright, chained, caps on, away from heat; never roll or drag; check for leaks with soapy water.</li>
<li><strong>Electrical checks:</strong> Earth the machine, inspect cables daily, use an RCD, keep the floor dry.</li>
<li><strong>Fire readiness:</strong> 10 m clear zone, extinguisher within reach, monthly inspection, annual drill.</li>
<li><strong>Container rule:</strong> Never weld on a closed or uncleaned aluminium tank, drum, or vessel purge and gas-test or refuse the job.</li>
</ul>
