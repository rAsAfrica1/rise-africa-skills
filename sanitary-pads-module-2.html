1. Disposable vs. Reusable Pads

Disposable pads are used once and thrown away. They are made of layers of plastic, cellulose, and super absorbent polymer (SAP). In Africa, a pack of 10 disposable pads costs between $0.50 and $2.00, depending on brand and country. A woman uses about 20 pads per cycle, so monthly cost can be $1 to $4. That is too expensive for many low-income households. Disposable pads are convenient and hygienic if you have clean water, soap, and a way to dispose of them safely. But in rural areas, disposal is a problempads end up in pit latrines or open dumps, causing environmental harm.

Reusable pads are made from cloth, usually cotton or flannel. They can be washed and used for 1 to 2 years. A set of 6 reusable pads costs $3 to $8. After the initial cost, you only spend on soap and water. Reusable pads are better for the environment and save money. But they require proper washing: rinse in cold water, wash with soap, dry in direct sunlight. If not dried properly, bacteria can grow and cause infections.

In Africa, many NGOs and social enterprises promote reusable pads because they are affordable and sustainable. For example, in Kenya, a group called ZanaAfrica makes reusable pads from local cotton. In Uganda, AFRIpads employs women to sew reusable pads. These pads create jobs and reduce waste.

When choosing between disposable and reusable, consider your lifestyle. If you have access to cheap disposables and proper disposal, they are easy. If you want to save money and help the environment, reusable pads are better. You can even make your own reusable pads from old cotton shirts or towels. But make sure the fabric is soft and absorbent. Always have a waterproof layer, like PUL (polyurethane laminate) fabric, to prevent leaks. In summary, disposable pads are convenient but costly and polluting; reusable pads are cheap, eco-friendly, but need washing. Choose based on your budget, water availability, and privacy.

2. Anatomy of a Disposable Pad

A disposable pad has five main layers. From top to bottom: top sheet, acquisition layer, absorbent core, back sheet, and adhesive. The top sheet touches your skin. It is made of non-woven polypropylene or cotton. It must be soft and allow liquid to pass through quickly. The acquisition layer, also called the distribution layer, is usually made of polyester or viscose. It spreads liquid evenly so the core can absorb it fast. Without this layer, liquid would pool in one spot and leak.

The absorbent core is the heart of the pad. It contains cellulose fluff pulp and super absorbent polymer (SAP). Cellulose comes from wood pulp, often from pine or eucalyptus trees. SAP is a powder that can absorb up to 300 times its weight in water. In a typical pad, SAP makes up 5 to 10 grams. The core is wrapped in tissue paper to keep the SAP from shifting.

The back sheet is a thin film of polyethylene (PE) or polypropylene (PP). It is waterproof and keeps liquid from leaking onto your underwear. Some back sheets are breathable, made of microporous film, to reduce sweating. The adhesive is a hot-melt glue that sticks the pad to your panty. It is usually applied in stripes or dots. Wings, if present, have their own adhesive to wrap around the panty.

To make a disposable pad at a small factory, you need a production line with rollers, a crusher for pulp, a SAP dispenser, and a cutter. A simple manual machine costs around $5,000 to $10,000. Raw materials cost about $0.05 to $0.10 per pad. In Africa, countries like Egypt, South Africa, and Nigeria have pad factories. But you can also import semi-finished pads and cut them locally. Understanding each layer helps you troubleshoot: if pads leak, check the SAP amount or back sheet quality. If they feel wet, improve the acquisition layer or top sheet.

3. Absorbent Materials: Cellulose, SAP, and Cotton

Cellulose is the most common absorbent material in disposable pads. It comes from wood pulp, which is beaten and fluffed into a soft, fluffy mass. Cellulose absorbs liquid by capillary actionliquid moves into tiny spaces between fibers. But cellulose alone can hold only about 5 to 10 times its weight in liquid. Under pressure, it releases liquid back, causing wetness. That is why SAP is added.

Super Absorbent Polymer (SAP) is a white powder, usually sodium polyacrylate. It can absorb 200 to 300 times its weight in distilled water, but only 20 to 50 times in urine because of salts. SAP locks liquid into a gel, so it does not leak even when you sit. In a pad, SAP is mixed with cellulose. A typical ultra-thin pad has 30% SAP and 70% cellulose. A thick pad may have less SAP and more cellulose. SAP is expensiveabout $2 to $3 per kilogram. So you must use it wisely.

Cotton is a natural fiber used in reusable pads and some disposable top sheets. Cotton is soft, breathable, and absorbs well. But it holds liquid and feels wet unless combined with a waterproof layer. In Africa, cotton is grown in countries like Burkina Faso, Mali, and Tanzania. You can buy cotton fabric locally. For reusable pads, use 100% cotton flannel or birdseye cotton. These are absorbent and wash well. Avoid synthetic fabrics like polyester because they do not absorb and can cause irritation.

To test absorbency, cut a 10 cm x 10 cm square of material, weigh it, soak it in water for 1 minute, then weigh again. The difference is the absorbency. Cellulose might absorb 8 times its weight, cotton 5 times, and SAP 200 times. But SAP is a powder, so you mix it with cellulose. A good pad core has 5 to 10 grams of SAP per pad. If you are making reusable pads, use 2 to 3 layers of cotton for the core. Always pre-wash cotton to remove oils that repel water. In summary, cellulose gives structure, SAP gives high absorbency, and cotton is best for reusable pads.

4. Top Sheet and Back Sheet Materials

The top sheet is the layer that touches your skin. It must be soft, non-irritating, and allow liquid to pass through quickly. Most disposable pads use non-woven polypropylene (PP) because it is cheap and wicks liquid away. Non-woven means the fibers are bonded together by heat, chemicals, or mechanical means, not woven like cloth. You can buy non-woven fabric in rolls from suppliers in China, India, or South Africa. A roll of 100 meters costs about $20 to $50. For reusable pads, the top sheet is usually cotton flannel or jersey. Cotton is breathable and gentle on sensitive skin. Some pads use a layer of silk or bamboo, but these are more expensive.

To test if a top sheet is good, put a drop of water on it. If the water spreads quickly and soaks in, it is good. If it beads up, it has a coating that repels liquidbad for pads. Also, rub the fabric against your inner arm. If it feels scratchy, it may cause chafing. In Africa, you can source non-woven PP from local textile shops or import from Alibaba. For reusable pads, buy cotton fabric from local markets. Always wash new fabric before use to remove sizing.

The back sheet is the waterproof layer. It prevents liquid from leaking onto your clothes. Disposable pads use polyethylene (PE) film, which is a thin plastic. PE is cheap and waterproof but not breathable, so it can cause sweating. Some premium pads use breathable PE film with tiny pores that let air through but not liquid. Breathable back sheets reduce rash and odor. For reusable pads, the back sheet is usually polyurethane laminate (PUL) fabric. PUL is a polyester fabric with a waterproof polyurethane coating. It is breathable and washable. A meter of PUL costs $5 to $10. You can also use nylon or waterproof mattress protector fabric, but PUL is best.

When making pads, ensure the back sheet is wider than the absorbent core to prevent leaks. The top sheet should be slightly wider than the core too, so it can wrap around. For disposable pads, the layers are glued together with hot-melt adhesive. For reusable pads, you sew the layers together. Always test your pad by pouring 10 ml of water on it. If the back stays dry, your back sheet works. If the top feels dry after 1 minute, your top sheet works.

5. Adhesives and Wings: Design and Function

Adhesives keep the pad in place. Disposable pads use hot-melt adhesive, which is a thermoplastic glue that is solid at room temperature but melts when heated. It is applied in stripes or dots on the back sheet. When you press the pad onto your panty, the adhesive sticks. The adhesive must be strong enough to hold the pad during movement but gentle enough to remove without tearing the panty. Common hot-melt adhesives are made from polymers like ethylene-vinyl acetate (EVA) or styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS). You can buy hot-melt glue sticks or pellets from suppliers. A 1 kg bag costs $5 to $10. For small-scale production, you can use a hot glue gun, but for larger scale, you need a glue applicator machine.

Wings are extensions on the sides of the pad that fold under the panty. They have adhesive on them to secure the pad. Wings prevent the pad from shifting and help stop side leaks. There are two types: straight wings and contoured wings. Straight wings are simple rectangles; contoured wings are curved to fit the panty better. Most pads have wings that are 4 to 6 cm long. The adhesive on wings is usually a repositionable adhesive, so you can adjust the pad. Some pads have wingless designs, but they tend to move more.

For reusable pads, you do not use adhesive. Instead, you use snaps, buttons, or Velcro. Snaps are plastic or metal fasteners that press together. They are durable and washable. You can buy snap pliers and snaps from craft stores. Velcro is easier but can collect lint and lose grip over time. Buttons are cheap but require buttonholes. In Africa, many reusable pad makers use snaps because they are secure and last long. A set of 100 plastic snaps costs about $5.

When designing wings, make sure they are long enough to wrap around the panty and overlap. A wing length of 6 cm works for most panties. The adhesive should cover at least 70% of the wing area. If you are making disposable pads, test the adhesive by sticking a pad to a cotton panty, then pulling it off. It should not leave residue or tear the fabric. For reusable pads, test the snaps by washing and drying the pad 20 times. They should still snap securely. In summary, adhesives and wings are critical for comfort and leak protection. Choose the right adhesive for disposables and the right fasteners for reusables.

6. Biodegradable and Eco-Friendly Options

Disposable pads take 500 to 800 years to decompose because of plastic back sheets and SAP. In Africa, where waste collection is often poor, used pads clog pit latrines and pollute waterways. Biodegradable pads aim to solve this. They use materials that break down naturally. For example, the back sheet can be made from cornstarch-based bioplastic (PLA) or paper. The top sheet can be cotton or bamboo. The absorbent core can be cellulose from bamboo or bagasse (sugar cane waste). SAP can be replaced with natural super absorbents like starch-based polymers or cellulose nanofibers. These are less absorbent than synthetic SAP but decompose in 6 to 12 months.

In Africa, several companies make biodegradable pads. In Kenya, EcoBags makes pads from banana fiber. In South Africa, Lil-Lets has a biodegradable range. In Rwanda, Sustainable Health Enterprises (SHE) makes pads from banana tree fiber. These pads cost moreabout $0.20 to $0.30 per padbut they are eco-friendly. You can also make your own biodegradable pads using local materials. For example, use dried banana leaves or sisal fibers for the core, wrapped in cotton. But these may not be as absorbent as commercial pads.

Reusable pads are the most eco-friendly option because they produce zero waste per use. After 2 years, a reusable pad can be composted if made from 100% cotton and natural materials. But if it has PUL or snaps, remove them before composting. In Africa, composting is common in rural areas. You can bury cotton pads in the garden; they decompose in 3 to 6 months.

To start a biodegradable pad business, source local materials. Use bamboo pulp from Asia or Africa. Bamboo grows fast and is sustainable. Use cornstarch bioplastic from suppliers in China or South Africa. For SAP, try starch-based SAP from companies like BASF or local innovators. Test your pad for absorbency and biodegradability. Bury a pad in soil and check after 3 months. If it has broken down, it is good. Also, educate customers: biodegradable pads must be composted, not thrown in regular trash, to decompose properly. In summary, biodegradable pads reduce environmental harm but cost more. Reusable pads are the greenest choice. Choose based on your budget and local waste systems.

<h2>Practical Reference: Types and Materials of Sanitary Pads</h2>

<p>This practical reference is designed for African agribusiness entrepreneurs, women's groups, and small-scale manufacturers who want to produce or distribute sanitary pads. It covers the core materials, their functions, sourcing considerations, and practical decision points that affect cost, quality, and market positioning. Whether you are setting up a micro-factory in Kenya, a cooperative in Nigeria, or a distribution network in South Africa, understanding pad anatomy and material choices is essential for profitability and customer satisfaction.</p>

<h3>Disposable vs. Reusable Pads: Business Decision Framework</h3>

<p>Disposable pads dominate urban markets due to convenience, but reusable pads are gaining traction in rural and eco-conscious segments. For a business, the choice depends on your target market, distribution capacity, and production infrastructure. Disposable pads require sophisticated machinery (often imported) and a steady supply of raw materials like superabsorbent polymer (SAP) and nonwoven fabrics. Reusable pads demand sewing capacity, cotton or bamboo fabric, and a business model that emphasizes durability and washing education.</p>

<h3>Anatomy of a Disposable Pad</h3>

<p>A disposable pad is a multi-layer engineered product. Each layer has a specific function:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Top sheet:</strong> The skin-facing layer. Typically made from perforated nonwoven polypropylene or polyethylene. It must be soft, hydrophobic (to keep moisture away from skin), and allow rapid fluid transfer.</li>
<li><strong>Acquisition/distribution layer (ADL):</strong> Often a spunbond or through-air-bonded nonwoven that quickly pulls fluid away from the top sheet and distributes it across the absorbent core.</li>
<li><strong>Absorbent core:</strong> The heart of the pad. Usually a blend of cellulose fluff pulp and superabsorbent polymer (SAP). Cellulose provides structure and wicking; SAP absorbs and locks away liquid under pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Back sheet:</strong> A liquid-impermeable layer, typically polyethylene film or a breathable nonwoven laminate. It prevents leakage and is often printed with adhesive strips.</li>
<li><strong>Adhesives and wings:</strong> Hot-melt adhesives attach the pad to underwear. Wings (side flaps) wrap around the panty crotch for security and are coated with adhesive on the underside.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Absorbent Materials: Cellulose, SAP, and Cotton</h3>

<p><strong>Cellulose fluff pulp</strong> is derived from wood pulp, often imported into Africa from Scandinavia, Brazil, or Canada. It is inexpensive, biodegradable, and provides excellent wicking. However, it has limited absorbency under pressure. <strong>Superabsorbent polymer (SAP)</strong> is a synthetic polyacrylate that can absorb 200300 times its weight in water. It is the key to leak protection but is not biodegradable and is typically imported. <strong>Cotton</strong> is used in reusable pads and some premium disposable pads. It is breathable, soft, and biodegradable but more expensive and slower to absorb than SAP-enhanced cores.</p>

<h3>Top Sheet and Back Sheet Materials</h3>

<p>The top sheet is critical for user comfort. Most disposable pads use <strong>perforated polypropylene nonwoven</strong> because it is cheap, soft, and allows fluid to pass through quickly while keeping the surface dry. Some premium brands use <strong>cotton-covered top sheets</strong> for sensitive skin. The back sheet is usually <strong>polyethylene film</strong> (impermeable) or a <strong>breathable nonwoven laminate</strong> (allows air circulation, reducing odor and irritation). For reusable pads, the top sheet is often <strong>cotton jersey</strong> or <strong>bamboo fleece</strong>, and the back sheet is <strong>polyurethane laminate (PUL)</strong> or <strong>fleece</strong> for waterproofing.</p>

<h3>Adhesives and Wings: Design and Function</h3>

<p>Adhesives are often overlooked but are a common cause of product failure. Hot-melt adhesives must bond to the back sheet and to underwear without leaving residue. In hot African climates, adhesives can soften and migrate, causing the pad to shift. Wings (side flaps) provide extra security by wrapping around the panty. They require a stronger adhesive or mechanical fastening (for reusables). For disposable pads, wing adhesive is typically a pressure-sensitive hot-melt. For reusable pads, wings may use snaps or Velcro. Businesses should test adhesive performance in local conditionshigh heat and humidity can degrade bonding.</p>

<strong style="color: #b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0; color: #b71c1c;">Superabsorbent polymer (SAP) dust can cause respiratory irritation and eye damage. If you are manufacturing pads, ensure workers wear N95 masks, safety goggles, and gloves when handling SAP powder. SAP should never be inhaled or ingested. Additionally, adhesives and nonwoven fabrics may release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) during hot-melt applicationensure proper ventilation in production areas. Always comply with local occupational health and safety regulations.</p>

<h3>Biodegradable and Eco-Friendly Options</h3>

<p>Africa faces a growing waste management crisis, and disposable pads contribute to plastic pollution. Biodegradable options include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bamboo fiber cores:</strong> Biodegradable, soft, and increasingly available in East Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Polylactic acid (PLA) back sheets:</strong> Compostable bioplastic derived from corn starch. Still expensive and not widely available in Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Reusable pads:</strong> The most sustainable option. A single reusable pad can replace hundreds of disposables, saving money and reducing waste.</li>
<li><strong>Unbleached cellulose:</strong> Reduces chemical use but may have lower absorbency.</li>
</ul>
<p>For businesses, eco-friendly pads can command a premium in urban, health-conscious markets. However, supply chains for biodegradable materials in Africa are still nascent. Reusable pads offer the best combination of local production potential, low capital investment, and environmental benefits.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Identify your target market: urban disposable users vs. rural reusable users.</li>
<li> Source cellulose and SAP from reliable importers; check lead times and customs duties.</li>
<li> Test adhesive performance in high heat and humidity conditions.</li>
<li> Ensure top sheet is soft and hydrophobic; test with synthetic menstrual fluid.</li>
<li> For reusable pads, pre-shrink cotton/bamboo fabric and use PUL for waterproofing.</li>
<li> Comply with safety standards for SAP handling and factory ventilation.</li>
<li> Explore biodegradable options if targeting eco-conscious consumers.</li>
<li> Calculate unit economics: material cost, labor, packaging, and distribution.</li>
</ul>
