1. What is Charcoal?

Charcoal is a black, porous material made by heating wood or other organic matter in the absence of oxygen. This process, called pyrolysis, drives off water and volatile compounds, leaving behind almost pure carbon. In Africa, traditional charcoal is made by slowly burning wood under a mound of earth or in a metal kiln. For example, a common method in Ghana uses a earth mound that takes 3-5 days to carbonize 1 tonne of wood, yielding about 250 kg of charcoal. But for drawing, you need a different kind: artist charcoal, which is made from willow, vine, or other soft woods, heated in a sealed retort at around 400Â°C for several hours. The result is a lightweight, brittle stick that leaves a rich black mark on paper.

Charcoal's structure is what makes it special. It is soft and crumbly, so it breaks easily and deposits a lot of pigment with little pressure. That means you can cover a large area quickly, which is why it's great for sketching and shading. It also comes in different hardnesses: soft charcoal is very black and smudgy, while hard charcoal is compressed and holds a sharper point. You'll also find charcoal pencils, which are charcoal powder mixed with a binder and encased in wood, and charcoal powder for dusting.

In Africa, charcoal is more than an art supply; it's a daily fuel for cooking. But for artists, it's a versatile medium that has been used for centuries. From the cave paintings of the Sahara to contemporary studios in Nairobi and Lagos, charcoal has left its mark. As you start this course, remember that charcoal is forgiving: you can erase it with a kneaded eraser or a chamois cloth, and you can blend it with your fingers or a tortillon. But it's also messy, so prepare your workspace.

By the end of this lesson, you should understand that charcoal is not just burnt wood; it's a carefully produced drawing tool with specific properties. Next time you pick up a stick, think about the process that made it and how that affects your mark-making. Practice making lines and smudges on a scrap paper to feel its texture.

2. History of Charcoal in Art

Charcoal has been used for drawing for thousands of years. Some of the earliest known charcoal drawings are on cave walls in Europe and Africa. In Africa, the Apollo 11 Cave in Namibia has charcoal drawings dating back 26,000 years, depicting animals and human figures. These ancient artists used charred sticks from their fires, much like you might use a burnt branch today. But charcoal as a fine art medium really took off during the Renaissance in Europe, when artists like Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo used it for preparatory sketches. They valued it for its speed and expressiveness.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, charcoal became a staple in art schools worldwide, including in Africa. During the colonial period, art education in places like Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa often followed European models, and charcoal was part of the curriculum. However, African artists soon adapted it to their own themes. For example, in the 1960s, the Zaria Art Society in Nigeria, led by Bruce Onobrakpeya and Demas Nwoko, used charcoal to depict local landscapes and traditions. They showed that charcoal could capture the textures of African lifeâmud huts, market scenes, and savannah grasses.

Today, charcoal is used by contemporary African artists like William Kentridge (South Africa) and El Anatsui (Ghana), though Anatsui is known for sculpture. Kentridge uses charcoal for his animated films and large drawings, often smudging and erasing to create movement. In Kenya, artists at the Kuona Trust in Nairobi regularly use charcoal for life drawing sessions. The medium's affordability and immediacy make it ideal for rapid sketches and expressive works.

For you as a learner, knowing this history connects you to a long lineage. When you draw with charcoal, you join artists from ancient cave painters to modern masters. It also reminds you that charcoal is not just for rough sketches; it can be a finished medium. Try to see charcoal works in galleries or online. Notice how artists use light and shadow, and how the blackness of charcoal can convey deep emotion. Your own practice is part of this ongoing story.

3. Charcoal vs. Other Drawing Media

When you draw, you choose a medium based on the effect you want. Charcoal is different from graphite pencil, ink, and pastel. Let's compare them practically. Graphite pencil, the common HB or 2B, is made of graphite and clay. It gives a precise, gray line that is hard to smudge. It's great for detailed work, but it can't produce the deep, velvety blacks of charcoal. Charcoal, on the other hand, is pure carbon and produces a matte black that is much darker. A single stroke of soft charcoal can be as dark as many layers of graphite. However, graphite erases cleanly, while charcoal leaves a stain even after erasing.

Ink, whether from a pen or brush, is liquid and permanent. It gives sharp, high-contrast lines and is excellent for calligraphy and comics. But ink cannot be blended or smudged easily, and it doesn't offer the range of tones that charcoal does. Pastel, which is pigment mixed with a binder, is similar to charcoal in that it's soft and blendable, but it comes in colors. Charcoal is monochromeâblack, white, and graysâwhich forces you to focus on value and form. That's why many art teachers start students on charcoal before moving to color.

In terms of tools, charcoal is cheaper than most media. In many African markets, you can buy a box of 12 charcoal sticks for the price of a single graphite pencil. You also need less equipment: a kneaded eraser, a blending stump, and paper with some tooth (texture). Graphite requires sharpeners and erasers that don't smudge. Ink requires pens and brushes that need cleaning. Charcoal is low-maintenance.

But charcoal has downsides. It's messy and can be easily smudged, so you need to work from top to bottom and use a fixative at the end. It also breaks easily, so store it in a rigid box. Graphite is more portable and less messy. So, when should you use charcoal? For quick studies, large-scale drawings, dramatic lighting, and expressive sketches. Use graphite for detailed botanical illustrations or architectural plans. Use ink for bold outlines. As you progress, you'll likely combine themâcharcoal for shadows, graphite for details. Experiment and see what feels right.

4. Basic Charcoal Safety and Handling

Charcoal is generally safe, but you must handle it with care. The main risks are dust and skin irritation. Charcoal dust is fine and can be inhaled, which over time may irritate your lungs. In a small studio in Accra or Lagos, where ventilation might be poor, this is a real concern. Always work in a well-ventilated area. Open a window or work outdoors if possible. If you're using charcoal powder, wear a dust mask (a simple N95 or even a cloth mask helps). Avoid blowing on your drawing to remove dust; instead, tap it off gently or use a drafting brush.

Charcoal can also dry out your skin. The dust absorbs natural oils, leading to cracked fingertips. If you have sensitive skin, wear thin cotton gloves or apply a barrier cream before drawing. Wash your hands with soap and water after each session. Don't eat or drink while working, as you might ingest charcoal dust. Also, be careful with fixative sprays. These are often aerosol cans containing solvents. Use them outdoors or in a spray booth, and hold your breath while spraying. A cheaper alternative is hairspray, but it's not archival and can yellow over time. For student work, it's fine.

Storage is important. Charcoal sticks are brittle. Keep them in a rigid box with foam or cloth padding. If you make your own charcoal, ensure it's fully cooled before handling. Never use charcoal made from treated wood or painted wood, as it may contain toxic chemicals. Buy artist-grade charcoal from art supply stores; in Africa, look for brands like Derwent, Faber-Castell, or local suppliers like Kenafric in Kenya. If you can't find them, you can make willow charcoal by burning willow twigs in a tin can with a lid. But do this outdoors and let it cool completely.

Finally, protect your clothing and workspace. Charcoal stains fabric. Wear an apron or old clothes. Cover your table with newspaper or a plastic sheet. Keep a damp cloth nearby to wipe your hands. If you get charcoal on your clothes, brush it off before washing; rubbing it in makes it worse. With these simple precautions, you can enjoy charcoal safely for years.

5. Overview of Charcoal Types

Not all charcoal is the same. For drawing, you'll encounter several types, each with different properties. The main categories are willow charcoal, vine charcoal, compressed charcoal, charcoal pencils, and charcoal powder. Let's go through them. Willow charcoal is made from willow branches. It's soft, light, and produces a gray-black line that erases easily. It's perfect for laying in initial sketches because you can adjust it without leaving heavy marks. Vine charcoal is similar but made from grapevines; it's slightly harder and darker. Both are fragile and come in thin sticks. In Africa, you might find locally made charcoal from acacia or eucalyptus, but these are often harder and more suitable for cooking than drawing. For art, stick to imported or specialized brands.

Compressed charcoal is made from charcoal powder mixed with a binder like gum arabic and pressed into sticks. It's much harder and darker than willow or vine. It comes in different grades: soft, medium, and hard. Soft compressed charcoal is very black and smudgy; hard is less messy and holds a point. You can sharpen it with sandpaper. This is the type most artists use for bold, dark areas. Charcoal pencils are compressed charcoal encased in wood, like a regular pencil. They come in grades from 2B to 6B, with higher numbers being softer and darker. They're great for details and controlled lines. You can sharpen them with a knife or sharpener, but be carefulâthe lead is brittle.

Charcoal powder is pure charcoal ground into a fine dust. You apply it with a brush, a sponge, or your finger. It's excellent for covering large areas quickly, like backgrounds. You can also use it to tone your paper before drawing. A little goes a long way. Finally, there's white charcoal, which is actually not charcoal but a white pigment (often calcium carbonate) mixed with a binder. It's used for highlights on toned paper. It doesn't erase like black charcoal.

When starting out, buy a mixed set: a few willow sticks, a couple of compressed sticks (soft and hard), one charcoal pencil, and a small jar of powder. In Africa, you can find these at art supply shops in major cities or online. Expect to pay around 500-1000 KES for a basic set in Nairobi, or 2000-5000 NGN in Lagos. As you practice, you'll discover which types suit your style. Keep them organized and labeled.

6. Setting Up Your Charcoal Workspace

A good workspace makes charcoal drawing easier and safer. You don't need a fancy studio; a corner of a room or a shaded outdoor area works. But you must set it up right. First, choose a surface. A drawing board or a sturdy table is ideal. Cover it with newspaper or a plastic sheet to catch dust. If you're working outdoors, be mindful of windâit will blow your charcoal dust and ruin your work. Work in a shaded area, like under a tree or a veranda, to avoid harsh sunlight that can make it hard to see values.

Next, gather your tools. You'll need: charcoal sticks (willow, compressed), charcoal pencils, a kneaded eraser, a hard eraser (like a vinyl eraser), a blending stump or tortillon, sandpaper for sharpening, a drafting brush or soft paintbrush for dusting, and a fixative (spray can or hairspray). Also, have a sketchbook or loose paper with a textured surface. Smooth paper doesn't hold charcoal well; it smudges too easily. Look for paper labeled 'charcoal paper' or 'ingres paper' with a weight of at least 120gsm. In Africa, you can buy locally made paper from places like the University of Nigeria's art department, or import Canson or Strathmore pads.

Lighting is critical. You need consistent, diffused light to judge values. If indoors, use a desk lamp with a daylight bulb (5000-6500K) placed to your side, not directly in front, to avoid shadows on your work. If outdoors, work in open shade. Avoid direct sun because it creates glare and heat, which can make you sweat and smudge your drawing. Also, set up a waste bin for charcoal dust and used sandpaper. Keep a damp cloth for wiping hands.

Organize your space so everything is within reach. Put your charcoal sticks in a shallow tray, sorted by type. Keep your erasers and stumps in a cup. Have a small container for charcoal powder if you use it. Finally, protect your clothes: wear an apron. After each session, clean your workspace with a damp cloth to prevent dust buildup. With a well-set-up workspace, you can focus on drawing. Practice setting up and taking down your space a few times until it becomes a habit.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Carbon Monoxide & Combustion Risk

Charcoal â whether artist's stick or cooking fuel â produces carbon monoxide (CO), an odourless, invisible gas, when burned or when dust is inhaled in confined spaces. In African urban and rural settings, CO poisoning from indoor charcoal use kills thousands annually. For art studios and briquette workshops: never work with burning charcoal indoors, always ventilate, store charcoal away from moisture and ignition sources, and wear a dust mask (N95 or equivalent) when handling charcoal powder or briquette dust. Pregnant workers and those with respiratory conditions should avoid prolonged exposure to charcoal dust.

Quick Reference Checklist â Setting Up a Charcoal Workspace or Micro-Enterprise

  1. Ventilation first: Install cross-flow ventilation; never work with combustion charcoal indoors.
  2. Source locally where possible: Vine and compressed charcoal can be made from local agricultural waste; import only what cannot be produced.
  3. Separate art and fuel stock: Artist-grade charcoal needs clean, dust-controlled storage; fuel charcoal needs dry, ventilated bulk storage.
  4. Budget for PPE: N95 masks, gloves, and eye protection are non-negotiable recurring costs.
  5. Understand your buyer: Art supply shops pay for consistency and packaging; fuel buyers pay for heat value and low smoke.

  7. Track your margins weekly: Fuel charcoal margins are thin; art charcoal margins reward branding.
  8. Build a supplier network: Reliable raw-material supply (wood, agri-waste, binder) is the single biggest constraint on scaling.
