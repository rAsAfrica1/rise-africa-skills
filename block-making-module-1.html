Dense Concrete Block (Solid)

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Easy difficulty

1 What is a dense concrete block?

A dense concrete block is a solid rectangular masonry unit made from cement, sand, and crushed stone, pressed into a mould and cured for 7 to 28 days. It is the workhorse of construction worldwide because it is cheap to make, strong, and works in every climate.

The UK standard size is 440 × 215 × 100 mm. In Africa, the most common sizes are 4.5-inch (115 mm), 6-inch (150 mm) and 9-inch (225 mm) widths. All are dense — meaning the mix has no air pockets and the block feels heavy for its size.

You can start a dense block business with just a hand mould and a tamper. Within a few months, most operators move to a small machine that makes 300–500 blocks a day.

💡 The dense block is the first product you should make — everything else (hollow, pavers, kerbs) builds on the same base mix and equipment.

2 Choosing your cement grade

Not all cement is the same. For block making you want general-purpose cement — either:

  • CEM I 52.5N — pure Portland, highest strength, more expensive
  • CEM II 42.5N — Portland with a small addition, cheaper, very common

For most block yards, CEM II 42.5N is the sweet spot. It gives plenty of strength for wall and foundation blocks without paying for premium cement you don't need.

Check the bag: it should have a manufacture date within the last 6 weeks, be dry (not clumped), and come from a supplier you trust. Damp or stale cement makes weak blocks — no mix ratio can fix that.

3 Sand and aggregate ratios

A good dense block mix by weight is:

  • Cement: 1 part
  • Sharp sand (0–4 mm): 3 parts
  • Crushed stone (4–6 mm): 5 parts
  • Water: just enough to make it "earth damp"

That works out to roughly 10–12% cement by weight. A 50 kg bag of cement makes about 20–24 dense blocks of 440 × 215 × 100 mm.

Stone size matters. Never use stone bigger than one-third of the thinnest part of the block wall. For a hollow block with 30 mm walls, that means 10 mm maximum stone. For solid blocks, 4–6 mm is ideal.

⚠ The squeeze test is your friend. When you squeeze a handful of mixed material, it should hold shape and leave your palm damp — not dripping, not crumbling.

4 Mixing by hand vs machine

By hand (up to 100 blocks/day): Spread sand and stone on a clean concrete floor, pour cement on top, and turn the pile three times with shovels — pulling from the bottom to the top. Add water slowly. Takes 20–30 minutes for a 200 kg batch.

By pan mixer (500+ blocks/day): A 350-litre pan mixer handles a batch in 3–4 minutes. Load dry materials first, run 1 minute, then add water slowly while mixing for 2–3 more minutes. The result is more consistent and you save 5–8% cement because the mix is uniform.

A small pan mixer costs $2,000–$4,000 and pays for itself in a few months at 500+ blocks/day. For under 200/day, hand mixing is fine and you save the capital.

5 Filling and compacting the mould

How you fill the mould determines whether your blocks pass or fail a strength test.

  1. Fill in two layers, not one dump. Each layer about half the mould height.
  2. Compact each layer with a tamper (hand mould) or vibration (machine). 5–8 seconds is usually enough — less for dry mixes, more for wet.
  3. Level the top with a straight edge. Cut off excess mix, don't push it down.
  4. Check corners are full. Honeycomb edges are the #1 defect and they happen when the mix is too dry or vibration is too short.

For machine production: fill the hopper, lower the mould onto the pallet, vibrate 3–5 seconds while pressing, then lift slowly. The block should keep its shape and edges should be sharp.

6 Demoulding the green block

The "green" block — the freshly demoulded one — is fragile. Handle it with care.

Hand moulds: Lift the mould straight up. The block stays on the ground or pallet. Do not slide or tilt the mould.

Machine: Lower the pallet onto a flat surface, then lift the mould up slowly. If the block slumps or collapses, your mix is too wet. Add more sand and try again.

Green blocks need 24 hours untouched. Move them only after that. If you're on the floor (egg-laying machine), leave them for 24 hours before lifting onto curing pallets.

💡 Buy 500+ production pallets if you're doing machine work. Blocks stay on pallets from the moment of demoulding until they're sold.

7 Water curing method (7–28 days)

Concrete gains strength through a chemical reaction between cement and water. If the block dries out too fast, that reaction stops and the block stays weak. Curing is the difference between a 5 N/mm² block and a 10 N/mm² block.

Two methods work well:

  • Spray curing: Spray blocks with water 2–3 times a day for the first 7 days. Keep them damp but not soaked.
  • Plastic sheet curing: Cover the pallet of blocks with a plastic sheet. The moisture inside stays put. Cheaper on labour.

At 7 days, blocks are usually 60–70% of final strength. At 28 days, they hit full strength. Do not sell blocks before 7 days minimum, and never before their batch passes a strength test.

8 Testing the finished block

You cannot tell a good block from a bad one by looking. You need to test.

Everyday checks (5 blocks per batch):

  • Measure length, width, height — should be within ±2 mm
  • Weigh a block — dense 100 mm block should be about 19–20 kg
  • Drop from waist height onto a hard surface — a good block chips, a bad one shatters

Strength test (per 5,000 blocks or every 2 weeks): Send 3 blocks to an accredited lab. They crush them and report N/mm². For standard wall blocks, aim for 7.3 N/mm² at 28 days. Foundations need 10 N/mm² or more.

✅ Record every batch: date, mix, operator, size test result. If a customer complains, you can trace back to the batch and fix the problem.
✅ Module 1 checkpoint: You now know what a dense block is, the correct mix ratios, how to fill and compact the mould, and how to cure and test. Move on to Module 2 when your first 10 test blocks pass a simple drop test.
