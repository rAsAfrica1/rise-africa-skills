1. Understanding Forge Types: Coal, Gas, and Propane

A forge is simply a controlled fire that gets hot enough to make metal soft. In Africa, the most common forge is a coal forge, often made from a car wheel rim, a metal bucket, or a simple clay mound. Coal forges burn charcoal or mineral coal. Charcoal is made by burning wood slowly with little air; it lights easily and is cheap, but burns fast. Mineral coal burns hotter and longer but needs a good air blast from a hand-cranked blower or electric fan. A coal forge needs a firepot (a shallow bowl) and a tuyere (a pipe that brings air into the fire). You can build one from scrap: a brake drum works well as a firepot, and a length of pipe with a hair dryer or hand blower provides air.

Gas forges run on propane or butane. They are cleaner and easier to light, but the fuel must be bought. A basic gas forge is a metal drum or pipe lined with ceramic fibre or firebrick to hold heat. A burner (often a simple pipe with a small hole for air) mixes gas and air, and the flame swirls inside the chamber. Gas forges heat metal without smoke or ash, so they are good in towns where coal smoke is a problem. However, they can be more expensive to run and require a steady supply of gas cylinders.

Propane is the same as cooking gas in many countries. A 9 kg cylinder will run a small forge for about 8 to 10 hours. You need a regulator and a hose rated for gas. Never use a homemade regulator or a leaking hose. Gas forges must have a way for exhaust gases to escape, or you risk carbon monoxide poisoning. Always work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed.

Choose your forge based on what you have. If you have scrap metal and wood to make charcoal, build a coal forge. If you live in a city with gas supply, a gas forge may be easier. Many smiths start with coal and later add a gas forge for smaller jobs. The key is to learn to control the fire, no matter what fuel you use.

2. Building and Maintaining a Coal Fire

To build a coal fire, start with a clean firepot. Remove old clinker (the glassy waste that blocks air) and ash. Place a handful of dry kindling or paper in the bottom, then add small pieces of charcoal or coal. Light the kindling from the top, and turn on the air blast gently. As the fire catches, add more coal around the edges, leaving a hollow in the centre. This hollow is where you will put your metal. The fire should be bright orange-yellow, not dull red.

Once the fire is going, you need to keep it fed. Add coal little by little around the outside, not directly on the hot centre. Green coal (new coal) smokes and gives off yellow smoke, which is normal. As it heats, it turns to coke and burns clean. Use a poker to break up clinker and keep air passages open. The fire should be about the size of a melon for most work. If you need a bigger fire, add more coal and increase air. If the fire gets too big, stop adding coal and let it burn down.

Maintaining the fire means watching the colour. A good forging fire is bright yellow-orange. If it is dark red, you need more air. If it is white and sparking, it is too hot and you are burning the metal. The metal should be placed in the fire at the same depth each time, usually about 2-3 cm below the surface of the coal. Rotate the metal so it heats evenly. Never leave metal in the fire while you do other tasks; it can burn.

In Africa, many smiths use charcoal instead of coal. Charcoal burns faster, so you need to add it more often. A charcoal fire is ready when the coals are glowing red with a little ash on top. You can use a hand-powered bellows made from a plastic bag or a wooden box to provide air. Keep the bellows or blower working steadily; too much air blows ash into the metal, too little makes a cold fire. After work, let the fire die down, then clean out the firepot and dispose of ash safely.

3. Controlling Fire Temperature and Size

Controlling the fire is the most important skill in blacksmithing. Temperature is controlled by the amount of air you blow into the fire and the amount of fuel. More air means more oxygen, which makes the fire hotter. But too much air can burn the metal or blow sparks. For most forging, you want a bright orange heat, around 1,100 to 1,200 degrees Celsius. That is hot enough to make steel soft like clay. If you need to weld, you need a white heat, around 1,300 degrees Celsius. You can judge temperature by colour: dark red is about 600C, bright red 900C, orange 1,100C, yellow 1,200C, white 1,300C.

To increase temperature, add more air by turning up the blower or pumping the bellows faster. You can also add more coal around the centre. To decrease temperature, reduce air or spread the coal out. The size of the fire also matters. A small fire concentrates heat, good for small parts like hooks. A large fire spreads heat, good for long pieces like tongs. You can shape the fire by piling coal into a mound for a small hot spot, or spreading it flat for a wide heat.

Use a damper or a valve on your air supply to make fine adjustments. If you have an electric blower, a simple switch or a speed control works. With a hand-cranked blower, you control speed by how fast you turn. Always keep a bucket of water nearby to cool the fire if it gets too hot. You can also sprinkle water on the coal around the edge to keep it from burning too fast.

Practice by heating a piece of scrap steel. Watch the colour change as you increase air. Learn to hold a steady heat. In Africa, where fuel may be scarce, controlling the fire also saves money. A well-controlled fire uses less coal or charcoal. Remember, a forge fire is like a living thing: it needs food (fuel), air, and attention. With practice, you will know exactly how much air and fuel to use for each job.

4. Using a Gas Forge: Setup and Adjustment

A gas forge is simpler to light than a coal forge, but you must set it up safely. First, place the forge on a stable, fireproof surface like concrete or bare earth. Never put it on wood or near flammable materials. Connect the gas hose to the burner and the regulator to the gas cylinder. Use soapy water to check for leaks: if bubbles form, tighten the connection. Open the cylinder valve slowly. Then open the burner valve a little and light the burner with a long match or spark lighter. Never lean over the forge when lighting; keep your face and hands to the side.

Once lit, adjust the air intake on the burner. Most burners have a sliding sleeve that controls air. If the flame is yellow and lazy, it needs more air. If it is blue and roaring, it is good. A proper flame is blue with a slight roar. If the flame blows out, turn off the gas, wait a minute, and try again with less gas. The forge chamber should heat up to orange in about 5 minutes. You can adjust the gas flow with the regulator or a needle valve. More gas means more heat, but also more fuel use.

To control temperature, you can adjust the gas and air. For most forging, you want the inside of the forge to be bright orange. If it is too hot, the metal will sparkle and burn. If too cold, the metal will not soften. You can also use a firebrick to block part of the opening to keep heat in. A gas forge is best for small to medium pieces. For long pieces, you may need to open both ends or use a larger forge.

In Africa, propane is available in many towns, but it can be expensive. A 9 kg cylinder lasts about 8 hours at full blast. To save gas, turn down the burner when you are not heating metal. Always turn off the gas at the cylinder when finished. Let the forge cool before moving it. Check the hose and regulator regularly for cracks. Never use a gas forge indoors without proper ventilation. Carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly.

5. Fire Safety and Emergency Procedures

Fire safety starts before you light the forge. Clear the area around your forge of dry grass, paper, rags, and any flammable liquids. Keep a bucket of water, a bucket of sand, and a fire extinguisher within arm's reach. In Africa, many workshops are outdoors or in open sheds, so wind can carry sparks. Work with your back to the wind if possible, and never light a forge near thatched roofs or fuel stores. Wear cotton or leather clothing, not synthetic, because synthetic melts and sticks to skin. Leather boots, gloves, and safety glasses are essential.

When lighting a coal forge, use dry kindling and never use petrol or paraffin to start the fire. That is a common and deadly mistake. If the fire gets out of control, do not panic. Turn off the air blast first. That will cool the fire quickly. Then spread the coals with a rake or shovel. If flames spread to nearby materials, use the water or sand. For a gas forge, if you smell gas, turn off the cylinder immediately. Do not light a match or switch on a light. Open all doors and windows. If the gas has been leaking, let it air out before trying to light.

Burns are the most common injury. For a minor burn, cool it with clean running water for 10 minutes, then cover with a clean cloth. Do not use butter or oil. For a serious burn, get medical help. If you get metal in your eye, do not rub it. Cover the eye and go to a clinic. Always keep a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and burn gel.

Emergency procedures: know where the nearest water tap and phone are. If a fire starts, call for help. If your clothes catch fire, stop, drop, and roll. Never run. In case of a gas leak, evacuate the area and call the fire service. Practice your emergency plan with everyone in the workshop. A few minutes of preparation can save lives and property.

6. Cleaning and Maintaining Your Forge

After every forging session, clean your forge. For a coal forge, let the fire die out completely. Do not pour water on a hot firepot; it can crack cast iron. Once cool, remove ash and clinker with a small shovel or spoon. Clinker is the glassy, rock-like waste that forms from impurities in coal. If left, it blocks air and makes the fire burn poorly. Dump ash in a metal container, not a plastic bin, because ash can stay hot for hours. Scrape the firepot clean and check the tuyere for blockages. A wire brush works well.

For a gas forge, turn off the gas at the cylinder and let the forge cool. Remove any scale or flux that has dripped onto the floor of the forge. A stiff wire brush or a scraper will do. Check the ceramic fibre lining for cracks. Small cracks are normal, but large ones let heat escape and can cause hot spots. You can patch small cracks with refractory cement. Check the burner tube for spider webs or dirt; a pipe cleaner or compressed air can clear it. Inspect the hose and regulator for cracks or wear. Replace any damaged parts immediately.

Maintain your air supply. For a hand-cranked blower, oil the bearings regularly. For an electric blower, clean the fan blades and check the wiring. A clogged blower reduces air and makes the fire cold. Keep the blower dry. For a bellows, check for holes in the leather or plastic; patch them with tape or glue. Store your forge under cover if possible. Moisture rusts metal parts. If you live in a humid area, wipe metal surfaces with an oily rag.

A well-maintained forge lasts for years and works better. Keep a log of any repairs or parts you replace. In Africa, where parts may be hard to find, improvise with local materials: a car brake drum for a firepot, a metal pipe for a tuyere, a plastic bag for bellows. But always prioritise safety. A clean forge is a safe forge. Spend ten minutes after each session to clean and check your equipment. It will save you time and money in the long run.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Forge and Fire Management for African Blacksmiths</h2>

<p>In African blacksmithing, the forge is not simply a tool it is the heart of the workshop, the difference between a profitable day and a wasted one. Whether you are forging <em>pangas</em> in Tanzania, <em>hoes</em> in Malawi, <em>knives</em> in Ghana, or <em>door hinges</em> in Nigeria, your ability to build, control, and maintain fire determines your output quality, your fuel costs, and your safety. This practical reference gives you the technical knowledge to run a forge that earns money.</p>

<p>For small-scale African smiths, the forge often represents the single largest recurring cost after labour. Charcoal prices in urban and peri-urban areas have risen sharply in many East African cities, a sack of charcoal that cost the equivalent of $8 in 2015 now costs $15$20. Gas forges, while cleaner and faster, require upfront investment and reliable gas supply. Understanding your options is a business decision, not just a technical one.</p>

<h2>Building and Maintaining a Coal Fire</h2>

<p>A properly built coal fire is the foundation of efficient forging. The goal is a deep, concentrated fire with a clean, hot centre not a wide, shallow fire that wastes fuel and produces inconsistent heat.</p>

<p><strong>Step-by-step fire building:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Clear the fire pot of old clinker and ash. A clean pot ensures proper airflow.</li>
<li>Place a small amount of paper or dry kindling at the bottom of the pot, directly above the tuyere.</li>
<li>Cover with a handful of dry wood shavings or small sticks. Light the kindling.</li>
<li>Once the wood is burning well, add a small amount of coal around the edges of the fire not on top. This allows the coal to coke (release volatiles) before reaching the hot centre.</li>
<li>Gradually push coked coal toward the centre as it ignites. Add more coal around the perimeter and repeat.</li>
<li>Maintain a fire that is roughly the size of a football (soccer ball) for general work. For welding, build a deeper, narrower fire.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Maintenance tips for African workshops:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Manage clinker:</strong> Impurities in coal melt into clinker a glassy, solid mass that blocks airflow. Remove clinker every 3045 minutes by lifting it out with a poker. In areas where coal quality is poor (common in parts of West Africa), clinker management is critical.</li>
<li><strong>Water management:</strong> A light sprinkle of water around the fire's edge helps control the fire size and reduces dust. Do not soak the fire.</li>
<li><strong>Fuel economy:</strong> Never let the fire burn wide and lazy. A focused fire uses 3040% less fuel than a sprawling one. This directly affects your profit margin.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Practical control levers:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Air blast:</strong> More air = hotter fire, but too much air wastes fuel and can burn steel. Use a hand-crank blower, bellows, or electric fan with a damper.</li>

<li><strong>Workpiece placement:</strong> Place the workpiece in the hottest zone just above the tuyere, buried in the fire. Do not lay it on top of the fire.</li>

<h2>Using a Gas Forge: Setup and Adjustment</h2>

<p>Gas forges are increasingly popular among African smiths producing high-value items knives, tools, decorative pieces because they are clean, fast, and do not require coal sourcing. However, they demand disciplined setup and safety.</p>

<p><strong>Setup checklist:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Place the forge on a stable, non-flammable surface (concrete, stone, or earth not wood).</li>
<li>Connect the gas hose to the regulator and burner. Check for leaks with soapy water.</li>
<li>Open the forge door or ensure ventilation before lighting.</li>
<li>Light a match or torch, hold it at the burner opening, then slowly open the gas valve.</li>
<li>Adjust the air choke and gas flow to achieve a neutral flame a steady blue flame with a slight roar.</li>
<li>Allow the forge to reach temperature (510 minutes) before inserting work.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Adjustment for different tasks:</strong> For welding, increase gas and air for a hotter flame. For tempering, reduce gas and use a lower, softer flame. Always adjust gradually and observe the workpiece colour.</p>

<h2>Cleaning and Maintaining Your Forge</h2>

<p>Maintenance is not optional it is a business investment. A well-maintained forge lasts years; a neglected one costs you fuel, time, and safety.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>After every session:</strong> Remove ash and clinker. Wipe down the forge body. Check the tuyere for blockages.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> Inspect the clay lining for cracks. Repair with fire clay or a clay-sand mixture. Check the air supply system (bellows, blower, or fan) for wear.</li>
<li><strong>Monthly:</strong> Deep-clean the fire pot. Check gas hoses and connections for cracks or leaks. Inspect the forge structure for heat damage.</li>
<li><strong>Seasonally:</strong> Re-line the fire pot if the clay is significantly degraded. Replace worn tuyere pipes. Service blowers or fans.</li>
</ul>

<ul>
<li> Fire pot cleared of clinker and ash before lighting</li>
<li> Fire built with kindling at bottom, coal added around edges first</li>

<li> Workpiece placed in hottest zone, not on top of fire</li>
<li> Water bucket or sand within arm's reach at all times</li>
