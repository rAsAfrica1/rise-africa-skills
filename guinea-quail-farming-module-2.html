1. Designing a Guinea Fowl House Using Local Materials (Mud, Thatch, Wire Mesh)

A good guinea fowl house keeps birds safe, dry, and healthy without costing much. You need three local materials: mud or clay soil, thatch or grass, and wire mesh (also called chicken wire). The house should be at least 2 meters long, 1.5 meters wide, and 1.2 meters high at the front, sloping to 0.8 meters at the back. This gives room for 8 to 10 guinea fowl. Start by digging a shallow trench 30 cm deep around the perimeter. Mix mud with water and a little cow dung to make strong walls. Build walls 20 cm thick, leaving a door 60 cm wide and 1 meter high. Use wooden poles (e.g., eucalyptus) as corner posts, buried 50 cm into the ground. For the roof, use thatch grass tied to wooden rafters. Thatch is cool and cheap, but it must be at least 10 cm thick to shed rain. Cover the entire house with wire mesh on the outside, including the door, to stop snakes and rats. Bury the wire mesh 30 cm into the ground to prevent digging predators. The floor should be packed earth, raised 10 cm above ground level to avoid flooding. Add a small window (30 cm x 30 cm) covered with wire mesh for ventilation. Inside, place a perch made from a strong stick 5 cm in diameter, fixed 60 cm above the floor. Guinea fowl like to roost high, so add a second perch at 1 meter. Clean the house weekly, replacing damp litter with dry grass or wood shavings. Total cost: about 5,000 to 10,000 shillings if you collect mud and thatch yourself. This design works in most African climates, from savannah to forest zones.

2. Building a Quail Cage System with Locally Available Timber and Netting

Quail are small and can be raised in cages, which saves space and protects them from predators. A simple cage system uses timber (e.g., 2 cm x 5 cm planks) and plastic or nylon netting with 1 cm holes. Build a cage 60 cm long, 40 cm wide, and 30 cm high. This holds 10 to 12 quail. For a small farm, make a stack of 3 cages, one above the other, with a 10 cm gap between them for droppings. Use a wooden frame: cut four legs 1 meter long, then attach horizontal supports every 35 cm. Wrap the frame with netting, leaving a door on one side (30 cm x 30 cm) that opens outward. Secure the netting with small nails or staples. The floor should be netting too, so droppings fall through. Place a plastic tray or old basin under each cage to catch manure. For the roof, use a piece of zinc or plastic sheet to keep rain out. Inside each cage, put a small feeder (a cut plastic bottle) and a drinker (an inverted bottle in a shallow dish). Quail need 24-hour light for egg production, so hang a solar bulb or kerosene lamp nearby. Clean the trays daily and wash the netting weekly with soapy water. Total cost: 3,000 to 6,000 shillings per cage if you use scrap timber. This system works in small yards or even on a veranda. Remember to place the cage in shade, as quail suffer from heat stress.

3. Essential Equipment: Feeders, Drinkers, and Brooders Made from Recycled Items

You do not need to buy expensive equipment. Recycled items work just as well. For feeders, take a clean plastic bottle (1.5 liters) and cut a hole 5 cm from the bottom. Fill it with feed and hang it upside down in the pen. The feed slowly falls into a shallow dish below. For guinea fowl, use a larger container like a 5-liter jerrycan with a hole cut in the side. For drinkers, use an old bucket or basin. Place a smaller tin inside, upside down, so water seeps out slowly. Or use a plastic bottle with a small hole in the cap, inverted into a dish. For quail, use shallow lids from jars. Always clean feeders and drinkers daily with clean water and a little soap to prevent disease. For a brooder, you need a warm, dry box. Use a cardboard box or an old wooden crate. Line it with dry grass or sawdust. For heat, use a charcoal stove (jiko) placed outside the box, with a metal pipe or tin to carry warmth inside. Or use a 60-watt incandescent bulb hung 30 cm above the chicks. For 50 chicks, one bulb is enough. Cover the box with a cloth or sack at night to keep heat in, but leave a small opening for air. Check the temperature with your hand: if it feels hot on your skin, it is too hot for chicks. They should be spread out, not huddled. Adjust the height of the bulb or move the stove closer or farther. Change litter every 2 days. Total cost: under 2,000 shillings for a brooder. This method is used by farmers in Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana.

4. Predator-Proofing Your Pens Against Snakes, Rats, and Hawks

Predators can wipe out your flock in one night. The main enemies are snakes, rats, and hawks. To stop snakes, use wire mesh with holes no bigger than 1 cm. Snakes can squeeze through small gaps, so check for holes around doors, windows, and roof edges. Bury the wire mesh 30 cm into the ground and bend it outward at the bottom to form an L-shape. Snakes cannot dig under that. Also keep the area around the pen clear of tall grass and rubbish, as snakes hide there. For rats, they chew through plastic netting and even thin wood. Use metal wire mesh (not nylon) for all openings. Set rat traps or use a cat. Store feed in sealed containers, not in open bags. Rats also carry disease, so control them quickly. For hawks and other birds of prey, cover the top of the pen with wire mesh or a roof. Guinea fowl are good at warning each other, but young chicks are easy targets. If you free-range, always have a watchman or a dog. For quail cages, hang shiny objects like old CDs or tin lids around the cage; hawks are scared of reflections. Also, place the cage under a tree or shade net so hawks cannot dive from above. Check your pen every evening before dark. Repair any holes immediately. A well-protected pen means you lose fewer birds and make more profit. Farmers in Uganda and Tanzania use these methods successfully.

5. Space Requirements: How Many Birds per Square Meter for Guinea Fowl and Quail

Overcrowding causes stress, disease, and fighting. For guinea fowl, give each bird at least 0.5 square meters (about 2 birds per square meter) in a house. If they have an outdoor run, you can keep 1 bird per square meter inside, but the run should be at least 2 square meters per bird. For example, a house 2 m x 1.5 m (3 square meters) holds 6 guinea fowl comfortably. If you keep 10, they will fight and get sick. For quail, they need much less space. In a cage, give each quail 0.02 square meters (about 50 quail per square meter). But for good health, keep 30 to 40 quail per square meter. A cage 60 cm x 40 cm (0.24 square meters) holds 10 to 12 quail. For breeding quail, give each pair 0.05 square meters. Always provide enough feeders and drinkers so that all birds can eat at once. For guinea fowl, one feeder (30 cm long) serves 10 birds. For quail, one small feeder serves 15 birds. If you see birds panting, huddling, or pecking each other, they need more space. Also, remember that guinea fowl fly well, so the house must be tall enough (at least 1.2 m) to prevent them from hitting the roof. Quail are ground birds but can fly short distances, so a cage height of 25 cm is enough. Adjust numbers based on your climate: in hot areas, give more space for air circulation.

6. Setting Up a Simple Brooder for Chicks Using a Charcoal Stove or Bulb

A brooder keeps chicks warm during their first 4 weeks. You can build one with a charcoal stove (jiko) or an electric bulb. First, choose a dry, draft-free corner of your house or a small room. Use a cardboard box (60 cm x 60 cm x 40 cm) or a wooden crate. Line the bottom with dry sand or wood shavings. For a charcoal stove, place the stove outside the box, and run a metal pipe (or a tin can with both ends open) from the stove into the box, 15 cm above the floor. The pipe carries warm air. Never put the stove inside the box because of fumes. For a bulb, hang a 60-watt bulb 30 cm above the floor of the box. For 50 chicks, one bulb is enough. Cover the box with a cloth or sack, leaving a small gap for air. Check the temperature with a thermometer: it should be 35Â°C in the first week, then reduce by 3Â°C each week until 21Â°C. If you don't have a thermometer, watch the chicks: if they huddle under the heat, it's too cold; if they spread out away from the heat, it's too hot; if they are evenly spread, it's just right. Provide feed and water in shallow dishes. Use a small stone in the drinker so chicks don't drown. Clean the brooder daily, removing wet litter. After 4 weeks, chicks can move to a larger pen. This simple brooder costs less than 3,000 shillings and works in rural areas without electricity.

Guinea fowl are hardy but they are not invincible. They roost high, fly well, and panic easily â so a house that works for chickens will often fail for guinea fowl. A charcoal stove or open flame inside a brooder produces carbon monoxide, which is odourless and can kill every chick in the box within an hour â and can also kill you if the brooder is inside a closed room. Never place a burning charcoal stove inside a sealed box or a room with no ventilation. If you use a bulb, ensure the wiring is insulated, the socket is off the floor, and the bulb is guarded so birds cannot touch it. Have a bucket of sand or water within reach at all times.




Predator-Proofing Your Pens



Predators are the number-one cause of stock loss for small-scale guinea fowl and quail keepers. Snakes and rats dig and squeeze; hawks strike from above. The defence is a combination of a buried apron, tight mesh, and overhead netting.



Against snakes and rats: Bury 1-inch wire mesh 50 cm deep and 50 cm outward around the entire perimeter â this is the single most effective measure. Rats will also climb smooth walls, so keep the area around the house clear of rubbish and store feed in sealed drums, not sacks on the floor.



Against hawks and other birds of prey: Cover the outdoor run with 1-inch mesh or shade netting at 2 m height, or string reflective tape and old CDs across the run. Guinea fowl are noisy and will alarm, but a hawk can still take keets in seconds if the run is open.



Against ants and termites: Stand cage and house legs in tins of used engine oil or water. Check weekly and top up.
