Module 5

🐕🐈 Senior Dog and Cat Nutrition

How to adjust a maintenance diet for the healthy older dog and cat: energy, protein density, joints, kidneys, brain, digestibility and body weight, using only figures that can be defended.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Calculate resting and maintenance energy requirements for an older dog and an older cat
  • Calculate a sourced omega-3 dose for joint support and check it against the safe upper limit
  • Distinguish a healthy-ageing dietary adjustment from a prescribed therapeutic renal diet
  • State plainly which brain-health claims can be supported from verified standards and which cannot
  • Rank common carbohydrate and fibre sources using published digestibility trial data
  • Score body condition and convert the score into a target weight and calorie allowance

The six lessons

1. Metabolic Changes in Aging Pets 2. Joint and Bone Support 3. Kidney and Urinary Tract Support 4. Cognitive Function and Brain Health 5. Digestibility Enhancement for Senior Pets 6. Weight Management in Seniors
Lesson 5.1~12 min

Metabolic Changes in Aging Pets

In this lesson
  • Calculate resting and maintenance energy requirements for an older dog and an older cat
  • Explain why protein density per 1000 kcal must rise when energy intake falls
  • Identify which senior energy figures are published and which the verified reference does not supply

An older animal is not a sick animal, and a senior diet is not a therapeutic diet. What changes with age is body composition, activity, digestive efficiency and the margin for error. Your job as a producer is to adjust a maintenance formula, not to invent a medical product.

Start with energy, because that is where most senior feeding goes wrong. Resting energy requirement is 70 multiplied by body weight in kilograms to the power 0.75, and that equation is valid at any weight. For animals between 2 and 45 kilograms there is a linear shortcut, 30 times body weight plus 70, which is close enough for a feeding guide but not for a formulation sheet.

Work a dog. A 12 kilogram neutered dog: 12 to the power 0.75 is 6.45, so RER is 6.45 x 70 = 451 kcal a day. As an ordinary neutered adult you multiply by 1.6, giving 722 kcal. As an inactive or obesity-prone animal, which is the row a quiet older dog belongs in, the multiplier is 1.2 to 1.4, giving 541 to 631 kcal. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram, that is 190 grams a day for the active adult and 142 to 166 grams for the older dog. The owner who keeps feeding the old ration is overfeeding by roughly a quarter.

Be honest about a gap here. The verified reference set gives no separate canine geriatric multiplier. Senior dogs are handled through the inactive and obesity-prone rows of 1.2 to 1.4. Anyone who quotes you a precise senior dog factor is quoting something the standards do not publish.

Cats are different and the direction surprises people. The published feline factors are 1.1 to 1.4 for a cat aged seven to eleven years, and 1.1 to 1.6 for a cat over eleven. The very old cat trends upward, not downward, because digestive efficiency declines and the animal must eat more to absorb the same amount. Work it: a 5 kilogram cat has an RER of 5 to the power 0.75 times 70, that is 3.34 x 70 = 234 kcal. At 1.1 that is 257 kcal; at 1.6 it is 374 kcal. On a 3800 kcal per kilogram dry food that is 68 to 98 grams a day. The spread is 44 percent. You cannot formulate your way out of that range, so the feeding guide must tell the owner to weigh the cat monthly and adjust.

Now the part that matters most for formulation. Nutrient minima do not fall with age. Adult maintenance for a dog is 18.0 percent crude protein and 5.5 percent crude fat on a dry matter basis, and for a cat 26.0 percent protein and 9.0 percent fat. Restated on energy, that is 45 grams of protein per 1000 kcal for the dog and 65 grams per 1000 kcal for the cat. If the older dog now eats 590 kcal instead of 722, and the food is unchanged, its daily protein intake has fallen by 18 percent even though the food still meets the profile. That is the argument for a senior diet: the same or higher protein and mineral concentration in fewer calories, not a lower-protein food. The old habit of cutting protein for old animals is not supported by anything in the standards for a healthy animal.

An example from Nakuru, Kenya. A producer selling one adult dog food added a senior line by holding the recipe at 24 percent crude protein and dropping fat from 12 to 9 percent, which cut energy density from about 3800 to roughly 3450 kcal per kilogram. The customer feeds a similar volume, the animal takes in about 9 percent fewer calories per gram, and protein per 1000 kcal rises. No new ingredient was needed, so the only real cost was a second bag design at USD 190 for plates and a slightly lower fat bill of about USD 0.04 per kilogram.

One last discipline. Age is not a diagnosis. If an older animal is losing weight, drinking more, or has a poor coat, that is a veterinary visit, not a bag of senior food. Print that sentence on your pack and you will build more trust than any claim.

Compare that with what the owner actually feeds, measured on a scale, and write the difference in grams.

Lesson 5.2~12 min

Joint and Bone Support

In this lesson
  • Calculate a sourced omega-3 dose for joint support and check it against the safe upper limit
  • Apply the calcium and phosphorus minima and maxima correctly in an adult and senior formulation
  • Separate joint ingredients that carry sourced dosing from those that do not

Joint problems are the commonest reason an owner in your market will ask for a senior food. Two things in this area can be defended with numbers, and several popular ones cannot. Know which is which before you print anything.

Start with the one that is fully sourced: long chain omega-3. The therapeutic dose of combined EPA and DHA for osteoarthritis is 310 mg per kilogram of body weight to the power 0.75, which works out at about 147 mg per kilogram of body weight per day. Take a 20 kilogram dog. 20 to the power 0.75 is 9.46, so the dose is 9.46 x 310 = 2932 mg of EPA plus DHA a day. Now check the ceiling. The safe upper limit is 370 mg per kg BW^0.75, equivalently 280 mg per 100 kcal or 2800 mg per 1000 kcal of metabolisable energy. For this dog that is 9.46 x 370 = 3500 mg. The joint dose of 2932 mg therefore uses 84 percent of the allowance. There is very little margin, and doses stack: diet plus a fish oil supplement plus an owner-added capsule will cross the line. Excess is associated with altered platelet function, gastrointestinal disturbance, effects on wound healing and supplement-drug interactions, and omega-3 combined with NSAIDs has been linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs. Many older dogs are on NSAIDs.

Compare the lower doses so you can see the scale: 140 mg per kg^0.75 for kidney disease, which is 1324 mg for the same dog, and 115 mg per kg^0.75 for cardiovascular support, which is 1088 mg. Osteoarthritis is the most demanding of the three.

There is a second constraint on fish oil that catches producers out. High-fat fish carry vitamin D. The AAFCO safe upper limit for dogs was cut from 5000 to 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter in 2016, a change that specifically reduced how much high-fat fish such as salmon could safely be used in pet food recipes. The EU legal ceiling is lower still at 2270 IU per kilogram of dry matter. Note also that no feline safe upper limit for EPA and DHA is given in the verified reference, so a cat joint claim built on a precise fish oil dose is not defensible from these standards.

Now bone. For an adult dog the calcium minimum is 0.5 percent of dry matter and phosphorus 0.4 percent, with a phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent. For an adult cat it is 0.6 and 0.5. The calcium to phosphorus ratio must sit between 1:1 and 2:1, with an optimum quoted at 1.2 to 1.4 to 1. Older animals do not need calcium topping up, and the reflex to add bone meal to an old dog's food is a way to breach the ratio. Where excess calcium really does damage is in the growing large-breed puppy: the maximum is 1.8 percent of dry matter for foods that may be fed to puppies weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, and 2.5 percent otherwise, because supplementation above 3 percent causes severe osteochondrosis. If you make one food for all ages, the 1.8 percent ceiling binds you.

Worked ration check, and a common trap. Suppose your senior dog recipe is 8 percent bone-containing meat and bone meal at 26 percent calcium and 12 percent phosphorus. Calcium contributed is 8 x 0.26 = 2.08 percent, phosphorus 8 x 0.12 = 0.96 percent. Calcium is already near the 2.5 percent limit and would exceed 1.8 percent if the pack could be fed to a large puppy, and the ratio is 2.08 divided by 0.96 = 2.17 to 1, above the 2:1 ceiling. Drop the inclusion to 5 percent: calcium 1.30, phosphorus 0.60, ratio 2.17 still. The ratio problem is in the ingredient, not the inclusion, so you must add a phosphorus source or change the meal.

An example from Kampala, Uganda. A small producer costed a joint line: locally landed Nile perch oil at USD 7.50 a litre delivering roughly 250 mg EPA plus DHA per millilitre. The 20 kilogram dog's 2932 mg needs about 11.7 ml a day, which is USD 0.09 a day or USD 2.63 a month. Glucosamine and chondroitin were quoted at USD 42 a kilogram, but no dose in the verified reference set supports a specific inclusion, so the honest route is to state the omega-3 content on the pack, which you can measure and defend, and leave the rest out.

Lesson 5.3~12 min

Kidney and Urinary Tract Support

In this lesson
  • Distinguish a healthy-ageing dietary adjustment from a prescribed therapeutic renal diet
  • Control dietary phosphorus load by calculating the contribution of ash-rich ingredients
  • Increase an older animal's water intake through the choice of product format

This lesson is about the healthy older animal. Diets for animals with diagnosed chronic kidney disease are staged and prescribed by a veterinarian, and that work is covered in Module 9 Lesson 3. Do not build a restricted renal diet from this lesson. Build a sensible maintenance food that does not load an ageing kidney unnecessarily.

The difference matters commercially as well as clinically. A therapeutic renal diet is deliberately formulated below the maintenance profile, which means it cannot carry a normal complete-and-balanced claim and must be sold under veterinary direction. A senior maintenance food stays fully within the profile. If you blur the two you will either mislabel a product or under-feed a healthy animal.

What you can legitimately do is manage phosphorus. The adult dog minimum is 0.4 percent of dry matter and the maximum is 1.6 percent; the adult cat minimum is 0.5 percent. Many small-producer recipes drift towards 1.2 or 1.4 percent phosphorus for no reason other than cheap bone-rich meals, and that is a load you have no need to impose. Note carefully that formulating towards the lower half of the legal range is not the same as restriction below the minimum, which is a clinical decision.

Work the arithmetic on a real recipe. Suppose a dry dog food contains 20 percent poultry by-product meal at 4.5 percent phosphorus, 8 percent fish meal at 2.8 percent phosphorus, and 4 percent bone-containing meat and bone meal at 12 percent phosphorus. Contributions are 20 x 0.045 = 0.90, 8 x 0.028 = 0.224, and 4 x 0.12 = 0.48, giving 1.60 percent before the premix adds any more. That is at the dog maximum. Now cut the meat and bone meal to 1 percent and replace the three points with maize: 20 x 0.045 = 0.90, 0.224, and 1 x 0.12 = 0.12, total 1.244, plus roughly 0.09 from 3 percent extra maize at 0.3 percent phosphorus, giving about 1.33 percent. You have removed a fifth of the phosphorus load without touching protein. Recheck calcium and the ratio afterwards, because bone meal was carrying both.

Water is the second lever and it is free. Dry foods run 2800 to 4800 kcal per kilogram and wet foods 700 to 1300 kcal per kilogram, which means a wet-fed animal takes in several times more water with its food. A 4 kilogram neutered cat needs about 238 kcal a day: at a wet-food density of 1000 kcal per kilogram that is 238 grams of food, roughly 2.8 pouches of 85 grams, most of it water. The same cat on dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram eats 63 grams. Older cats in hot climates are the group most likely to be quietly under-drinking, so a small wet or semi-moist line, or simply telling customers to add warm water to the ration, is a genuine service that costs you nothing.

Be honest about the limits of your evidence. The verified standards give minima and maxima for healthy animals. They do not give urinary pH targets, magnesium limits for struvite control, or staged restriction levels; magnesium was not even captured from the cat table. Anyone selling you a urinary formula recipe with precise mineral targets is quoting figures you cannot check. Refer those customers to a veterinarian and keep your product honest.

An example from Mwanza, Tanzania. A producer noticed that three of her repeat customers had older cats and asked her local vet what she was seeing. The answer was dehydration and constipation rather than kidney failure. Instead of launching a renal line, she introduced a 400 gram wet pouch pack at USD 0.62 to produce and USD 1.10 wholesale, sold as one pouch a day alongside dry food. Her ingredient cost per 1000 kcal is far higher for wet than dry, so she priced it as a supplement to the dry ration rather than a replacement, which kept the customer's monthly spend acceptable.

The rule to hold on to: if an animal is drinking more, urinating more, losing weight, or straining to urinate, that is a veterinary case today, not a product recommendation. Print your local vet's number on the bag if they will let you.

Lesson 5.4~12 min

Cognitive Function and Brain Health

In this lesson
  • State plainly which brain-health claims can be supported from verified standards and which cannot
  • Apply the vitamin E, vitamin A and choline minima correctly in a senior formulation
  • Write pack wording about behaviour change that directs the owner to a veterinarian

This is the lesson where the honest answer is the most valuable product feature you have. Cognitive decline in old dogs and cats is real, and owners will pay for anything that promises to help. The verified standards used throughout this course contain nutrient minima and maxima for healthy animals. They do not contain a validated brain-health formulation, a dose for medium chain triglycerides, a dose for an antioxidant blend, or evidence for any cognition claim. If you print one, you are inventing it.

So work with what is real. Three sets of nutrients on the profile have defensible roles in nervous tissue and in protecting fat from oxidation, and you can meet or comfortably exceed their minima without claiming anything you cannot prove.

First, vitamin E. The dog minimum is 50 IU per kilogram of dry matter and the cat minimum 40 IU per kilogram, and no maximum is listed. Vitamin E is the antioxidant that protects dietary fat, including the long chain omega-3 you may be adding for joints, from going rancid in the bag and in the animal. The verified reference is explicit that a specific ratio of vitamin E to polyunsaturated fat is not retrieved, so raise vitamin E when you raise fish oil, document that you did, and do not publish a ratio as if it were a standard.

Second, choline, which is a structural component of cell membranes and of acetylcholine. The dog minimum is 1360 mg per kilogram of dry matter and the cat minimum is 2400 mg, nearly twice as high. Choline is cheap. Choline chloride at 60 percent purity bought at USD 2.20 a kilogram supplies 600 grams of choline per kilogram of product, so meeting the cat minimum of 2400 mg per kilogram of feed needs 2400 divided by 600000 kilograms, that is 4 grams of the product per kilogram of feed, costing 0.004 x 2.20 = USD 0.0088 per kilogram of feed. Under one cent. There is no excuse for a senior cat food to sit at the minimum.

Third, the B vitamins, which the profile sets for both species. For the dog, thiamine 2.25, riboflavin 5.2, pantothenic acid 12, niacin 13.6, pyridoxine 1.5, folic acid 0.216 and vitamin B12 0.028 mg per kilogram of dry matter. For the cat the same list runs much higher in places: thiamine 5.6, riboflavin 4.0, pantothenic acid 5.75, pyridoxine 4.0, folic acid 0.8, B12 0.020, and niacin 60 mg per kilogram, more than four times the dog figure. That niacin gap exists because cats cannot synthesise substantial amounts of niacin from tryptophan, so adding tryptophan does not compensate. These are water-soluble vitamins lost in processing and in storage, which is exactly why premix quality and shelf life, covered in Module 7, matter more in an old animal's food than in a young one's.

One more grounded point. Cats cannot cleave beta-carotene to retinol because they lack the intestinal dioxygenase enzyme, so a cat needs preformed vitamin A from animal sources at 3332 IU per kilogram of dry matter for adult maintenance. Beta-carotene in a cat food is a colourant, not a vitamin. Dogs can make the conversion and their minimum is 5000 IU per kilogram at both life stages.

An example from Accra, Ghana. A producer wanted a Brain Care line. Her vet reviewed the draft pack and struck out every cognition claim. What survived was a fair statement of the guaranteed analysis, a line saying the food supplies vitamin E and choline above the minimum for adult maintenance, and a boxed note reading: if your older animal is pacing at night, getting lost in familiar rooms, forgetting house training or changing its interaction with the family, see your veterinarian. She reported that the box drove more clinic referrals and more repeat sales than the claim would have, because two clinics started recommending the food by name.

The commercial lesson is the general one. In a market with no strong enforcement, the producer who refuses to make unprovable claims is building the only durable asset available, which is trust.

Lesson 5.5~12 min

Digestibility Enhancement for Senior Pets

In this lesson
  • Rank common carbohydrate and fibre sources using published digestibility trial data
  • Set practical inclusion limits for rendered by-product meals from faecal-quality evidence
  • Calculate the metabolisable energy of a formulation using the modified Atwater route

Older animals extract less from the same food, which is why the published feline factors rise after eleven years. You cannot change the animal, so you change the food. Digestibility is also the one senior claim you can genuinely test in your own shed, by weighing what goes in and scoring what comes out.

Start with starch, because a great deal of nonsense is sold here. In a controlled comparison of six carbohydrate sources in extruded dog diets, starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent across all diets. The claim that dogs cannot digest grain is not supported. What did differ was the rest of the ration. Brewer's rice and cassava flour gave the greatest digestibility of dry matter, organic matter and gross energy, followed by maize and sorghum, with pea and lentil diets lowest. For an African producer this is unusually good news, because cassava and rice by-products are among the cheapest starches available across the continent, and they happen to be the most digestible in this trial. Note also the glycaemic pattern: brewer's rice, maize and cassava gave the highest immediate post-prandial response within 30 minutes, while sorghum, lentil and pea gave a higher later response. If you want a slower release, sorghum is your local option.

Now fibre. In a Beagle trial comparing beet pulp, cellulose and Miscanthus grass all at 10 percent inclusion, beet pulp gave dry matter digestibility of 81.3 percent against 78.2 for Miscanthus and 77.2 for cellulose, and organic matter 86.1 against 82.1 and 80.8. But protein digestibility was lower with beet pulp at 84.5 percent against 87.9 and 87.6, fat was 88.8 against 90.7 and 90.9, and the stool score was 3.15 for beet pulp against 3.68 and 3.64, meaning softer stools. Beet pulp is moderately fermentable: it lifts energy and dry matter digestibility, modestly depresses protein and fat digestibility, and softens the stool at 10 percent. For an old animal already loose, that is the wrong direction. The widely quoted 7.5 percent practical ceiling for beet pulp could not be verified in the reference set, so treat it as a rule of thumb rather than a standard, and use the 10 percent trial data as what is actually known.

Protein meals next. In a Beagle trial, bone protein meal was tested at 0, 6, 12 and 24 percent and feather meal at 0, 5, 10 and 20 percent. The conclusion was that bone protein meal could be included up to 12 percent and feather meal up to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores. Digestibility itself held up at higher levels, with apparent crude protein digestibility of 78.0 percent at zero bone protein meal and 82.4 percent at 24 percent, but faecal quality suffered. In a senior food, faecal quality is the thing the customer sees, so use the lower limits: 12 percent and 5 percent.

Soybean meal deserves a caution. A systematic review of 17 studies found soybean meal reduced dry matter digestibility in most studies, increased crude protein digestibility in several, decreased fat digestibility in multiple, and reduced gross energy digestibility across most trials. No maximum inclusion consensus exists. Soy is cheap and widely available in Nigeria and Zambia, but it is not a free lunch in a senior food.

Finally, calculate the energy of what you have built, using the modified Atwater route. Gross energy in kcal per 100 g is 5.7 times protein plus 9.4 times fat plus 4.1 times the sum of nitrogen-free extract and crude fibre. Take a diet at 26 percent protein, 12 percent fat, 3 percent crude fibre, 45 percent NFE, 8 percent moisture. GE = 5.7 x 26 = 148.2, plus 9.4 x 12 = 112.8, plus 4.1 x 48 = 196.8, total 457.8 kcal per 100 g. Dog energy digestibility is 91.2 minus 1.43 times crude fibre times 100 divided by 100 minus moisture, that is 91.2 minus (1.43 x 3 x 100 / 92) = 91.2 minus 4.66 = 86.5 percent. DE = 457.8 x 0.865 = 396.0. ME for dogs is DE minus 1.04 times protein, that is 396.0 minus 27.0 = 369.0 kcal per 100 g, or 3690 kcal per kilogram.

An example from Ibadan, Nigeria. A producer swapped 8 percent maize for 8 percent cassava flour at USD 0.28 a kilogram against maize at USD 0.41, saving USD 0.0104 per kilogram of feed, and reported firmer stools from her older-dog customers. She then verified it properly by asking six owners to score stools for two weeks before and after.

Lesson 5.6~12 min

Weight Management in Seniors

In this lesson
  • Score body condition and convert the score into a target weight and calorie allowance
  • Calculate a weight-loss ration that still meets protein and mineral minima
  • Design a monthly weighing and follow-up routine that a small producer can run

Most older dogs and cats you will meet are either too fat or, later, quietly too thin. Both are feeding problems you can help with, and both start with a scale and a set of fingers, not a formula.

Score the body first. Run your hands over the ribs. If you can feel each rib easily with light pressure and see a waist from above and a tuck from the side, the animal is about right, a 5 on the 9-point scale. If you must press to find ribs and there is no waist, that is 7. If ribs are invisible under fat deposits over the back and tail base, that is 8 or 9. Every point above 5 is roughly 10 percent of body weight. So a 24 kilogram dog scoring 8 is about 30 percent over target, and the target weight is 24 divided by 1.30 = 18.5 kilograms.

Now calculate on the target weight, not the current weight. RER for 18.5 kilograms is 18.5 to the power 0.75 times 70. 18.5 to the power 0.75 is 8.92, so RER is 624 kcal. The published weight-loss factor for dogs is 1.0 times RER, so the allowance is 624 kcal a day. Compare that with what the dog is getting: if the owner feeds 350 grams of a 3800 kcal per kilogram food, that is 1330 kcal, more than double. On the same food, 624 kcal is 164 grams. Give the owner the grams, not the calories, and give them a cup or a scoop that has been weighed and marked.

Cats are stricter. The published feline weight-loss factor is 0.8 times RER, and obesity-prone cats sit at 1.0. A 6 kilogram cat that should weigh 4.5 kilograms has an RER at target of 4.5 to the power 0.75 times 70. 4.5 to the power 0.75 is 3.09, so RER is 216 kcal, and at 0.8 the allowance is 173 kcal a day, which on a 3800 kcal per kilogram food is 46 grams. That is a small pile of food, and it must be split into at least two meals. Never starve a cat quickly; rapid weight loss in cats is dangerous and any plan should be run past a veterinarian.

Here is where formulation comes in. Cutting the ration cuts every nutrient with it. The dog above now eats 164 grams instead of 350, so at 24 percent crude protein it takes in 39 grams of protein instead of 84. The way to protect the animal is a food that is less energy dense and more nutrient dense: lower fat towards the minimum of 5.5 percent for dogs or 9.0 percent for cats, note that the cat fat minimum does not drop for adult maintenance, raise fibre to fill the gut, and hold protein well above the minima of 18.0 percent for dogs and 26.0 percent for cats. Remember from Lesson 5 that beet pulp at 10 percent raises dry matter digestibility but softens stools, whereas cellulose is a diluent that does not, so a light food often uses a less fermentable fibre on purpose.

Work the economics, because this is a product decision. A light formula at 8 percent fat instead of 14 percent replaces 6 points of fat at USD 1.30 a kilogram with 6 points of maize bran at USD 0.22, saving 0.06 x 1.08 = USD 0.065 per kilogram of feed. But the customer now buys fewer kilograms, because the animal eats less. On a 20 kilogram dog at 164 grams instead of 350 grams a day, monthly volume falls from 10.5 to 4.9 kilograms. Losing half a customer's volume is the reason many producers quietly avoid selling weight control. The way to make it work is to price the light line higher per kilogram and to keep the customer for the years the animal now lives.

The other half of the problem is the thin old animal. If body condition falls below 4 despite adequate feeding, that is a veterinary case: dental disease, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism in cats and cancer all present this way. Do not sell more food into unexplained weight loss.

An example from Bulawayo, Zimbabwe. A producer runs a free monthly weigh-in outside a veterinary clinic on the first Saturday. She records name, weight and body condition score in a book, gives the owner a printed card with the target weight and the grams per day, and sells the light food in 2 kilogram packs at USD 3.40. Ninety animals are on her book. The weigh-in costs her a morning and a borrowed scale, and it is the reason her repeat rate is high.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the RER of a 12 kg dog?

12 to the power 0.75 is 6.45, and 6.45 x 70 = 451 kcal. 722 kcal is the figure after applying the neutered adult factor of 1.6.

2. Which multiplier row does the verified reference use for a quiet older dog?

No separate canine geriatric multiplier is published in the reference set. Senior dogs are handled through the inactive and obesity-prone rows.

3. Why do the published energy factors for cats over eleven years trend upward?

The very old cat's factor range rises to 1.1 to 1.6, the opposite of the usual assumption, because absorption falls with age.

4. An older dog now eats fewer calories of an unchanged food. What happens to its daily protein intake?

Percentage minima are concentrations. Fewer grams of food means fewer grams of protein, which is why senior diets raise protein per 1000 kcal rather than lowering protein.

5. An owner says her ten-year-old cat is drinking much more and losing weight. What is the correct response?

Those are clinical signs, not signs of ageing. Age is not a diagnosis, and a producer must not treat symptoms with a product.

6. What is the daily osteoarthritis EPA plus DHA dose for a 20 kg dog?

20 to the power 0.75 is 9.46, and 9.46 x 310 = 2932 mg. 3500 mg is the safe upper limit for this dog and 1324 mg is the kidney dose.

7. Why is the joint dose of fish oil a formulation risk?

2932 mg against a limit of 3500 mg leaves very little margin, and doses from diet, supplements and owner additions all stack.

8. A dog on an NSAID is also given a high fish oil dose. What is the documented concern?

Omega-3 combined with NSAIDs has been linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs, alongside altered platelet function and effects on wound healing.

9. A meat and bone meal supplies calcium 2.08 percent and phosphorus 0.96 percent of the diet. What is wrong?

2.08 divided by 0.96 is 2.17, which breaches the maximum calcium to phosphorus ratio of 2:1. Lowering the inclusion does not fix a ratio problem.

10. Which joint ingredient can be stated on a pack with a defensible dose from the verified standards?

Sourced dose figures and a safe upper limit exist for EPA and DHA. The other ingredients have no dose in the verified reference set, so no specific inclusion can be defended.

11. What is the difference between a senior maintenance food and a therapeutic renal diet?

A renal diet restricts nutrients below the profile on purpose and therefore cannot carry a normal complete-and-balanced claim. That work is prescribed and monitored by a veterinarian.

12. A recipe contributes 0.90, 0.224 and 0.48 percent phosphorus from three meals. What is the total and what does it mean?

0.90 plus 0.224 plus 0.48 is 1.604 percent, which is at the dog phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent before the premix adds any more.

13. Why does wet food help an older cat's water intake?

Meeting the same calorie requirement on wet food means eating several times the weight of food, and most of that extra weight is water.

14. A customer asks for a urinary formula with a specific pH target. What should you do?

Urinary pH targets and magnesium limits are not in the verified reference set, and magnesium was not even captured from the cat table. Do not quote figures you cannot check.

15. How can you reduce dietary phosphorus without cutting protein?

Bone-rich meals carry most of the phosphorus load. Swapping some out for a low-phosphorus cereal cuts phosphorus while protein is preserved by the remaining meat meals, but calcium and the ratio must be rechecked.

16. What does the verified reference set provide on canine cognitive decline?

The standards cover requirements for healthy animals. No cognition dose or blend is in the reference, so any such claim would be invented.

17. Why is the cat niacin minimum 60 mg per kilogram against the dog's 13.6?

Because tryptophan does not substitute in the cat, niacin has to be supplied as such, and supplementing tryptophan will not offset a low-niacin cat formula.

18. How much 60 percent choline chloride at USD 2.20 per kilogram is needed to meet the cat choline minimum of 2400 mg per kilogram of feed?

The product supplies 600 g of choline per kilogram, so 2400 mg needs 4 g of product per kilogram of feed, costing 0.004 x 2.20 = USD 0.0088.

19. Why is beta-carotene not a vitamin A source for a cat?

Cats cannot perform the conversion, so they require preformed vitamin A from animal sources. Beta-carotene functions only as a colourant or antioxidant in a cat food.

20. An owner reports her old dog is pacing at night and getting lost indoors. What belongs on your pack?

You cannot support a cognition claim from the verified standards, and behaviour change in an old animal needs veterinary assessment. Referral language builds trust and clinic relationships.

21. What was starch digestibility across the six carbohydrate sources tested in extruded dog diets?

Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent in every diet, which is why the claim that dogs cannot digest grain is not supported.

22. Which two starch sources gave the highest dry matter, organic matter and energy digestibility?

Brewer's rice and cassava flour were highest, followed by maize and sorghum, with pea and lentil diets lowest.

23. Beet pulp at 10 percent inclusion had which effect on stool?

Dogs fed beet pulp had softer stools than those fed cellulose or Miscanthus grass, which is the wrong direction for an older animal that is already loose.

24. What inclusion limits kept faecal scores acceptable in the by-product meal trial?

Digestibility remained acceptable at higher levels, but faecal quality was reduced at 24 percent bone protein meal and 20 percent feather meal, so the practical limits are 12 and 5 percent.

25. Using modified Atwater, a diet at 26 percent protein, 12 percent fat, 3 percent fibre and 45 percent NFE has what gross energy per 100 g?

5.7 x 26 = 148.2, 9.4 x 12 = 112.8, 4.1 x 48 = 196.8, total 457.8 kcal per 100 g. 396 kcal is the digestible energy after applying digestibility.

26. A 24 kg dog scores 8 out of 9. What is the approximate target weight?

Three points above a score of 5 is about 30 percent over target, so target weight is 24 divided by 1.30, which is 18.5 kg.

27. Which weight should the calorie allowance be calculated from?

Calculating from current weight would simply maintain the excess. RER is worked on the target weight and then multiplied by the weight-loss factor.

28. What is the published weight-loss factor for cats?

The feline weight-loss factor is 0.8 times RER, with obesity-prone cats at 1.0. Weight loss in cats must be gradual and supervised.

29. Why must a light food be more nutrient dense per calorie?

Restricting the ration cuts protein, minerals and vitamins in proportion. Holding concentration up is what protects the animal while calories fall.

30. An old cat is losing weight despite eating well. What is the correct action?

Unexplained weight loss is a clinical sign. Selling more food into it delays diagnosis and damages the producer's reputation.

Module 5 capstone

Build and cost a senior product for one real animal in your own town. Step 1: find three dogs or cats aged seven years or older whose owners will let you work with them. For each, record species, breed type, age, sex, neuter status, body weight on a scale, and a body condition score from 1 to 9 using the rib-and-waist check taught in Lesson 6. Step 2: for each animal calculate RER as 70 multiplied by body weight in kilograms to the power 0.75, then apply the multiplier you can defend: for dogs the inactive and obesity-prone row of 1.2 to 1.4, for cats 1.1 to 1.4 at seven to eleven years and 1.1 to 1.6 above eleven years. Write down the range, not a single number, and say in one sentence why it is a range. Step 3: write down what each animal actually eats now, weigh a day's ration on a kitchen scale, and estimate its energy density. Compare that with the calculated requirement and record the gap in kilocalories and in grams. Step 4: draft one senior formulation on paper that meets the adult minima of 18.0 percent crude protein and 5.5 percent fat on a dry matter basis for the dog, or 26.0 percent protein and 9.0 percent fat for the cat, and that stays below the dog phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent dry matter. Price every ingredient at the price you were actually quoted this week, in USD per kilogram, and show the cost of a 50 kilogram batch and the cost per kilogram. Step 5: write the feeding guide for that food for a 5, 10, 20 and 30 kilogram dog or a 3, 4 and 5 kilogram cat, showing the arithmetic. Step 6: write half a page listing every claim you would like to make on the pack, and beside each one write where the evidence comes from. Delete every claim you cannot source. Submit the three animal records, the formulation, the costing, the feeding guide and the claims page.
