1. Vehicle Maintenance and Safety Checks

Before you start any errand or delivery, you must check your vehicle. Whether you use a car, motorcycle, or bicycle, a quick daily inspection prevents breakdowns and accidents. For a car, check tyre pressure when tyres are cold. A typical sedan needs 32-35 PSI. In Nigeria or Kenya, many roads are rough, so under-inflated tyres can burst. Check engine oil with the dipstick: level should be between MIN and MAX. Top up with the correct grade (e.g., 5W-30 for many cars). Check brake fluid, coolant, and windshield washer fluid. Test all lights: headlights, brake lights, indicators. A burnt brake light can cause an accident and a fine. For motorcycles, check chain tension (should have 2-3 cm slack), brake pads, and tyre tread (minimum 1.6 mm). Bicycles: check brakes, tyre pressure (often 40-65 PSI), and chain lubrication. Keep a basic toolkit: spare tyre, jack, wheel spanner, jumper cables, torch, and first aid kit. In many African cities, roadside mechanics are common, but knowing how to change a tyre yourself saves time and money. Every morning, spend 5 minutes on this check. Also, keep your vehicle clean inside and outclients judge your professionalism. If you notice any strange noise or warning light, address it immediately. Don't ignore a check engine light; it could be a simple sensor or a major issue. Regular servicing every 5,000 km or as per manual is essential. For deliveries, a breakdown means late delivery and lost income. So treat your vehicle like your business partner.

2. Navigating Traffic and Using GPS Efficiently

Traffic in African cities like Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or Johannesburg can be brutal. To save time, use GPS apps like Google Maps or Waze. But don't rely blindlythey can be outdated. Before leaving, check the route and estimated time. If possible, avoid peak hours: 7-9 AM and 4-7 PM. For deliveries, start early or late. Use GPS to find alternative routes when traffic is heavy. Waze gives real-time traffic from other users, but in areas with poor data, download offline maps. Learn to read traffic patterns: in many cities, certain roads are one-way in the morning and reverse in the evening. Also, know your area: GPS may not show a new bypass or a closed road. Combine GPS with local knowledge. For multiple stops, plan your route to minimize backtracking. Use apps like Google My Maps to plot all stops and optimize order. Keep your phone charged with a car charger and a power bank. If you use a motorcycle, a phone mount is essential. Always have a backup: a paper map or ask for directions. In areas with no street names, use landmarks: 'the big blue mosque' or 'opposite the market'. When giving your location to a client, share your live location via WhatsApp. That builds trust. Also, be aware of your surroundingsdon't stare at GPS while driving. Pull over to check. For delivery, call the recipient when you are 5 minutes away. This reduces waiting time. Efficient navigation means more deliveries per day and happier clients.

3. Handling Fragile and Perishable Items

Fragile items include glassware, electronics, ceramics, and artwork. Perishable items include food, flowers, and certain chemicals. For fragile items, use proper packaging: bubble wrap, foam peanuts, or newspaper. Place items in a sturdy box with at least 2 inches of padding on all sides. Mark the box 'FRAGILE' in bold letters. In your vehicle, secure the box so it doesn't slide. For a motorcycle, use a delivery box with straps. For perishables, temperature control is key. If delivering fresh fish or meat, use a cooler with ice packs. For cakes, keep them level and away from direct sun. In hot African climates, a car without AC can ruin chocolate or dairy. Plan your route to deliver perishables first. If you have multiple stops, put perishables in a cooler bag with a frozen water bottle. For flowers, wrap stems in wet paper and plastic. For medications that need refrigeration, use a small cooler with a thermometer. Always handle items gentlydon't toss boxes. When picking up, ask the sender about special handling. If an item is very fragile, consider using a professional courier with insurance. But as a PA, you may need to do it yourself. Document the condition before and after: take photos. If damage occurs, you can prove it wasn't your fault. Also, avoid stacking heavy items on top of fragile ones. For perishables, note the time limit: fresh milk spoils in 2 hours without cooling. So plan your trip accordingly. Communicate with the recipient about arrival time so they can receive immediately. Never leave perishables at the door in the sun.

4. Delivery Confirmation and Tracking

Delivery confirmation is proof that you completed the job. Without it, a client may claim non-delivery and refuse to pay. Always get a signature or an OTP (one-time password) from the recipient. For high-value items, take a photo of the recipient holding the item, or a photo of the item at the doorstep with a timestamp. Use apps like Google Maps to share your live location with the client. For tracking, many delivery companies use barcode scanners. As a PA, you can use free tools: WhatsApp live location, or apps like 'Glympse'. When you pick up an item, note the time and condition. Send a message to the client: 'Picked up at 10:15 AM, item is in good condition.' When you deliver, send another message: 'Delivered at 11:30 AM, signed by Mrs. Adeyemi.' Keep a logbook: date, item, pickup location, drop-off location, recipient name, time, and any issues. For multiple deliveries, update the client after each stop. If the recipient is not available, call them. If no answer, wait 10 minutes, then leave a note and inform the client. Never leave an item without confirmation unless instructed. For cash-on-delivery, collect payment and issue a receipt. Use a simple receipt book or a mobile app like 'Receipt Maker'. If you use a motorcycle, a waterproof phone holder is essential. Also, keep your phone charged. In areas with poor network, send an SMS instead of WhatsApp. After delivery, ask the client to confirm receipt via message. This creates a paper trail. Good tracking builds trust and repeat business.

5. Managing Multiple Deliveries in One Trip

When you have several deliveries in one trip, planning is everything. First, list all pickup and drop-off locations. Use a mapping app like Google My Maps to plot them. Then optimize the order: start with the farthest pickup or the one with the earliest deadline. Group deliveries by area. For example, if you have three drops in Ikeja and two in Yaba, do Ikeja first if it's on the way. Consider traffic: use Waze to check live conditions. For perishables, deliver those first. For fragile items, place them on top or in a secure spot. Use a checklist to avoid missing any item. Load your vehicle in reverse order: last delivery goes in first. For a motorcycle, use a delivery box with compartments. Keep a manifest: list each item, recipient, address, and contact number. Call each recipient when you are 10 minutes away. This reduces waiting time. If a recipient is not ready, ask if you can come back later or leave with a neighbor. But never leave without confirmation. For cash-on-delivery, keep a small cash box and change. Track your fuel: multiple stops can use more fuel. In many African cities, parking is a challenge. So plan to park legally and walk short distances. If you have many small items, use a trolley or a large bag. Time yourself: each stop should take no more than 10 minutes. If a stop takes longer, you fall behind. Communicate delays to the client. After each delivery, update your log. At the end of the trip, reconcile all items. Did you deliver everything? Collect all signatures. Return any undelivered items to the office. Efficient multi-delivery saves time, fuel, and increases your daily earnings.

6. Dealing with Parking and Traffic Violations

Parking and traffic violations can cost you money and time. In many African cities, traffic police may ask for bribes. But you can avoid violations by knowing the rules. Always park in designated areas. If you must park on the street, check for signs: no parking zones, loading zones, or time limits. In Lagos, for example, parking on the yellow line is illegal. Use parking apps like 'Parkopedia' to find legal spots. For deliveries, use hazard lights only when briefly stopping, but not for long. If you park illegally, you risk a fine or towing. If stopped by police, stay calm and polite. Ask for their identification and the specific offense. In some countries, you can pay fines at a bank or online. Never pay a bribe if you can avoid itask for a receipt. If you are ticketed, note the date, time, and officer's name. You can contest it if it's wrong. For traffic violations like running a red light or speeding, the fine can be high. In South Africa, a speeding fine can be R500 or more. So drive carefully. Use GPS with speed alerts. Also, keep your vehicle papers updated: driver's license, insurance, roadworthiness, and vehicle registration. In many African countries, police check these frequently. If you don't have them, you can be fined. For motorcycle riders, always wear a helmetit's the law and saves lives. If you are a PA using your own car, consider getting a dashcam. It can prove your innocence in an accident or false accusation. If you get a violation, pay it on time to avoid penalties. Keep a record of all fines for your taxes. Finally, be aware of your surroundingsdon't park in dark or unsafe areas. Your safety is more important than a quick delivery.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Transportation and Delivery Services

In African cities from Lagos and Nairobi to Accra, Kampala, and Kigali the errand and personal assistant business lives or dies on the reliability of its transport. A client does not pay you for a story about traffic; they pay you for a parcel that arrives intact, on time, and confirmed. This practical reference gives you the operating standards, checklists, and data you need to run a transport and delivery arm that clients trust and that turns a profit. Whether you are using a motorbike (boda boda), a tuk-tuk, a saloon car, or a shared van, the same disciplines apply: maintain the vehicle, plan the route, protect the cargo, confirm the delivery, and manage the money.

Lesson 1: Vehicle Maintenance and Safety Checks

In many African markets, breakdowns are the single biggest cause of missed deadlines and lost clients. A boda boda that stalls on the Third Mainland Bridge or a van that fails on Thika Road costs you the client's trust and your reputation. Build a daily and weekly inspection routine.

Business tip: Keep a small spare kit tyre repair plugs, fuses, a basic spanner set, and a phone power bank. In cities where roadside assistance is unreliable, self-recovery is a competitive advantage. Budget 35% of monthly revenue for maintenance; a breakdown on a high-value delivery costs far more.

Lesson 2: Navigating Traffic and Using GPS Efficiently

Traffic in African capitals is not an inconvenience it is a planning variable. Nairobi's peak-hour congestion, Lagos' go-slow, and Accra's Circle traffic can double your travel time. Use GPS not just for directions but as a scheduling tool.

Efficiency habits: Download offline maps for areas with weak data; pin the exact gate or landmark, not just the estate name; share your live location with the client; and batch pickups on the same corridor. For fragile items, avoid the roughest routes even if slightly longer.

Lesson 3: Handling Fragile and Perishable Items

Fragile goods (electronics, glassware, cakes) and perishables (meat, fish, dairy, vaccines, flowers) demand different handling. Perishables need a cold chain; fragile items need cushioning and minimal vibration. In hot African climates, a two-hour delay can spoil a food delivery.

  • Fragile: wrap in bubble wrap or newspaper, use a rigid box, fill gaps, mark "FRAGILE THIS SIDE UP", and never stack heavy items on top.
  • Perishable: use an insulated bag with ice packs, pre-cool the bag, keep it out of direct sun, and deliver within the shortest possible window.
  • Documentation: photograph the item at pickup and at delivery; note any pre-existing damage in writing or on the app.
SAFETY WARNING: Never carry perishable food, medicine, or vaccines in the same compartment as fuel, chemicals, or cleaning agents. Cross-contamination can cause food poisoning or render medicine unsafe. For meat, fish, and dairy, maintain below 5C; for frozen goods, below 18C. If you cannot maintain the cold chain, decline the job rather than risk a client's health and your liability.

Lesson 4: Delivery Confirmation and Tracking

Confirmation is your proof of performance and your trigger for payment. Never mark a delivery "done" without evidence. Use a simple system: a photo of the item at the door or in the recipient's hands, a signature or OTP, and a timestamp. Share a tracking link or live pin with the client so they can see progress without calling you every ten minutes.

Business benefit: Confirmed deliveries reduce disputes, speed up payment, and build a data trail you can use to price future jobs accurately.

Lesson 5: Managing Multiple Deliveries in One Trip

Multi-drop trips are where profit is made or lost. Plan the route so that the most perishable or urgent item is delivered first, and the heaviest last. Group deliveries by area to cut fuel and time. Keep a manifest with recipient names, phone numbers, and payment status. Confirm each drop before moving to the next, and never leave the vehicle unlocked with remaining cargo.

Lesson 6: Dealing with Parking and Traffic Violations

Parking fines and traffic offences eat into margins. In many African cities, parking is informal and enforcement varies. Use designated bays where available, avoid blocking entrances, and keep small change for legal parking fees. If you receive a violation, record it, pay promptly where legitimate, and log it as a business expense. Repeated violations should trigger a review of your routes and loading points.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Complete the daily vehicle safety check before the first trip.
  • Confirm pickup address, recipient name, phone number, and deadline.
  • Pack fragile items with cushioning and perishables with cold packs.
  • Download offline maps and plan around peak traffic windows.
  • Share live location or tracking link with the client.
  • Photograph the item at pickup and at delivery.
  • Obtain signature, OTP, or photo proof before marking delivered.
  • Log fuel, parking, and any fines as business expenses.

Bottom line: In the errand and personal assistant business, transport is not a cost centre it is your product. Maintain the vehicle, plan the route, protect the cargo, confirm the delivery, and record every transaction. Do that consistently, and clients will pay a premium for reliability in markets where reliability is rare.
