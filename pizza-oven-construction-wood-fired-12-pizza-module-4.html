Lessons

1. Building the Dome: Brick-by-Brick Technique

Start by laying a level base of firebricks on your oven floor. For a 12-pizza oven, the internal diameter should be about 80 cm (32 inches). Mark the centre with a nail or a small peg. Use a piece of string tied to the centre to check radius as you build. The dome is built in rings, each ring leaning inward. The first ring sits directly on the floor, using full bricks stood on their sides. Use a rubber mallet to tap bricks into place. For the second ring, cut bricks to fit the curve. A angle grinder with a diamond blade is best for cutting firebricks. Keep the gaps between bricks small, about 2-3 mm. Fill every gap with refractory mortar. As you go up, each ring gets smaller. The bricks should lean in so that the dome curves smoothly. A common mistake is to make the dome too flat; it should be a perfect hemisphere. Check with a straight edge from the centre to the top of each ring. The dome height should be about 40 cm (half the diameter). At the top, you will need a keystone brick or a small circle of bricks to close the dome. Do not use ordinary cement; it will crack at high heat. Work in small sections so the mortar does not dry before you place the next brick. Keep a bucket of water and a sponge to clean excess mortar as you go. After each ring, let the mortar set for at least 30 minutes before continuing. This slow, careful work ensures a strong dome that will not shift when fired.

2. Using a Dome Guide or Sand Mold

A dome guide or sand mold helps you build a perfect curve without guessing. For a sand mold, pile clean river sand in the centre of your oven floor. Shape it into a dome that matches the internal size of your oven: 80 cm diameter, 40 cm high. Pack the sand firmly and smooth it with a trowel. Cover the sand with a layer of wet newspaper or plastic sheet so mortar does not stick. Then lay bricks over the sand, using it as a form. Start at the base and work upward, resting each brick on the sand. The sand supports the bricks until the mortar sets. Once the dome is complete and the mortar is hard (after 24 hours), dig out the sand through the oven mouth. For a wooden dome guide, cut a curved template from plywood that matches the dome profile. Place it inside the oven and build against it. You can also use a metal rod bent to the curve, anchored at the centre. This guide helps you check the angle of each brick. In Africa, sand molds are popular because sand is cheap and available. Make sure the sand is clean and free of stones. After removing the sand, sweep the interior and wipe with a damp cloth. The sand mold method is faster for beginners, but you must be careful not to disturb the bricks when removing the sand. Always let the mortar cure fully before removing the mold.

3. Mixing and Applying Refractory Mortar

Refractory mortar is specially made to withstand high heat. You can buy it as a dry powder or make your own mix. A common homemade recipe is 3 parts fine sand, 1 part Portland cement, and 1 part fireclay. Add water slowly and mix with a hoe or trowel until it is like thick peanut butter. Do not make it too wet; wet mortar weakens the joint. Let the mix sit for 10 minutes, then stir again. Use a small trowel to apply mortar to the sides of each brick. A joint thickness of 2-3 mm is ideal. Too thick a joint will crack. Press the brick into place and wiggle it slightly to spread the mortar. Scrape off excess with the trowel. For the dome, you need mortar that stays workable for about 20 minutes. Mix only what you can use in that time. In hot African climates, mortar dries fast, so keep a spray bottle of water handy to mist the bricks if they dry out. Never use ordinary cement mortar; it will crumble at 500C. Refractory mortar costs more but lasts for years. If you cannot find fireclay, you can use a mixture of clay soil and sand, but test it first by firing a small sample. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when mixing dry powder. After building, clean tools with water before the mortar hardens.

4. Creating the Oven Mouth and Arch

The oven mouth is the opening where you place pizzas and manage the fire. For a 12-pizza oven, the mouth should be about 40 cm wide and 25 cm high. This size allows you to fit a pizza peel and reach the back. Build the mouth using a brick arch. First, make a wooden arch template from plywood. The arch should be a semicircle with a radius of 20 cm. Place the template at the front of the oven. Then lay bricks over the template, using mortar to hold them together. The bricks should be angled so they support each other. Start from the bottom corners and work toward the top. The keystone brick at the centre locks the arch. After the mortar sets (at least 24 hours), remove the template. The arch must be strong enough to support the bricks above the mouth. You may need to use a lintel or a metal bar for extra support. The mouth should be slightly smaller than the dome height to keep heat inside. A common mistake is making the mouth too large, which lets heat escape. Also, build a small landing or shelf in front of the mouth for loading pizzas. Use firebricks for the landing. After the arch is built, smooth the mortar with a damp sponge. Check that the opening is level and the arch is symmetrical. This ensures your pizza peel slides in easily.

5. Ensuring Proper Dome Curvature for Heat Circulation

The dome shape is critical for heat circulation. A properly curved dome reflects heat down onto the pizza, cooking it evenly. The ideal dome is a hemisphere: height equals half the diameter. For an 80 cm diameter, the height should be 40 cm. If the dome is too flat, heat escapes out the mouth. If too tall, the fire is too far from the pizza. To check curvature, use a string from the centre of the floor to the top of the dome. The string should touch the dome at all points. Another method is to use a template cut to the curve. As you build, place the template inside to check the shape. The bricks should lean inward at an angle of about 45 degrees near the top. This creates a smooth curve. The inner surface should be as smooth as possible; rough spots disrupt airflow. After building, you can smooth the interior with a mixture of refractory mortar and water, applied with a brush. Heat circulation works like this: fire at the back, hot air rises, hits the dome, and is pushed down toward the floor and out the mouth. A good dome will heat the floor evenly. Test by firing the oven and checking for hot spots with an infrared thermometer. If one area is cooler, you may need to adjust the dome shape. In Africa, where wood may be scarce, an efficient dome saves fuel. Take your time to get the curve right.

6. Curing the Dome: Gradual Drying Process

Curing is the most important step to prevent cracks. After building, the dome is full of moisture. If you fire it up quickly, steam will build up and crack the mortar. Curing takes about 7 days. Start with a small fire using dry twigs or paper. Keep it burning for 2 hours, then let it cool. Do this once a day for 3 days. The fire should be small, not roaring. On day 4, make a slightly larger fire and burn for 4 hours. On day 5, burn for 6 hours. On day 6, burn for 8 hours. On day 7, you can do a full firing. During curing, watch for steam escaping from the dome. If you see cracks forming, stop and let it cool. You can patch small cracks with refractory mortar. In humid African climates, curing may take longer. Never use water to cool the oven; it will cause thermal shock. After curing, the dome should be dry and hard. Test by firing to pizza temperature (400-450C). The dome should glow slightly. If it does not hold heat, you may need more curing. A well-cured oven will last for decades. Keep a log of your curing fires. This gradual process is the key to a durable oven.

<h2>Practical Reference: Constructing the Oven Dome</h2>
<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single, field-ready guide for building the dome of a wood-fired pizza oven. It is written for artisan bakers, hospitality entrepreneurs, and construction micro-enterprises across Africa who want to add a high-margin pizza offering to their business. A well-built dome is the heart of the oven: it stores heat, radiates it evenly onto the pizza, and determines how many pizzas you can bake per hour and therefore your revenue per firing.</p>

<p>Before you start, confirm your business case. A 12-pizza wood-fired oven (internal floor diameter approximately 90100 cm) can bake 46 pizzas per firing cycle in 6090 seconds each, with residual heat available for bread, roasts, or drying for hours afterwards. In many African markets, a single oven can serve a roadside restaurant, a weekend market stall, or a guesthouse kitchen. The dome you build now will either become an asset that pays for itself in months, or a liability that cracks, smokes, and wastes fuel. This reference helps you build the former.</p>

<h3>Dome Geometry and Heat Circulation</h3>
<p>The dome must be a smooth, continuous curve ideally a catenary or a shallow hemisphere so that heat reflects from the crown down onto the floor and back up the sides. A flat or irregular dome creates hot spots that burn one side of a pizza and leave the other raw. The classic rule for a 12-pizza oven is a dome height of 6065% of the floor diameter. For a 100 cm floor, that means a dome apex of 6065 cm above the floor. The mouth (opening) should be about 63% of the dome height roughly 3841 cm high and 5055 cm wide to balance heat retention with access for a peel.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Dome Construction</h3>
<p><strong>1. Build the dome brick-by-brick.</strong> Use firebricks (alumina content 3040%) laid with thin joints (58 mm) of refractory mortar. Start at the perimeter and work inward in rings, keeping each brick slightly tilted toward the centre so the dome curves smoothly. Tap each brick with a rubber mallet to seat it; never use a steel hammer, which shatters firebrick. Cut bricks with a masonry angle grinder fitted with a diamond blade and always wear eye protection and a dust mask.</p>

<p><strong>2. Use a dome guide or sand mold.</strong> A sand mold is the cheapest and most forgiving method: pile damp sand into the dome shape, pack it firm, cover it with wet newspaper, and lay bricks over it. After the mortar cures, scoop the sand out through the mouth. Alternatively, build a plywood or steel guide rib that you rotate as you lay each ring. The sand method is preferred in many African workshops because sand is locally available and the mold doubles as a support that prevents bricks from sliding before the mortar sets.</p>

<p><strong>3. Mix and apply refractory mortar.</strong> A reliable field mix is 3 parts clean river sand, 1 part Portland cement, and 1 part fireclay (or 1 part hydrated lime for better plasticity). Add water slowly until the mortar holds a ball but is still workable. Mix only what you can use in 3040 minutes. Butter both faces of each brick and press firmly; wipe excess mortar immediately with a damp sponge. Keep joints thin thick joints are the number-one cause of cracking.</p>

<p><strong>4. Create the oven mouth and arch.</strong> The mouth is the thermal bottleneck of the oven. Build a semicircular arch over the opening using a wooden template or a steel form. The arch must be self-supporting: each voussoir (arch brick) should be wedge-shaped and bedded with mortar that is still slightly soft when the form is removed after 2448 hours. Line the mouth with a steel or cast-iron door that can be closed during heating and partially opened during baking.</p>

<p><strong>5. Ensure proper dome curvature.</strong> Check the curve with a template or a string tied to the centre point. Every ring should be concentric and level. A dome that is too flat loses heat through the top; one that is too steep creates a narrow baking zone and forces you to use more fuel. Aim for a smooth catenary that rises evenly from the floor to the apex.</p>

<p><strong>6. Cure the dome gradually.</strong> This is the step most builders rush and the one that decides whether the oven lasts five years or five months. After the mortar has set (2448 hours), build a small fire and keep it low for 23 hours. Increase the fire slightly each day for 57 days. Trapped moisture turns to steam and, if released too fast, cracks the dome. In humid or rainy-season conditions, extend curing to 1014 days.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING FIRE, BURNS, AND SILICA DUST</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px;color:#7f1d1d;">
<li><strong>Never</strong> light a full fire in a green (uncured) oven. Steam pressure can explode brickwork and throw hot fragments.</li>
<li>Cutting firebrick releases crystalline silica. Wear a fitted N95/FFP2 mask, safety goggles, and gloves. Cut outdoors or with water suppression.</li>
<li>Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand within 3 metres of the oven at all times.</li>
<li>The oven exterior can exceed 200C. Build a minimum 10 cm insulating layer (vermiculite, perlite, or ceramic fibre) and keep children and customers at least 1 metre away during firing.</li>
<li>In shared or market settings, secure the oven against tipping and anchor it to a concrete base wind and crowding are real risks.</li>

<h3>Business Notes for African Operators</h3>
<p>Build the dome with locally available materials wherever possible: river sand, fireclay from brick suppliers, and firebricks from regional kilns. In East Africa, firebricks are commonly sourced from Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania; in West Africa, from Nigeria and Ghana. Imported refractory bricks cost 35 times more, so reserve them for the floor and the hottest part of the dome if budget is tight. A well-built 12-pizza oven can be constructed for a fraction of the cost of an imported unit, and the skills you gain become a sellable service to other food businesses.</p>

<p>Track your economics: a cured oven that bakes 40 pizzas per day at an average margin of $2$4 per pizza can generate $80$160 daily. The dome is the engine of that revenue. Treat the curing schedule as an investment, not a delay.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Verify floor diameter (90100 cm) and dome height (6065 cm) before laying the first ring.</li>
<li> Use firebricks and thin (58 mm) refractory mortar joints throughout.</li>
<li> Build the sand mold or dome guide and check concentric rings with a template.</li>
<li> Form the mouth to 3841 cm high and 5055 cm wide with a self-supporting arch.</li>
<li> Wear N95 mask, goggles, and gloves when cutting brick; keep a fire extinguisher nearby.</li>
<li> Cure gradually over 714 days with increasing fire size never rush.</li>
<li> Apply 10 cm of vermiculite or perlite insulation before the first commercial firing.</li>
<li> Record fuel use and bake times to calculate cost per pizza and payback period.</li>
</ul>
