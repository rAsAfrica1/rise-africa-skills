Lesson 1

The IPM Mindset — Prevention First, Spray Last

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Order the five lines of IPM
  • Understand why spraying first makes everything worse
  • Set the discipline of scouting before spraying

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is not a soft alternative to spraying. It is the only approach that works over a full season. It has five lines, used in order:

  1. Resistant varieties. The single best defence. Buy seed with resistance ratings for the diseases that hit your area. Cost is often only 20–40% more than non-resistant seed, and saves 50%+ of a season's spray costs.
  2. Cultural practices. Rotation, sanitation (removing diseased plants), spacing for airflow, mulching, staking, drip irrigation (keeps foliage dry), clean seed, and destroying crop residues after harvest.
  3. Biological control. Encourage natural enemies (ladybirds, parasitic wasps, spiders, lacewings). Some biological products — Trichoderma, Bacillus subtilis, Bt — are effective against specific problems and safe.
  4. Monitoring. Scout the plot weekly. Identify the problem before spraying. Spraying a fungicide for a bacterial disease does nothing and wastes money.
  5. Chemical control — last, targeted, correct. Only when (a) scouting confirms a problem above threshold, (b) the correct product is chosen for the specific pest or disease, (c) the label rate is followed, and (d) the pre-harvest interval is respected.

Most smallholder tomato growers spray on a fixed weekly schedule regardless of what is in the plot. This selects resistant pests, destroys natural enemies, kills pollinators, exposes workers to chemicals, and rarely solves the actual problem. Scouting first changes everything.

Lesson 2

Early and Late Blight

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish early blight from late blight by symptom
  • Understand the weather conditions that trigger each
  • Use cultural + chemical control correctly

Early blight (Alternaria solani)

Brown-black spots with concentric rings (target pattern) on lower leaves first. Spots enlarge, leaves yellow, then die. Fruit develops dark sunken spots at the stem end. Worse in warm, humid weather. Spreads by splashing water, wind, and on crop residue.

Late blight (Phytophthora infestans)

Water-soaked grey-green patches that rapidly turn brown-black. White fuzzy growth on the underside in humid conditions. Stems and fruit affected. Fruit develops firm brown patches. Explosive in cool, wet weather — can kill a plot in a week.

Control for both

  1. Resistant varieties where available
  2. Remove lower leaves touching the soil to improve airflow
  3. Water at the base — drip is ideal; overhead sprinkler encourages blight
  4. Mulch to prevent soil splash onto leaves
  5. Remove infected leaves immediately, bag and destroy (do not compost)
  6. Rotate away from tomato, potato, pepper for at least 2 seasons
  7. Spray preventively in high-risk weather (cool, wet, humid). Copper hydroxide, chlorothalonil, or mancozeb are common. Rotate actives to prevent resistance.

Lesson 3

Bacterial Wilt and Fusarium Wilt

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish bacterial wilt from Fusarium wilt by the stem cut test
  • Understand why wilt is a soil problem, not a leaf problem
  • Use rotation, resistant varieties and grafting to manage it

Symptoms — both look similar

Sudden wilting of the whole plant, often starting on one side. Leaves stay green at first, then yellow and dry. Plant dies within 1–2 weeks. Often appears in patches in the field and spreads along rows.

The stem cut test

Cut the stem 10 cm above the soil. Suspend the cut stem in a glass of clean water for 5 minutes.

  • Bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum): a milky white stream of bacteria oozes from the cut stem into the water.
  • Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum): no bacterial ooze. Internal tissue shows brown discolouration of the vascular ring.

Control — both are soil-borne and permanent in the field

  1. Resistant varieties. The most reliable defence. Look for "BW" or "F" resistance in the variety description.
  2. Rotation. At least 3–4 seasons away from tomato, potato, pepper, eggplant. Rotate with cereals, legumes, or leafy greens.
  3. Raised beds and good drainage. Both pathogens thrive in waterlogged soils.
  4. Grafting. Grafting a susceptible tomato variety onto a resistant rootstock is standard practice in commercial tomato, and effective against bacterial wilt. Requires skill and rootstock seed.
  5. Soil solarisation. Cover wet soil with clear plastic for 4–6 weeks in hot weather. Kills pathogens in the top 20 cm.
  6. Do not compost infected plants. Bag and burn.
Lesson 4

Whitefly and Tuta absoluta

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Identify whitefly and Tuta absoluta damage
  • Use traps, biological control, and targeted sprays
  • Understand Tuta's resistance to pesticides — and what to do about it

Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci)

Small white flies on the underside of leaves. They suck sap, excrete honeydew (sticky), and transmit viruses — including Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV), which has no cure and destroys the crop.

Control:

  • Yellow sticky traps at 20–40 traps per hectare to monitor and reduce populations
  • Reflective silver mulch repels whitefly
  • Neem oil or insecticidal soap sprays (targeting the underside of leaves, applied in the evening)
  • Encourage natural enemies (Encarsia wasps, predatory bugs)
  • Resistant varieties (TYLCV-resistant tomato)
  • Imidacloprid or thiamethoxam as last resort — but whitefly develop resistance quickly, so rotate actives

Tuta absoluta (tomato leafminer)

The most destructive tomato pest of the last 15 years in Africa. The larva mines the leaves, then bores into the fruit and stem. Damage can reach 80–100% of the crop if unmanaged. The adult is a small grey-brown moth.

Control:

  • Pheromone traps (4–10 per hectare) — monitor male flight and reduce mating
  • Mass trapping with pheromone traps (20–40 per hectare) — reduces population
  • Biological — Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) sprayed evening, weekly; Nesidiocoris tenuis is a predatory bug that feeds on Tuta eggs and larvae
  • Remove and destroy infected leaves and fruit — bag and burn
  • Rotate crops away from tomato and potato
  • Chemical — Tuta is resistant to many pyrethroids and organophosphates. Effective actives include chlorantraniliprole, spinosad, emamectin, and Bacillus thuringiensis. Rotate actives every 2–3 sprays. Never spray the same active twice in a row.

Lesson 5

The Weekly Scouting Routine and Spray Calendar

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Set up a 15-minute weekly scouting routine
  • Decide when spraying is justified and when it is not
  • Respect pre-harvest intervals to keep fruit saleable

Scouting routine (15 minutes per 1,000 m² per week)

  1. Walk a W pattern through the plot
  2. At 10 points, stop and check 5 plants: look at the top leaves, the underside of leaves, and near the soil line
  3. Record: number of whitefly, presence of Tuta mines, any wilted plants, any blight lesions, any blossom-end rot
  4. If a problem is present, identify it correctly. Photo it if unsure. Ask your extension officer or a WhatsApp group.
  5. Spray only if the problem is above threshold or if the crop is in a high-risk weather window

Typical spray calendar

StagePreventive action
Weeks 1–3 (establishment)Copper spray if wet weather; check for cutworms
Weeks 4–6 (vegetative)Bt for Tuta if mines appear; neem for whitefly if needed; remove lower leaves
Weeks 7–10 (flowering/fruit)Calcium foliar every 2 weeks; copper if blight risk; Bt weekly if Tuta pressure
Weeks 11–14 (harvest)Only products with a short pre-harvest interval (PHI)

Pre-harvest intervals

Every pesticide has a PHI — the days between the last spray and the harvest that can be sold. Respecting the PHI is a legal obligation and a commercial one: buyers increasingly test for residues, and rejected loads are uninsurable. Keep a spray log: date, product, rate, PHI, expected safe harvest date.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. In IPM, what is the correct order of response?

Correct: prevent, monitor, then target. Spraying first makes pests resistant and destroys natural enemies.

2. What distinguishes bacterial wilt from Fusarium wilt?

Correct: milky ooze from the cut stem. The stem cut test is definitive.

3. What triggers late blight?

Correct: cool, wet weather. 15–22 °C, high humidity. Can kill a plot in a week.

4. Why is Tuta absoluta so hard to control chemically?

Correct: resistance develops quickly. Rotate actives every 2–3 sprays; combine with pheromone traps and Bt.

5. What is a pre-harvest interval (PHI)?

Correct: minimum days between last spray and sale. Legal and commercial obligation.

Module 4 capstone

Draw up your pest and disease plan for the next tomato crop. Include: (1) the three main threats in your area based on last season; (2) your resistant varieties; (3) your rotation plan; (4) your scouting schedule and what you will look for; (5) your spray products, rates, PHIs, and rotation plan. Keep a spray log for the whole season.
