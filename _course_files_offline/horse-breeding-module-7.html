7. Farriery and Hoof Care

No foot, no horse. This is the oldest rule in the industry, and it is true.

Module 7 of 12 · Horse Breeding for Profit

Why hooves matter more than anything

A horse carries 60-70% of its weight on its front feet. Each hoof takes the full impact of every stride. A horse taking 8,000 strides per day is putting enormous stress on four small structures. When they fail, the horse is lame - and a lame horse has no value.

Daily hoof care

  1. Pick out feet daily - morning and after work. Remove stones, mud, manure.
  2. Check for heat - run your hand down the hoof wall and across the sole. Heat means inflammation.
  3. Check digital pulse - feel the artery behind the fetlock. A strong pulse means trouble.
  4. Check for cracks, splits, or wounds.
  5. Look at the frog - should be firm and black. Soft, smelly = thrush.
  6. Check for loose shoes - a risen clench means the shoe is about to come off.

A balanced diet plus a biotin supplement is the best support for hoof health. Improvements take 6-12 months to show - hooves grow slowly.

Environment for hoof health

  • Dry bedding. Wet bedding causes thrush and soft hooves.
  • Regular movement. Circulation in the hoof depends on movement. Horses in stalls 24/7 have poor hooves.
  • Varied surfaces. Grass, gravel, and hard ground - all help natural wear.
  • Mud management. Rotate paddocks, use hard standings around gates and troughs.
  • Avoid standing water. Horses that stand in water develop soft, weak hooves.

Working with a farrier

A good farrier is worth their weight in gold. Find one who:

  • Is trained and certified (where certification exists).
  • Shows up on time and communicates clearly.
  • Explains what they are doing and why.
  • Trims to natural balance, not fashion.
  • Adjusts to your horse's needs, not a one-size-fits-all.
  • Can recognise lameness and refer to a vet.

Book farrier visits every 6-8 weeks for a whole year in advance. Slotting in urgently is difficult.

The 4-week check

Between farrier visits, check every horse's hooves weekly:

  1. Pick out and inspect all four.
  2. Look for cracks, splits, or separation.
  3. Feel for heat.
  4. Check digital pulse.
  5. Look at the frog for thrush.
  6. Watch the horse walk on hard ground.
  7. Note anything unusual and tell the farrier next visit.
The farrier rule: Book farrier visits six months in advance. A missed trim leads to hoof problems, and hoof problems lead to lameness. Lameness costs far more than the farrier ever will.
Practical exercise 7: Pick out all four feet of 3 horses. Check for heat, digital pulse, and thrush. Book the next 3 farrier visits with your farrier.
