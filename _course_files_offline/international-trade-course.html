Practical Reference

The module so far has covered the fundamentals of poultry production as a business. This Practical Reference section converts that knowledge into operational tools you can use on the farm, in the feed store, and in the bank manager's office. Every figure below is drawn from published, verifiable sources so you can cite them in loan applications, grant proposals, or investor decks without losing credibility. Rise AFRICA's approach is simple: treat the flock as a factory, the feed as raw material, and every egg or bird as a unit of revenue. The numbers that follow are your benchmarks.

Read the chart as a cash-flow map. The steep climb between weeks 18 and 30 is where your feed investment converts into peak revenue; this is the window where a single day of poor management (heat stress, water outage, disease challenge) costs the most. The slow decline after week 45 is normal, but if mortality accelerates above the dashed line, you are losing money every day. Cull aggressively, review biosecurity, and check your feed quality before blaming the breed.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Chemicals, Drugs and Food Safety

This module touches on veterinary drugs, disinfectants and feed additives. Handle all of them as hazardous materials.

  • Antibiotics: Never use antibiotic growth promoters or treat birds with human-critical antibiotics (e.g. colistin, fluoroquinolones) for routine prevention. This drives antimicrobial resistance and can make your eggs and meat unsellable to formal buyers. Follow national veterinary drug regulations and observe the full withdrawal period before slaughter or egg collection.
  • Coccidiostats and feed additives: Use only products registered in your country. Overdosing causes residues; underdosing causes resistance. Weigh carefully â do not "eyeball" doses.
  • Disinfectants (formalin, glutaraldehyde, chlorine, quaternary ammonium): Wear gloves, eye protection and a respirator when fumigating or spraying. Formalin is a confirmed carcinogen â never fumigate hatcheries or stores while people or birds are present. Ventilate thoroughly before re-entry.
  • Aflatoxin risk: Never feed mouldy maize, groundnut cake or cottonseed cake. Aflatoxin B1 causes liver cancer in humans and stunted growth in birds. Store feed on pallets, keep it dry, and use a binder if your local maize is known to be contaminated.
  • Zoonoses: Salmonella and avian influenza can pass to humans. Wash hands after handling birds, cook poultry and eggs thoroughly, and report sudden mass deaths to veterinary authorities immediately â do not sell or consume the carcasses.
  • Personal protective equipment: Boots, overalls and gloves are a business cost, not a luxury. They protect your workers, your family and your market reputation.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh your feed, don't guess it. Use a hanging scale or calibrated scoop for every batch. A 5% overfeed on a 500-bird flock wastes roughly USD 75 per cycle â pure lost profit.
  2. Track FCR weekly. Record total feed consumed and total live weight (or egg output) every 7 days. If FCR drifts more than 0.15 above your target, investigate feed quality, disease and ventilation before changing anything else.
  3. Keep a daily mortality and water-intake log. Water intake is the earliest warning sign of disease or heat stress. A sudden 20% drop in drinking is a red flag â act the same day.
  4. Vaccinate on schedule and keep cold chain intact. Newcastle, Gumboro (IBD), Marek's and fowl pox vaccines lose potency if the cold chain breaks. Buy from licensed agro-vets and use within the stated window.
  5. Observe drug withdrawal periods without exception. Write the withdrawal date on the pen wall. Selling eggs or birds with residues destroys buyer trust and may be illegal.
  6. Separate your enterprise accounts. Open a dedicated mobile-money or bank account for the poultry business. Mixing household and farm cash is the number-one cause of "profitable" farms that never have money.
  7. Negotiate feed and chick prices quarterly. Bring your FCR and volume data to the table. A USD 0.05/kg feed reduction on 3,000 kg is USD 150 â often more than a month's profit on a small flock.
  8. Build one buyer relationship before you scale. A signed supply agreement with a hotel, school, supermarket or processor is worth more than an extra 200 birds. Lock in demand, then grow supply.
