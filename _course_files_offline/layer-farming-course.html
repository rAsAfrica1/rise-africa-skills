Layer Farming for Profit

A well-managed layer flock produces income every single day for 12-14 months. Eggs are the most reliable cash flow in smallholder livestock - but only if feed cost is controlled and disease is kept out.

Home · All courses · Layer Farming for Profit

What this course covers

  • How to calculate the real economics of a 100-layer flock before you buy chicks
  • How to house and equip layers for maximum lay rate and minimum disease
  • How to raise pullets from day-old to point of lay at 18 weeks
  • How to feed layers for the best feed cost per egg
  • How to vaccinate and keep your flock disease-free
  • How to collect, grade, store, price and sell eggs for the best price

Modules

  • Module 11. The Layer Business
  • Module 22. Housing and Equipment for Layers
  • Module 33. Raising Pullets to Point of Lay
  • Module 44. Feeding Layers for Maximum Eggs
  • Module 55. Health, Vaccination and Egg Quality
  • Module 66. Eggs: Collection, Grading and Selling

Who this course is for

  • Smallholders starting a first layer flock on 100-500 birds
  • Farmers whose current flock is laying below 70% and losing money
  • Groups setting up a collective egg enterprise for local markets
  • Anyone wanting daily cash flow from livestock
  • Agripreneurs supplying schools, hotels and shops with eggs

What you will need

  • A small house or the ability to build one (4m x 4m holds 100 layers)
  • A reliable point-of-lay pullet or day-old chick supplier
  • A layer mash feed supplier
  • Daily time - 20 minutes for 100 birds, 45-60 minutes for 500
  • Starting capital of 700-1,500 USD for a 100-layer flock to first egg

â  SAFETY WARNING â HEALTH, FEED AND CHEMICALS

  • Withdraw all medication before slaughter or egg sale. Observe the full withdrawal period on every drug label â commonly 5â7 days for many antibiotics and up to 28 days for some coccidiostats. Eggs sold during withdrawal can leave drug residues that make buyers sick and can get you banned from formal markets.
  • Never use human antibiotics or chloramphenicol, nitrofurans or arsenic-based growth promoters in layers. These are banned for food-producing animals in most African countries and in all export markets. Residues are not destroyed by cooking.
  • Store vaccines and veterinary drugs cold and locked away. Keep them out of reach of children, away from feed and food, and never re-use a needle between birds.
  • Wear gloves, boots and a mask when handling disinfectants, formalin, or fumigants. Formalin vapour is a respiratory carcinogen â ventilate fully before entering a fumigated house.
  • Salmonella risk is real. Wash hands after handling birds, litter and eggs. Never wash eggs in dirty water and then store them â you push bacteria through the shell. Sell cracked eggs only after cooking, or discard them.
  • Aflatoxin kills flocks and buyers. Reject mouldy, damp or discoloured maize and groundnut cake. Store all feed on pallets in a dry, ventilated room and use it within 4 weeks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate before you buy. Write down your local chick price, feed price and egg price. If feed cost per egg is above 60% of your egg selling price, do not start.
  2. Build the house first. 4m x 4m for 100 layers, with 1 feeder and 1 drinker per 25 birds, 25cm of perch space per bird, and a solid roof that blocks midday sun.
  3. Vaccinate on a written schedule. Marek's at day-old (hatchery), Newcastle at day 7, 21 and 42, Gumboro at 14 and 21, Fowl Typhoid and Fowl Pox before point of lay. Record every date and batch number.
  4. Feed to the curve. 100â110g per bird per day at point of lay, rising to 115â120g at peak, and never drop below 105g while birds are still laying above 60%.
  5. Weigh your feed, do not guess. Use the digital scale. A 5g per bird error on 100 birds wastes 18kg of feed a month â that is real money.
  6. Count eggs daily. Write the number on the wall. A sudden 5% drop in 48 hours means heat, water shortage, disease or a feed problem. Act the same day.
  7. Collect eggs twice a day minimum. Three times in hot weather. Store at 15â20Â°C in clean trays, pointed end down, and rotate stock oldest-first.
  8. Decide the cull date in advance. When lay drops below 60% and feed cost per egg exceeds the egg price, sell the flock and restock with new pullets. Do not keep unprofitable birds out of sentiment.
