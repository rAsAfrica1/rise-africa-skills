â  SAFETY WARNING â Health, Food and Chemical Handling
  • Never use veterinary drugs off-label. Antibiotics, coccidiostats, and dewormers must be used only under the direction of a qualified animal health professional, and only at the dose and withdrawal period stated on the label. Selling meat with drug residues is illegal in most African markets and destroys buyer trust permanently.
  • Respect withdrawal periods before slaughter. If a rabbit has received any medication, record the date and do not slaughter or sell until the full withdrawal period has passed. Keep a simple written treatment log â buyers and regulators increasingly ask for it.
  • Handle manure and urine with gloves. Rabbit waste can harbour zoonotic organisms. Compost manure for at least 30 days before applying it to vegetable beds, and never apply fresh manure directly to crops you will eat raw.
  • Store chemicals away from feed. Disinfectants, pesticides, and rodenticides must be locked in a separate cabinet, never in the feed store. Rodenticide bait stations must be placed where rabbits cannot reach them.
  • Slaughter hygiene. Use clean water, a dedicated knife, and a clean surface. Wash hands and equipment with soap and disinfectant after handling carcasses. Chill meat promptly â do not leave carcasses in ambient heat for more than two hours.
  • Predator and pest control chemicals. If you use acaricides or insecticides around the rabbitry, ventilate the area and remove animals during application. Rabbits are highly sensitive to fumes and can die from respiratory exposure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm three buyers before you buy stock. Call three restaurants, butcheries, hotels, or supermarkets and ask what price they pay per kilogram and how often they need supply. A farm without a confirmed buyer is a hobby.
  2. Price your inputs locally. Get written quotes for breeding stock, cages or cage materials, and pelleted feed from at least three suppliers. Feed is 55â65% of your recurring cost â it decides your margin.
  3. Match breed to market. Californian and New Zealand White for meat; local or crossbreeds for heat tolerance and hardiness. Do not buy expensive purebreds before you have a buyer who pays a premium for them.
  4. Build for heat and predators. Shade, cross-ventilation, and a roof that reflects heat. Mesh floors and raised cages to keep rabbits off damp ground and out of reach of snakes, dogs, and rats.
  5. Keep a breeding calendar. Record mating date, palpation result, kindling date, litter size, and weaning date for every doe. Track does that repeatedly produce fewer than six kits â cull or replace them.
  6. Weigh feed, don't guess. A mature doe needs roughly 120â180 g of pellets plus forage per day, plus free-choice water. Overfeeding wastes money; underfeeding delays market weight and stretches your payback period.
  7. Separate grow-outs by age and sex. Mixing ages spreads disease; mixing sexes after 10 weeks leads to unwanted pregnancies and fighting. Group by weight and move to market on schedule.
  8. Record every shilling and every kilogram. A simple notebook with daily feed used, deaths, and sales will tell you within one cycle whether the business is working. Reinvest the first two cycles' profit into cages and breeding stock.
