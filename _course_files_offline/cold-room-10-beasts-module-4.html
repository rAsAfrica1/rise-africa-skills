Module 4

Refrigeration

Sizing the unit, choosing the type, and installing it so it survives the load it will actually carry — not the load the seller quoted.

Read time: 30 minutesThen: Module 5 — The Room Inside
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Calculate the heat load of your cold room from first principles
  • Choose between monoblock, split and remote condensing units on evidence
  • Read a refrigeration unit's rated capacity and know what it actually means at your conditions
  • Size the unit with an honest safety margin — not too small, not wasteful
  • Install the unit so it drains correctly, defrosts correctly and lasts
  • Produce a written refrigeration specification for your cold room

Lesson 4.1

What the refrigeration unit must do

Refrigeration is not cooling. It is moving heat. The unit does not "make cold". It absorbs heat from the air inside the cold room and rejects that heat into the air outside. Every kilogram of heat that enters the room — through the walls, the door, the floor, the carcasses, the people, the lights — must be picked up by the evaporator and dumped by the condenser to the outside world.

The unit is therefore sized on the total heat gain of the room per hour. That number, in watts or kilowatts, is the refrigeration load. Everything else follows.

The five heat sources

  1. Transmission through the envelope. Heat conduction through walls, ceiling, floor and door. Depends on panel thickness, area and the temperature difference across each surface.
  2. Infiltration through door openings. Every time the door opens, warm air enters. Depends on door size, opening frequency, and whether there is a strip curtain.
  3. Product load. The heat the carcasses bring in with them. A warm 250 kg carcass at 38°C must be cooled to 2°C. That is a real heat load and it is often the largest single item on the list on kill day.
  4. Internal heat gains. Lights, fans, people working inside the room. Small individually, but they add up.
  5. Defrost load. Heat added during defrost cycles. The unit has to remove the heat it just added during the next cooling cycle.

Doing the arithmetic honestly

Full heat load calculation is an engineering exercise. For a 10-beast room, the following simplified method gives you a working number within about 20% of the real load, which is the margin an honest specification carries anyway:

Transmission, walls and ceilingPanel thickness in mm determines U-value; multiply U × area × ΔTU ≈ 0.030 / thickness(m). So 100 mm panel U ≈ 0.30 W/m²·K

A worked example — 10-beast room, tropical climate

Internal dimensions 5.5 × 3.0 × 2.7 m, panel 100 mm PUR, 10 carcasses at 250 kg each, killed at 38°C, room held at 2°C, outside 32°C, door opens 20 times per day.

The unit must therefore have a rated capacity of at least 4.5–5.5 kW at your operating conditions. On days with no new product, the load drops to under 2 kW.

The rule: size the unit for the kill-day load, not the average. A unit sized for average load will be overrun every kill day and will fail prematurely. Add a 25–40% safety margin over the calculated peak.

Lesson 4.2

The three unit types, compared honestly

MonoblockEverything in one unit — compressor, evaporator, condenser, controls. Mounted through a wall.Simple to install, no refrigerant pipework on site, moveable later, low install cost, one enclosure to protectCondenser sits close to the room, in warm air, reducing efficiency. Not serviceable in the field — the whole unit goes back to a workshop for repair.

Compressor away from the food.Requires a qualified refrigeration technician to install and charge. More install cost. Lines must be kept short and properly insulated.
Remote condensing unitEvaporator inside; compressor and condenser in a separate, weatherproof housing anywhere on site. Long pipe runs allowed.Best efficiency. Compressor away from noise and heat. Can serve multiple rooms.Highest install cost. Requires skilled technician. Long pipe runs need careful sizing.

Which one for a 10-beast room?

Split system is the correct choice for most rural African cold rooms. The reasons:

  1. Efficiency. Rejecting heat to outside air rather than back into the room's vicinity keeps the compressor cooler and reduces running cost by 10–25%.
  2. Serviceability. If the compressor fails, it can be replaced in the field. A monoblock must be removed and shipped back to a workshop — a week of downtime minimum.
  3. Availability. Split refrigeration is what air-conditioning technicians are trained on across Africa. Monoblocks in cold-room sizes are specialist.

Monoblocks are worth considering for a small room (5–8 m³) where the wall is thick, the site is confined, and you want a unit you can move later. For a 10-beast room, they are usually the wrong choice.

Do not install a split system without a qualified refrigeration technician. Refrigerant handling, vacuum pumping, pressure testing and charging are all skills that cannot be learned from a book, and getting them wrong either kills the compressor on day one or releases refrigerant that damages the environment.

Lesson 4.3

Reading the rating on the box

Suppliers quote a "capacity" for a refrigeration unit. That number is measured under specific conditions, and those conditions are almost never your conditions. Understanding this prevents the most common mistake in small cold room construction — installing a unit that is rated correctly and yet cannot do the job.

What the rated capacity actually means

Standard ratings are given at a specified evaporating temperature and a specified condensing temperature. For a room held at 2°C, the evaporating temperature is typically around −5°C to −8°C. For a condenser rejecting heat to 32°C air, the condensing temperature is around 45°C.

The rated capacity is at those specific figures. If your room is hotter, or your outside air is hotter, or your pipe runs are long, the actual capacity is less than the rating. How much less depends on the application.

The three ways capacity shrinks from the rating

ConditionEffect on capacity
Higher outside temperature (condenser works harder)−1 to −2% per °C above rated condition
Lower internal temperature (evaporator colder, compressor works harder)−3 to −5% per °C below rated condition
Long pipe runs (over 10 m) with poor insulation or small diameter−5 to −15%, plus additional refrigerant charge needed

The practical rule

Order a unit whose rated capacity is 1.5× to 2× your calculated peak load. That may sound like a lot, but:

  • Standard ratings are for temperate conditions; tropical conditions reduce the achieved capacity by 10–25%.
  • Calculated loads carry uncertainty; a 20–30% margin is honest engineering, not a waste.
  • The unit will run less often, cycle longer at low load, and last longer than a unit sized exactly at the load.

If the calculated load is 4 kW, order a unit rated at 6–7 kW. It will not consume 6–7 kW of power continuously — it consumes power proportional to the actual heat removed. It just means it does not run flat-out, does not short-cycle, and does not fail.

Short-cycling — the failure mode nobody mentions

A unit that is undersized runs continuously and cannot hold temperature. A unit that is massively oversized is also a problem: it cools the room quickly, then the thermostat cuts it out, and it starts again a few minutes later. Short cycling:

  • Wears out the compressor contactor and the compressor itself
  • Prevents proper defrost of the evaporator coil, causing ice build-up
  • Lowers efficiency because the compressor never reaches steady state

The sweet spot is a unit that runs 60–80% of the time on a typical day, and 100% of the time on a kill day, but which can also idle for periods in cool weather without short-cycling. A unit rated at 1.5× to 2× your peak load meets all three.

Lesson 4.4

Installation — where the unit is placed and why

Two identical units, installed by different technicians, can have lives that differ by a factor of three. Installation is not just connecting pipes. It is the layout decisions that decide how the unit performs and how long it lasts.

Evaporator placement, inside the room

  • Never blow cold air directly onto carcasses. Direct airflow on meat causes surface drying, a dark dry crust ("case hardening") and shortens the shelf life. Aim the airflow up and out, or install a fabric air duct if the room is long.
  • Do not place the evaporator directly above the door. Every door opening dumps warm humid air straight onto the coil, causing rapid ice build-up.
  • Leave clearance for service. The evaporator fan and coil need to be reached for cleaning. Leave at least 300 mm free on the service side.
  • Place the unit so the defrost water drains to the room drain. Do not let defrost water fall onto a carcass, a rail, or the floor without a route to the drain.

Condenser placement, outside the room

  • Never in full afternoon sun. A condenser in the sun at 40°C is rejecting heat to the wrong temperature. Place on the shaded side of the building, under a shade roof, or under an awning.
  • Good airflow around and through the coil. Minimum 500 mm clearance on the air-intake side, and no recirculation of its own hot air.
  • Not in a confined corner. The condenser needs to reject a large amount of heat. A courtyard with no through-flow is a bad location.
  • Off the ground, on a solid base. Flood, mud and animals destroy condensers. Mount at least 300 mm above ground on a concrete pad or brackets.
  • Accessible for service. The technician has to get to the compressor, fan and controls. Leave space for this — you will regret saving 30 cm.

Refrigerant lines

  • Keep runs as short as possible. Under 10 m ideally; under 20 m absolute maximum without specialist design.
  • Insulate the suction line fully. A bare suction line condenses, drips, and loses efficiency. Insulation thickness should match the pipe diameter.
  • Use the correct pipe diameter. Undersized pipe adds pressure drop and reduces capacity. Use the manufacturer's tables, not an estimate.
  • Pressure-test and vacuum before charging. Never charge a system that has not been evacuated. Moisture in the system forms acid and destroys the compressor from inside.
  • Never top up refrigerant without finding the leak. A system that loses refrigerant has a leak. Adding refrigerant just delays the failure and dumps more gas into the atmosphere.
The most common installation failure: a condenser in the sun with 200 mm clearance on two sides, in a courtyard with no airflow, connected by undersized pipe, charged without evacuation. The unit works for three months and fails on the first really hot day of the following season. It was never a refrigeration problem. It was an installation problem.

Lesson 4.5

Controls, defrost and the temperature log

The controller

A modern refrigeration unit is controlled by a digital thermostat with a temperature display. The minimum you want:

  • Setpoint and hysteresis. Room temperature target (2°C for chilled) and how far above target before the compressor starts (usually 2–3°C hysteresis).
  • Defrost timer. The evaporator must be defrosted periodically — off-cycle, electric, or hot-gas. Without defrost, the coil ices up and capacity collapses.
  • Alarm output. A simple audible alarm when the room exceeds a safe temperature. This is what saves the meat when the door is left open or the compressor fails at 3 a.m.
  • Remote display. A display outside the door shows the internal temperature without having to open the room. Cheap and valuable.

Defrost — the type matters

TypeHow it worksBest for
Off-cycleCompressor stops; the fan keeps running to melt the light frost.Rooms held above 2°C with light frost. Simple and reliable.
ElectricA heating element in the coil runs on a timer to melt the ice.Rooms with moderate humidity and regular door openings. Standard for cold rooms.
Hot-gasHot refrigerant is routed back through the coil to melt ice.Larger systems; more efficient than electric but more complex.

For a 10-beast room, electric defrost on a timer is the standard and the correct choice. Off-cycle only works in very dry conditions and low door traffic.

Defrost cycle settings

  • Frequency: every 4–6 hours in humid weather, every 8–12 hours in dry weather.
  • Duration: until the coil is clear, usually 10–20 minutes with an electric defrost.
  • Termination: by coil temperature or by a fixed time — better to use coil temperature so the cycle ends when the ice is gone, not just when the timer expires.

The temperature log

Keep a written or digital log of room temperature at least twice a day, once in the morning before work starts and once in the afternoon. Why:

  • The health inspector will ask for it.
  • You will spot a slow drift before it becomes a problem — a compressor losing charge, a door gasket failing, a controller drifting.
  • If a meat load is rejected, the log is your only defence.
Photograph the temperature display twice a day for the first month. Ten seconds of work twice a day gives you a month of evidence that the room holds temperature correctly. It also shows you the daily pattern — when the room works hardest, how long recovery takes after loading — which is real management information.

Lesson 4.6

Writing your refrigeration specification

The one-page document you hand to a refrigeration supplier or installer:

Room details

  • Internal dimensions: length × width × height in metres
  • Internal volume in m³
  • Operating temperature: 2°C (chilled) or −18°C (frozen)
  • Outside design ambient temperature: highest expected local temperature in °C
  • Floor insulation: yes / no, thickness
  • Wall and ceiling panel: core material, thickness

Load

  • Peak heat load (from your calculation): kW
  • Average daily load: kW
  • Basis of the calculation: what assumptions, what ΔT

Equipment

  • Type: split system / monoblock
  • Rated capacity (at your conditions): kW
  • Refrigerant: type and charge weight
  • Evaporator: air-cooled, with defrost type stated
  • Controller: digital, with high-temperature alarm
  • Voltage and phase (single or three)
  • Standby power integration: manual or automatic changeover

Installation requirements

  • Evaporator position and airflow direction
  • Condenser position and clearance dimensions
  • Refrigerant pipe route and length
  • Electrical supply details and isolator position
  • Defrost drain route to floor drain
  • Commissioning requirements: pressure test, vacuum, charge weight, temperature pull-down test with record

Warranty and service

  • Warranty period on parts and labour
  • Service interval recommended by the manufacturer
  • Local service capability — name and phone of the nearest technician who works on this brand
The rule: get two quotations and compare them on all of the above. A quotation that does not state the rated capacity at your conditions, the refrigerant type, and the commissioning procedure is not a quotation you can accept. It is a guess.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. Which component of the cold room's heat load is usually the largest on kill day?

Correct: the carcasses. A warm carcass at 38°C brings a large heat load that the unit must remove over the following 12–24 hours.

2. A refrigeration unit is rated 6 kW at standard conditions. Why might it not achieve 6 kW in a rural African cold room?

Correct: conditions reduce capacity. The rating is for standard conditions. Real-world capacity is lower by 10–30% depending on the site.

3. What is the correct size for the refrigeration unit relative to your calculated peak load?

Correct: 1.5× to 2×. Handles derating and uncertainty, and avoids short-cycling. It does not consume 1.5× the power — it just does not run flat-out.

4. Why must the condenser NOT be placed in direct afternoon sun?

Correct: less efficient heat rejection. The condenser rejects heat to the surrounding air. Hot air means poor rejection and reduced capacity.

5. Why must a refrigeration system be evacuated (vacuum-pulled) before charging?

Correct: moisture destroys the compressor. Residual water plus refrigerant forms acid that corrodes the compressor internals. Vacuum before charge is non-negotiable.

Practical assignment — Module 4

Produce a written refrigeration specification for your own cold room, in the format of Lesson 4.6.

Submit:

  1. Your full heat load calculation, showing each component's arithmetic. State your assumptions for ΔT, wall area, product weight, cooling hours and door opens.
  2. Your calculated peak load (kW), and the equipment rated capacity you will order (1.5× to 2× of peak).
  3. At least two quotations from refrigeration suppliers for split systems meeting your specification. Each must state rated capacity, refrigerant type, defrost type, controller, and installation requirements.
  4. A photograph of a proposed condenser location at your site, showing shading and clearance.
  5. Your defrost cycle settings and controller configuration (target, hysteresis, alarm setpoint).
  6. One paragraph (150 words) stating the single most important installation detail in your opinion, and why failing it would kill the unit prematurely.

Marks are weighted toward points 1 and 6. Any supplier can put a rated kW on paper. The skill is in knowing what that number means at your site, and where you have to protect it after the truck leaves.
