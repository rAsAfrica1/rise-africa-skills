Practical Reference

Freezing is a capital-intensive business discipline, not a preservation hobby. In sub-Saharan Africa, where grid power is intermittent and diesel is expensive, the difference between a profitable frozen-food operation and a cash-burning one comes down to three numbers: kilowatt-hours per kilogram frozen, kilograms lost per month to temperature abuse, and the price premium your product earns for consistent quality. This reference block gives you the benchmark figures, the equipment trade-offs, and the operating discipline you need to hit those numbers.

Never refreeze meat, poultry or fish that has fully thawed. Thawing allows bacteria such as Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli to multiply rapidly; refreezing does not kill them. If a load has been above â18Â°C for more than a few hours, treat it as unsafe for human consumption and dispose of it â do not sell it at a discount.

Refrigerants are hazardous. Ammonia (R717) is toxic and flammable in high concentrations; ammonia leaks have killed workers in African cold stores. Never enter a plant room without a calibrated gas detector and a second person outside. Never weld or braze refrigerant lines without purging with nitrogen. All refrigerant handling must be done by a certified technician under your national environmental authority's regulations (e.g. NEMA in Kenya, ZEMA in Zambia, EMA in Zimbabwe). Wear gloves and eye protection when handling dry ice or liquid nitrogen â contact causes instant frostbite.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Verify core temperature, not air temperature. Probe the thickest part of the product with a calibrated thermometer before it leaves the blast freezer. Air temperature lies; core temperature does not.
  • Pre-cool within 2 hours of harvest or slaughter. Every hour at ambient temperature shortens frozen shelf life. Build pre-cooling into your buying schedule, not as an afterthought.
  • Size your refrigeration to your worst-case power gap. If your area loses grid power 6 hours a day, your cold store needs enough thermal mass and backup to hold â18Â°C for at least 8 hours.
  • Track kilowatt-hours per kilogram. This is your true production cost. If it rises above 0.35 kWh/kg, you have a door-seal, defrost-cycle or loading-practice problem.
  • Never overload a freezer. Loading more than 10% above rated capacity extends freezing time, raises energy cost and degrades texture.
  • Package before freezing, not after. Vacuum-seal or glaze to prevent freezer burn and weight loss â unglazed fish can lose 3â5% of saleable weight per month.
  • Keep a temperature log. Record cold-store temperature twice daily. Buyers, retailers and export inspectors will ask for it; a documented chain is a sales asset.
  • Price your product on total cost, not competitor price. Add energy, labour, packaging, spoilage and finance cost per kilogram, then set your margin. Selling below cost is not a strategy.
