Lesson 1

Reading the Price Calendar

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Map the seasonal price cycle for vegetables in your market
  • Plan production to hit the peaks, not the gluts
  • Understand price elasticity in African vegetable markets

Every vegetable has a price calendar. In most African markets the shape is the same: low prices during the main harvest season (when everyone with a field brings produce) and high prices in the dry season and just before rains (when supply dries up).

The typical tomato price curve over a year in a Southern African urban market:

  • Peak supply: April–June (end of rainy season) — prices at their lowest, $0.20–0.40/kg
  • Declining supply: July–August — prices rising, $0.50–0.80/kg
  • Low supply: September–November — prices at their highest, $0.80–1.50/kg, spikes to $2+ in some years
  • Rainy season: December–March — supply variable, prices moderate

A farmer who harvests in April sells into the glut. A farmer who harvests in October sells into the shortage. Same crop, same cost, 3–5x the price.

How to use it

  1. Track your local market's prices for one full year. Write them down weekly.
  2. Work backwards from the peak price months to your planting dates.
  3. Plan the majority of your production for peak months. A smaller production for the off-peak months keeps cash flowing and keeps buyers loyal.
Lesson 2

Staggered Planting — the Anti-Glut Discipline

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Stagger plantings to supply continuously
  • Avoid the "everything harvests at once" trap
  • Match supply rhythm to buyer rhythm

The most common beginner mistake is planting the entire plot on the same day. Six weeks later, all the kale is ready. Eight weeks later, all the tomato is ripe. You cannot sell it all — the price collapses, buyers dictate terms, and a large fraction rots.

The staggering rule

Divide each crop into 4–6 plantings per year, spaced 10–14 days apart for short crops (leafy greens, radish) and 3–4 weeks apart for long crops (tomato, cabbage, onion).

Example: for a 1,000 m² tomato plot supplying a restaurant weekly, divide the plot into 4 sub-plots of 250 m² each. Plant one sub-plot every 3 weeks. Result: continuous production over 12 weeks from each planting wave, and 4 harvests over the season instead of one big glut.

Match buyer rhythm

  • Market vendors buy daily or every other day — small, steady volumes.
  • Restaurants and hotels buy weekly or fortnightly — one delivery per week, on a specific day.
  • Supermarkets buy to contract — fixed day, fixed quantity, fixed grade.
  • Processors buy in bulk seasonally — usually once or twice per year for tomato paste.

Design your staggering to fit your main buyer's rhythm. A weekly buyer wants a steady weekly quantity, not a big dump followed by nothing.

Lesson 3

Buyer Relationships and Contracts

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Build loyalty with your best buyers
  • Understand when to accept a contract and when to walk away
  • Handle payment risk

Building buyer loyalty

  • Consistency beats price. A buyer who gets the same quality, every week, on time, will pay a premium and will not switch for a 5% cheaper supplier.
  • Communicate before problems. If a crop fails or quality dips, call the buyer the day you know, not the day you were supposed to deliver.
  • Deliver to spec. Read the specification once and follow it exactly. Do not send what you have if it does not match.
  • Invoice properly. A numbered, dated invoice with your business name and contact is professional and reduces payment delays.

Contracts — read every line

Same discipline as any contract. Watch for:

  • Delivery schedule you cannot meet
  • Rejection clauses with no appeal
  • Payment terms beyond 30 days without interest
  • Exclusive supply clauses that lock you to one buyer
  • Penalty clauses that trigger automatically

Small farmers sometimes sign contracts they cannot meet and lose money. Read, ask, negotiate. A buyer who will not discuss terms is not a buyer you want.

Payment risk

  • Cash on delivery is best. Accept it whenever offered.
  • 30-day terms for a known buyer is normal. Set a credit limit and stick to it.
  • Never let a single buyer hold more than 30% of your outstanding receivables. If they go bust, you lose that 30% and no more.
Lesson 4

Records, Costing and the Margin

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Calculate the true cost per bed, per cycle
  • Compare margins across crops and buyers
  • Use records to make better decisions next season

Cost per 1,000 m² tomato cycle (indicative)

ItemUSD
Seed$15–40
Compost / manure$20–60
Fertiliser$40–100
Pesticides / fungicides$30–120
Water / irrigation (fuel or fees)$20–80
Labour (planting, weeding, harvest)$60–200
Crates, packaging, transport$20–60
Total per 1,000 m² per cycle$205–660

Revenue comparison

At 4 t (fresh retail) per 1,000 m² at $0.60/kg = $2,400 gross. At $0.30/kg in a glut = $1,200 gross. At 8 t/ha yield (good management) = $4,800 gross. The price makes the difference between $1,000 profit and $4,000 profit on the same cost base.

Records to keep every cycle

  • Bed-by-bed cost and revenue
  • Crop, variety, planting date, harvest dates
  • Yield per bed, per crop
  • Buyer, grade, price per kg, payment date
  • Pest and disease incidents and what worked

After 3 cycles, these records tell you which bed, which crop, and which buyer made the most money. They are the difference between guessing and knowing.

Lesson 5

Scaling and the Year-Round Plan

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Plan a 12-month vegetable calendar that keeps the business running
  • Decide when to expand
  • Understand the risks of over-scaling

The 12-month plan

Write a calendar with rows for months and columns for: crop, plot size, planting date, expected harvest, target buyer, price target. Every month has one or two active crops. No month has empty plots and no month has everything at once.

Example rotation:

  • Jan–Feb: leafy greens (fast cash)
  • Feb–Mar: tomato nursery, transplant March
  • May–Jun: tomato harvest, sell into rising prices
  • Jul–Aug: cabbage / onion planting
  • Sep–Oct: cabbage / onion harvest, peak prices
  • Nov–Dec: leafy greens, second tomato cycle

When to expand

Expand only when:

  • The current plot has run 2 successful cycles with records showing profit
  • Water supply is confirmed for the larger area
  • Buyers are confirmed for the larger volume — do not grow more than you can sell
  • Labour is available for the larger area
  • A working capital buffer covers the larger inputs for 3 months

When NOT to expand

  • Because you had one lucky season
  • Because you want to "make more money" without the market to absorb the volume
  • Before you can keep records on the current plot
  • Before you know your cost per cycle

Most small vegetable businesses die not from lack of ambition but from over-scaling. A 1,000 m² plot run well is worth more than 1 hectare run badly.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. When are vegetable prices usually highest?

Correct: in the dry season. Low supply = high prices. The peak supply months are the worst time to sell.

2. What is the anti-glut technique called?

Correct: staggered planting. Continuous supply beats one big glut every time.

3. How should you handle payment risk from a single buyer?

Correct: cap exposure at 30%. If that buyer fails, you lose 30% and no more.

4. When should you expand the vegetable plot?

Correct: after 2 profitable cycles with confirmed support. Over-scaling is the most common cause of small farm failure.

5. What records should you keep per cycle?

Correct: full cycle records. After 3 cycles, records show which bed, crop and buyer made the most money.

Module 6 capstone

Write your 12-month vegetable production calendar and business plan. Include: (1) the crops for each month and the plot size for each; (2) your staggered planting dates; (3) your target buyers per crop; (4) the expected revenue and costs per cycle; (5) the two triggers that would make you expand, and the two that would make you change crop.
