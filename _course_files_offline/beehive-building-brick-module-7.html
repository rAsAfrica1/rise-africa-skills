Module 7

Hive Stands, Base Plates and Ant Barriers

A masonry hive cannot be lifted onto a stand. Its base plate and plinth do the same job: keep the hive dry, off the ground, and defended against ants and predators.

Module 7 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Design a plinth and base plate for a fixed masonry hive
  • Prevent moisture rising from the ground into the hive
  • Build an ant barrier that works continuously
  • Keep the hive dry through the wet season
  • Prevent rodents and reptiles from entering the base
  • Complete the base system for your masonry hive

Lesson 7.1

The plinth — raising the hive off the ground

A wooden hive sits on a 300–500 mm stand. A masonry hive cannot be lifted — so the plinth is cast as part of the base, raising the whole structure to the correct height.

Plinth dimensions

DimensionValue
Height above ground300–400 mm
FootprintHive footprint + 200 mm each side (for weather protection)
ConstructionBrick or rubble base with a concrete top
Top surfaceLevel, waterproof, self-draining

Why the plinth matters

  • Termite defence. A plinth of brick or concrete has no timber in contact with soil. Termites cannot cross.
  • Damp control. Raising the hive above ground reduces moisture wicking up through the walls.
  • Flood defence. In high-rainfall areas, a raised plinth keeps the hive base above surface water.
  • Pest defence. Rodents and reptiles cannot easily climb a well-built plinth with a smooth surface.
  • Working height. A hive at 300–400 mm is a comfortable working height for inspections.

Building the plinth

  1. Mark the footprint — hive external dimensions plus 200 mm each side.
  2. Excavate to 150 mm deep and compact rubble into the base.
  3. Build up with brick or block to the target height, leaving a rubble-filled core.
  4. Cap with a 75–100 mm concrete slab, level and smooth.
  5. Ensure the slab drains — slope 1:100 away from the hive on all sides.
  6. Cure the concrete 28 days before building the hive on top.

Lesson 7.2

The base plate

The base plate (floor) of the hive sits on top of the plinth. It is the surface the bees work from and the bottom of the cavity.

Materials

  • Cast concrete slab — 75–100 mm thick, cast in place or pre-cast. Best.
  • Brick floor with a screed top — brick laid flat, then a 25 mm screed. Acceptable.
  • Stone slab — traditional in some areas. Rarely available in the correct size.

Design features

  • Slope: 1:200 toward the entrance end so any condensation or rain that enters drains out.
  • Smooth top: so bees do not build comb on the floor and cleaning is easy.
  • Damp-proof course between plinth and floor: a layer of polyethylene prevents moisture rising from the plinth into the hive.
  • Floor must be level across the width so top bars hang level.

Damp-proof course

Between the concrete plinth and the base plate, install a damp-proof course:

  • 0.5 mm polyethylene sheet (cheap and effective)
  • Bitumen-painted layer (traditional, but not food-safe if it enters the hive — avoid)
  • Slate or tile course (durable but heavy)

Polyethylene is the standard for a small hive. It stops rising damp from reaching the hive cavity.

Lesson 7.3

The ant barrier

Ants are the single most common cause of colony absconding in African hives. They enter the hive for honey and brood, and if the colony cannot defend against them, it leaves.

The three ant barrier methods for masonry hives

  1. Ant moat. A shallow tray of oil or water surrounding the hive base. Ants cannot cross. Standard for wooden hives but harder for masonry.
  2. Powder barrier. A band of ash, diatomaceous earth, or fine sand around the base. Ants cannot cross because the powder clogs their joints.
  3. Grease band. A band of heavy grease or old engine oil painted around the base. Ants cannot climb through it.

Building an ant barrier for masonry

A masonry hive plinth has no legs to put in moats. The correct approach:

  • Perimeter powder or grease band. A 100 mm wide band of ash, diatomaceous earth, or grease on the concrete slab, all the way around the hive base.
  • The band must be continuous — no gaps.
  • Refresh monthly — rain, dust, and wind destroy the band.
  • Combine with cleaning the surrounding area. Remove vegetation, food sources, and ant nests within 2 m of the hive.

Ant-proof hive design

A more permanent solution is to design the hive with an overhanging lip on the base plate. Ants cannot climb an overhang — they must go around and down, and the underside of the overhang is a physical barrier.

For a masonry hive, the plinth should be narrower than the base plate, so the base plate overhangs the plinth by 30–50 mm. Combined with a grease band on the overhang, this is effective.

The ant rule: ants do not climb oil, ash, or overhangs. Provide at least one of these — continuously — and the colony is defended.

Lesson 7.4

Rodent and reptile prevention

What gets in

  • Rats and mice — climb into the hive at the entrance if the entrance is over 20 mm high. Also dig under the plinth.
  • Snakes — enter for the warmth and the honey. Typically enter through the entrance or a crack.
  • Mongooses, genets — dig under the plinth to reach the cavity.
  • Honey badgers — extremely rare but a problem where they occur. They dig under the plinth and rip the hive apart. Only a properly designed masonry hive with a deep footing stops them.

Defences

  • Entrance height 15–20 mm. Blocks rats and mice.
  • Footing depth 300 mm minimum. Blocks most digging animals.
  • Rubble core in the plinth. Wrapped in wire mesh to prevent digging through.
  • Rendered smooth external surfaces. No handholds for climbers.
  • Overhanging base plate. Difficult to climb around.
  • Perimeter barrier. A 300 mm band of gravel or rubble around the plinth discourages digging.

The mesh footing

A common solution: wrap the footing in a 25 mm steel mesh, fixed horizontally at the top of the footing. Any animal digging down hits the mesh and cannot proceed. Cost: $30–80 per hive. Worth it in areas with digging predators.

Lesson 7.5

Wet-season protection

The plinth and base plate must work in the wet season, not just the dry. Water is the enemy of a masonry hive.

Water paths to block

  1. Rising damp — from wet ground through the plinth. Blocked by the damp-proof course under the base plate.
  2. Surface flow — rain running across the ground into the hive base. Blocked by the plinth height and perimeter drain.
  3. Splashback — rain hitting the ground near the base and splashing up. Blocked by a 200 mm gravel band around the plinth and by roof overhang.
  4. Roof runoff — water from the roof falling directly onto the hive walls. Blocked by roof overhang (Module 5).
  5. Wall wicking — water absorbed by brick walls, moving upward and inward. Blocked by exterior render and paint.

The wet-season checklist

  • Perimeter drain cleared of debris before the rains.
  • Gravel band around the plinth refreshed.
  • Roof gutters cleared.
  • Damp-proof course intact (check before first rains).
  • Any cracks in the plinth sealed with mortar.
  • Ant barrier refreshed (rain destroys powder bands).
  • Hive entrance reduced (if robbing season coincides with rains).
The plinth rule: a masonry hive is only as good as its base. 300–400 mm plinth, concrete base plate, damp-proof course, ant barrier, perimeter drain. All five. Miss one and the wet season finds it.

Knowledge check — Module 7

1. Why does a masonry hive need a plinth instead of a stand?

Correct: too heavy. The plinth raises the hive to the same working height a stand would give a wooden hive.

2. What is the correct height for a hive plinth?

Correct: 300–400 mm. Matches the height a wooden hive stand gives. Comfortable working height.

3. What is the simplest and most effective ant barrier for a masonry hive?

Correct: powder or grease band. Ants cannot cross a continuous band. But it must be refreshed monthly — rain and dust destroy it.

4. Why should the base plate overhang the plinth?

Correct: physical barrier. Ants cannot climb an overhang. Combined with a grease band, it is very effective.

5. What is the recommended minimum footing depth to block digging predators?

Correct: 300 mm. Plus wire mesh wrapping for extra defence against honey badgers and mongooses.
