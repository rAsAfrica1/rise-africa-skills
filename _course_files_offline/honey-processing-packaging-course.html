Practical Reference

Honey is one of the few agricultural products that can be harvested, processed and sold by a single operator with under US$300 of equipment. The margin, however, sits in the processing and packaging stage, not in the beekeeping itself. Raw comb sells cheaply at the farm gate; filtered, moisture-controlled, correctly labelled honey in a sealed jar sells for two to five times more in urban supermarkets and export corridors. This reference block gives you the numbers, the standards and the operating discipline to capture that margin.

Honey must never be fed to infants under 12 months. Honey can carry Clostridium botulinum spores, which cause infant botulism; the spores survive pasteurisation and cannot be removed by home processing. Commercial honey sold in shops carries the same risk and must carry a warning label. In addition, never use lead-based solder, galvanised or unlined metal containers, or recycled chemical drums for honey storage â honey is acidic (pH 3.2â4.5) and will leach heavy metals into the product. Always use food-grade stainless steel, food-grade plastic or glass. Do not use antibiotics or unregistered miticides in hives; residues are rejected at export and are a public health hazard. Handle hot honey and hot water baths with heat-resistant gloves to avoid burns.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test moisture before every batch. Use a calibrated refractometer; reject or re-dry any batch above 20% moisture. This single step protects your shelf life and your reputation.
  2. Harvest only fully capped frames. Uncapped honey is unripe, ferments, and destroys the value of the whole batch.
  3. Keep extraction and packing areas clean and dry. Honey absorbs ambient moisture and odours; work in a closed, screened room away from livestock and fuel.
  4. Use only food-grade containers and clean, dry jars. Glass or food-grade PET; never recycled chemical drums, galvanised buckets or unlined metal.
  5. Label every batch. Include product name, net weight, batch number, production date, best-before date, producer contact and â if selling in a regulated market â the required nutrition and infant warning statement.
  6. Price by channel, not by habit. Farm-gate comb, bulk bucket and retail jar are three different businesses with three different margins. Track each separately.
  7. Keep a simple batch record. Date, hive source, moisture reading, litres in, jars out, cost per jar, sale price. This is the data that unlocks credit and supermarket contracts.
  8. Register and certify early. Contact your national bureau of standards and ministry of agriculture for food-handling registration before you approach supermarkets or exporters.
