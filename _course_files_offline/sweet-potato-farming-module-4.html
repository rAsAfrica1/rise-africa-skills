Lesson 1

Sweet Potato Weevil — Why It Is the Main Threat

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Recognise weevil damage in roots and vines
  • Understand the weevil life cycle and why it is hard to beat
  • Know the losses it causes

Sweet potato weevil (Cylas spp.) is the single biggest cause of storage and market losses for African sweet potato. Damage can be 20–80% of the roots in heavily infested fields.

Damage signs

  • Small holes on the root surface
  • Tunnels inside the root filled with dark frass
  • Bitter taste and unpleasant smell in the affected root
  • Premature yellowing or wilting of vines in heavy infestations
  • Roots rot in storage faster if weevil-damaged

The life cycle

The adult weevil is a small ant-like beetle. Females lay eggs in the root or vine. Larvae tunnel inside. The full cycle takes 4–6 weeks. Adults can survive in crop residue, on stored roots, and on wild alternative hosts. Population builds through the season and is highest at harvest — meaning the last-formed roots are the most damaged.

Why it is hard to beat

  • The larvae are inside the root — most sprays cannot reach them
  • Adults survive in crop residue between seasons
  • Alternative hosts keep populations alive even in a clean field
  • Weevil pressure is higher in dry conditions
Lesson 2

The Stacked Defence Against Weevil

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Deploy six integrated tactics against weevil
  • Combine sanitation, pheromone traps, resistant varieties and timely harvest
  • Understand which tactics pay back most

1. Sanitation — the single most powerful tactic

  • Do not leave harvested roots or vine residue in the field.
  • Remove and destroy crop residue after harvest — plough in or burn.
  • Remove alternative hosts (wild Ipomoea species) around the field.
  • Never store damaged roots — they become weevil breeding sites.

2. Timely harvest

Weevil populations build during the season. A crop left in the ground for 5–6 months has far more damage than one harvested at 4 months. Do not delay harvest. This single decision reduces damage by 30–50%.

3. Vine sanitation

Plant cuttings free of weevil eggs. Inspect each cutting — any with small holes or tunnels should be discarded.

4. Pheromone traps

Commercially available traps use the female sex pheromone to attract males. Placed at 20–40 traps/ha, they reduce the male population and slow mating. Cost-effective where weevil pressure is high.

5. Resistant or tolerant varieties

Some varieties have partial resistance to weevil. Ask your national research institute for the current resistant variety list. Resistance is not immunity — combine with other tactics.

6. Chemical control — last

Some systemic insecticides and soil drenches reduce weevil at early stages. Not usually cost-effective on their own for weevil and not recommended as a primary tactic. Do not spray for weevil without an extension officer's guidance.

Lesson 3

Other Pests — Rats, Nematodes, Vine Borer

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify the other major pests of sweet potato
  • Use the same IPM approach across all pests
  • Decide when the damage justifies intervention

Rats and other rodents

The second biggest pest after weevil. Rats tunnel under ridges and eat roots. Damage can be 10–40% in heavily infested fields.

  • Community rat control — coordinated baiting across neighbours is the only effective method
  • Traps and cats
  • Clean field borders — remove tall grass and cover where rats nest
  • Harvest on time — late harvests are more damaged

Nematodes (root-knot, lesion)

Cause galls and lesions on roots, reducing yield and quality. Worse in sandy soils and continuous sweet potato.

  • Rotation with cereals or legumes
  • Plant resistant varieties where available
  • Add organic matter — this supports nematode-antagonistic soil biology
  • Solarisation of soil in hot weather — clear plastic for 4–6 weeks

Vine borer

Less common than weevil. Larva bores into the vine, causing wilting of the affected vine. Rarely causes major losses. Hand-remove affected vines.

Aphids and whitefly

Both transmit viruses. As with the weevil, vine quality is the main defence. Remove infected plants.

Lesson 4

Diseases — Root Rot, Scab, Virus

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify the four main diseases
  • Distinguish fungal rot from bacterial rot and virus
  • Use varietal choice, rotation and drainage to manage them

Root rots (Fusarium, Pythium, Phytophthora)

Brown to black rotting roots at harvest. Worse in waterlogged soils and continuous sweet potato. Control:

  • Raised ridges and good drainage — the most important tactic
  • Rotation with cereals or legumes
  • Varietal resistance
  • Clean vine stock

Sweet potato scab (Streptomyces)

Raised, corky lesions on the root surface. Cosmetic damage that reduces market value but does not affect eating quality. Worse on alkaline or sandy soils and continuous cropping.

  • Rotation and pH management
  • Varietal tolerance
  • Avoid fresh manure (raises pH in the short term)

Sweet potato virus disease (SPVD)

Complex of two viruses transmitted by whitefly and aphids. Symptoms: stunted growth, mottled or distorted leaves, small roots. The most damaging single disease complex in African sweet potato.

  • Plant certified clean vines
  • Replace vine stock every 2–3 years
  • Remove infected plants in the field
  • Varietal resistance (partial)

Alternaria leaf spot and anthracnose

Foliar diseases. Rarely yield-limiting on their own. Sanitation and airflow reduce incidence. Fungicide rarely justified.

Lesson 5

Scouting and Threshold Decisions

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Set a weekly scouting routine for sweet potato
  • Decide when a problem crosses a threshold
  • Avoid spraying when it is not justified

Weekly scouting (10–15 min per 1,000 m²)

  1. Walk a W pattern through the plot
  2. Check 10 points: look at vine tips for wilting, leaf undersides for aphids and whitefly, and soil surface near the ridge for weevil activity
  3. At 8 weeks and again at 12 weeks, dig up 2–3 plants at random to inspect roots for weevil damage
  4. Record the observations with dates

When to act

  • Weevil: act immediately if you find weevil tunnels in dug-up roots — increase pheromone traps, remove residue, plan early harvest
  • Rats: act when damage is visible — coordinate with neighbours
  • Aphids and whitefly: remove infected plants; consider neem or soap only if populations are heavy
  • Root rot: no spray helps — ensure drainage and rotate next season

The general discipline: sweet potato does not usually justify routine spraying. The primary tactics — clean vines, sanitation, drainage, timely harvest — do the work. Sprays are a last resort and rarely economic.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. What is the single biggest pest of sweet potato in Africa?

Correct: sweet potato weevil. Can cause 20–80% root loss in heavily infested fields.

2. What is the cheapest single tactic against weevil?

Correct: field sanitation. Free, immediate, and reduces weevil by up to 50%.

3. What is the most damaging disease complex in African sweet potato?

Correct: SPVD. Complex of two viruses spread by whitefly and aphids; causes stunting and small roots.

4. How does delayed harvest affect weevil damage?

Correct: increases damage 30–50%. Each month past 4 months multiplies weevil population on the roots.

5. What prevents sweet potato scab?

Correct: rotation and pH management. Fresh manure raises pH short-term and worsens scab.
