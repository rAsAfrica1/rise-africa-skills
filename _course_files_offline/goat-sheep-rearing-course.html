Zoonotic diseases in goats and sheep â brucellosis, Q fever, toxoplasmosis, and orf â can infect humans. Pregnant women, children, and immuno-compromised workers are at highest risk.

  • Always wear gloves and cover open cuts when handling afterbirth, aborted foetuses, or sick animals.
  • Never drink raw (unpasteurised) goat or sheep milk. Boil or pasteurise before consumption or sale.
  • Follow withdrawal periods strictly on all dewormers, antibiotics, and acaricides. Selling meat or milk inside the withdrawal window is illegal in most African markets and leaves residues in the food chain.
  • Dispose of needles and expired veterinary drugs through approved veterinary channels â never in household waste or water sources.
  • If you or a worker develop fever, joint pain, or flu-like symptoms after handling animals, seek medical attention and tell the clinician you work with livestock.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm your market first. Call three buyers â butcheries, hotels, or livestock traders â and get current prices per kilogram live weight before you buy a single animal.
  • Vaccinate and deworm on schedule. Build a 12-month animal health calendar with your vet: PPR and pasteurellosis vaccines, plus strategic deworming twice a year.
  • Track feed cost per animal per day. Feed is 60â70% of your cost base. If you cannot state your cost per kilogram of gain, you cannot price your animals profitably.
  • Maintain a breeding register. Record service dates, kidding/lambing dates, offspring count, and dam ID. Without records you cannot cull poor performers or prove genetics to buyers.
  • Keep mortality under 10%. Weigh every animal monthly. Sudden weight loss is your earliest disease signal â act within 48 hours.
  • Build a quarantine pen. Every new animal stays isolated for 21 days before joining the herd. This single practice prevents most herd-wide disease outbreaks.
  • Secure water year-round. A lactating doe needs 5â8 litres per day. Drought is the leading cause of enterprise collapse in African small ruminant farming.
  • Reinvest 20â30% of female offspring. Retain the best-performing daughters as breeding stock. Buying replacements every year destroys your margin.
