Practical Reference

Soil conservation is not an environmental gesture â it is a margin decision. Every centimetre of topsoil you lose is revenue you will never recover, and every kilogram of organic matter you build is yield you can bank. Across sub-Saharan Africa, the economics of soil restoration are unusually favourable because the starting point is so degraded: the marginal return on the first tonne of compost or the first contour ridge is far higher than on a farm already at yield ceiling. This reference block gives you the hard numbers, the decision rules, and the safety boundaries you need to run soil restoration as a business, not a hobby.

â ï¸ Safety Warning â Compost, Manure and Soil Amendments

Fresh manure and inadequately composted organic material can carry E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria, parasitic worm eggs and, in some cases, antibiotic residues. These are a direct food-safety and health risk to you, your workers and your customers.

  • Compost properly. Maintain a thermophilic phase of 55â65 Â°C for at least 3 consecutive days, turning the pile so all material reaches temperature. Pathogens are killed by heat, not by time alone.
  • Never apply fresh manure to vegetables eaten raw. Apply only fully matured compost, and observe a minimum 90â120 day interval between manure application and harvest for crops in contact with soil.
  • Wear gloves and boots when handling manure, and wash hands with soap before eating, drinking or smoking. Keep children and animals out of composting areas.
  • Do not burn crop residues or plastics in the field. Burning destroys organic matter and releases toxic compounds. If you make biochar, use a closed retort or kiln, never an open smouldering pit, and keep it away from dwellings.
  • Pesticide containers are not water containers. Never reuse agrochemical drums for drinking water, compost tea or feed. Triple-rinse and puncture before disposal.
  • If you sell compost or soil products, label them clearly and keep batch records. A single contamination incident can end a supply contract permanently.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test before you treat. Take a soil sample (0â20 cm, 15â20 sub-samples per hectare in a W pattern) and get pH, organic carbon and available P tested before buying any input.
  2. Stop the loss first. Contour ridges, stone bunds or grass strips come before compost. You cannot build topsoil faster than a bare slope removes it.
  3. Set a compost target. Aim for 5â10 tonnes per hectare per year on degraded land; build the pile before the rains, not during them.
  4. Keep residue on the field. Retain at least 30% ground cover. Residue is feed for your soil, not just for your livestock â split the difference deliberately.
  5. Log every input and yield. Record compost tonnes applied, rainfall, and yield per plot. Without records you cannot prove ROI to a lender or buyer.
  6. Check your compost temperature weekly. If it is not heating, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio or moisture is wrong â fix it before the batch fails.
  7. Protect workers. Gloves, boots, handwashing station and a clear no-eating rule in the composting yard.
  8. Price your product honestly. If you sell compost, state nutrient content and maturity. Reputation is your cheapest marketing.
