Lesson 1

Knowing When to Harvest

In this lesson
  • Judge harvest timing by vine senescence
  • Understand the trade-off between fresh and stored yam
  • Plan for the ceremonial peak market

Signs of maturity

  • Vines yellow and dry back naturally
  • Leaves drop; stems dry to brown
  • Tubers are firm, skin is well-set
  • Soil cracks appear at the base of the mound (tuber pushing upward)

Timing

  • Early harvest: 7–8 months, for fresh market — smaller tubers but earlier cash
  • Full maturity: 9–10 months for white and yellow yam; 10–12 months for water yam
  • Late harvest: risk of tuber moth, rodent damage, and rot in the soil

Market timing

The ceremonial peak in West Africa is around Christmas, New Year, Easter and major festivals. Prices are 30–60% higher. If you can harvest in November-December, you sell into the peak. If you can store until then, you also sell into the peak.

Lesson 2

Harvesting Without Damage

In this lesson
  • Lift yam without cutting or bruising
  • Handle carefully to preserve storage life
  • Sort immediately into grades

Timing of day

Harvest in the cool of the morning. Yam lifted in hot sun softens and bruises easily.

Lifting

  • Use a yam fork or a strong digging stick
  • Start at the side of the mound, work inward to avoid cutting the tuber
  • Lift the tuber carefully by the head (the top of the tuber), not the middle
  • Shake off loose soil gently

Handling

  • Place tubers in the shade immediately
  • Do not drop or stack more than 3 deep
  • Cut the vine at 5–10 cm from the tuber head
  • Never wash in the field — brush off soil

Sorting

  • Seed grade: 100–400 g whole tubers, clean, no damage
  • Table grade 1: large, uniform, no blemish
  • Table grade 2: medium, minor blemish
  • Processing grade: broken, oversized or blemished, for flour and chips
  • Reject: rotten or heavily damaged
Lesson 3

Curing and Storage

In this lesson
  • Cure yam before storage
  • Choose a storage method
  • Understand storage life

Curing

Cure yam by keeping it in a shaded, ventilated place for 7–14 days. This heals the tuber surface, reduces rot, and improves storage life. Do not cure in the sun — the tubers will dry out and crack.

Storage methods

  • Yam barn: traditional West African structure. Tubers tied to a frame under shade, elevated off the ground. Allows airflow and inspection. Storage life 3–6 months. Loss 10–20%.
  • Pit storage: tubers layered in a dry pit, covered with soil. Used in drier areas. Losses can be high if the pit is not well drained.
  • Platform storage: tubers on a raised platform in a shed. Good airflow. Loss 10–15% over 3–5 months.
  • Cool room: commercial scale. Temperature 12–15°C. Storage life 6–8 months. Loss under 5%. Capital $3,000–15,000.

Storage discipline

  • Only store sound, undamaged tubers
  • Keep in shade, cool, dry, ventilated
  • Inspect weekly; remove any rotting tubers
  • Do not store near fruit or onions (ethylene causes sprouting)

Lesson 4

The Seed Yam Market

In this lesson
  • Understand the seed yam business
  • Price and margin the operation
  • Build the buyer network

The business

Seed yam is the highest-margin product in yam. A seed yam producer sells small tubers (100–400 g) to other farmers for planting. Price 2–3x food yam. Certified seed commands the top of that range.

Economics per hectare

  • Inputs: seed yam 1,200 kg, other costs — $1,500–3,000
  • Output: 12–20 t/ha seed yam at $1.50–3.00/kg = $18,000–60,000 gross
  • Net margin: $10,000–40,000 per hectare

The trick is that seed yam sells for 2–3x food yam, and a well-managed seed operation yields as much tonnage as a food yam operation. That is why seed yam is the highest-return business in the yam value chain.

Requirements

  • Clean seed to start with (certified pre-basic if possible)
  • Strict nematode and virus management
  • Grading by size (small, medium, large seed)
  • Reputation — other farmers only buy seed from trusted sources
  • Certification if selling into institutional programmes
The rule: the seed yam business rewards discipline. Get nematode and virus control right and you sell at 2–3x. Get it wrong and you sell the same crop at food price.
Lesson 5

The One-Page Business Plan

In this lesson
  • Complete your one-page plan
  • Set year 1 and year 2 targets
  • Decide scale triggers

Before next season, fill in this one page:

  1. Business type: food yam, seed yam, processing, or combination?
  2. Capital tier: how much will you invest?
  3. Species and variety: which and why?
  4. Area: how many hectares?
  5. Target yield: t/ha (start with 12 t/ha)
  6. Buyers: three named contacts
  7. Revenue target: price × yield
  8. Total cost: every line
  9. Net margin: revenue − cost
  10. Value step: what product to add in 12 months (flour, chips, or certified seed)
  11. Scaling trigger: what condition before expanding

Read it at harvest. Read it again next season.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. When is yam ready to harvest?

Correct: 9–10 months, vines senesced. Water yam takes 10–12 months.

2. How do you cure yam?

Correct: shade, ventilated, 7–14 days. Not sun — that cracks the tuber.

3. What is the best storage structure for yam?

Correct: yam barn. Traditional, effective, 3–6 months storage.

4. Why is the seed yam business the highest margin?

Correct: 2–3x price. But requires strict quality control.

5. When is the ceremonial peak for yam in West Africa?

Correct: November–December. Festival demand spikes prices 30–60%.
