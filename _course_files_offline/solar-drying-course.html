📚 Course Modules

Module 1: Why solar drying beats open sun drying▾
  • A solar dryer traps heat under clear plastic/glass, reaching 45–65°C vs ~30°C in open sun — drying in 1–2 days instead of 3–5
  • The enclosure keeps out flies, dust, rain and animals — the main causes of contaminated dried food
  • Faster drying means less time in the spoilage zone and better colour and vitamins
🛠️ Practical task: Dry two identical batches of sliced tomato: one open-sun, one under a clear plastic sheet raised on a frame. Compare speed and cleanliness.
Module 2: Building a direct solar dryer▾
  • Basic cabinet dryer: black-painted box, clear cover at ~20–30° tilt toward the sun, mesh trays, vent holes low (air in) and high (moist air out)
  • Materials: timber or brick frame, clear UV plastic or old window glass, mosquito mesh trays, black paint
  • Size guide: 1 m² of tray area dries roughly 4–8 kg of fresh produce per load
🛠️ Practical task: Build a 1 m² cabinet dryer using the design above. Total materials should cost under $30 — apply the Iron-Clad Price Check on the plastic and mesh.
Module 3: Preparing produce▾
  • Wash, then slice evenly: tomatoes 5–8 mm, mango 5 mm, leafy greens whole
  • Blanch vegetables 1–3 minutes in boiling water to keep colour and stop enzymes
  • Dip fruit in lemon juice solution to reduce browning
  • Load trays in a single layer, pieces not touching
🛠️ Practical task: Prepare and load one full tray each of a fruit and a vegetable, recording weights before drying.
Module 4: Managing the drying process▾
  • Target temperature 50–60°C; above 65°C food case-hardens (dry outside, wet inside)
  • Turn pieces every few hours; bring trays in at night or cover against dew
  • Vegetables are done when brittle; fruit when leathery and no juice when squeezed
  • Final moisture targets: vegetables ~10%, fruit 15–20%
🛠️ Practical task: Dry your loaded trays to completion, weighing every few hours and plotting weight loss.
Module 5: Packaging and storage▾
  • Cool completely before packing — warm food sweats and moulds in the bag
  • 'Condition' fruit: put in a jar for 4–7 days, shake daily; any condensation means dry further
  • Pack in airtight bags/jars, store dark and cool; well-dried produce keeps 6–12 months
🛠️ Practical task: Condition, pack and label one batch with product name, date and weight.
Module 6: The solar drying business▾
  • Weight loss is 80–90% for tomatoes — 10 kg fresh becomes about 1 kg dried, so dried products command premium prices
  • Best sellers across Africa: dried tomatoes, mango, kale/ulimi, chillies, okra, fish
  • Sell to shops, schools, and city markets in the off-season for 2–4× fresh-season prices
🛠️ Practical task: Calculate: if fresh tomatoes cost $0.50/kg and dried tomatoes sell at $6/kg, what is your gross profit per 10 kg fresh batch after $1 of packaging?

⚠️ Safety & legal essentials

  • Use food-grade or well-washed plastic; never dry on bare ground or old fertilizer sacks
  • Dry fish and meat require salting first — a solar dryer alone may not be fast enough to beat spoilage in humid weather
  • Check for mould before packing; one mouldy piece spoils the bag

📈 Turning it into a business

  • A $30 dryer paying back in 2–3 batches of dried tomato is common
  • Contract with farmers for gluts (harvest surplus) at low prices
  • Offer drying-as-a-service: farmers pay per kg to use your dryer

🧠 Self-check quiz

Q1. What temperature range should the dryer run at?
Show answer

50–60°C; above ~65°C causes case-hardening.

Q2. Why blanch vegetables before drying?
Show answer

It stops enzymes that cause browning and flavour loss, and speeds drying.

Q3. How do you test if fruit is dry enough?
Show answer

Leathery texture, no juice when squeezed, and no condensation during 4–7 days of conditioning in a jar.

Q4. Roughly how much dried product comes from 10 kg fresh tomatoes?
Show answer

About 1 kg — 80–90% is water.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Food, Mould and Chemicals

  • Mould is a health hazard, not a cosmetic problem. Some moulds produce aflatoxins and ochratoxins that survive cooking and cause liver damage and cancer. If you see mould, smell mustiness, or feel condensation inside a jar after 4â7 days of conditioning, discard the whole batch. Do not scrape and re-sell.
  • Never dry on bare ground, tarpaulin, or old fertiliser/cement sacks. These carry chemical residues, animal waste and dust. Use food-grade mesh or clean stainless/tin trays only.
  • Fish and meat need salt-curing first. A simple solar dryer may not reach safe temperatures fast enough in humid weather. Follow your national food-safety authority rules for cured fish and meat.
  • Blanching water and lemon dips: use clean, treated water. Never reuse dip solution across batches â it becomes a bacterial bath.
  • Pesticide residue: if you buy from farmers, ask about spray intervals. Wash all produce in clean water before slicing. Do not dry produce still wet with spray.
  • Check your national regulations (e.g. Zimbabwe's Food and Food Standards Act, Kenya's KEBS, Nigeria's NAFDAC) before selling packaged dried food. Unregistered food sales can be seized and fined.
