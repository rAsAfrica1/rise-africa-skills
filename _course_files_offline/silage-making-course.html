Practical Reference

This reference block is built for operators who already understand the silage process and now need numbers they can act on. Silage is a margin business: every percentage point of dry matter (DM) lost between field and feed trough is money you paid for and never sold. The figures below come from published research and official extension guidance, and they are the benchmarks you should hold your own operation against.

Table 1: Silage Storage Losses by Method and Management Level

Storage Method Typical DM Loss Best-Case DM Loss Main Loss Driver
Bunker / pit, well sealed8-12%5-7%Surface spoilage at feed-out face
Bunker / pit, poorly sealed20-30%15%Aerobic spoilage, rain ingress
Tower / upright silo10-15%8%Top-layer spoilage, unloading losses
Bag / tube silage5-10%3-5%Bag puncture, UV degradation
Round bale wrapped8-15%6%Number of wrap layers, handling tears

Source: FAO/CAF, Silage Making for Small Scale Farmers (2012); Teagasc, Silage Losses and Preservation (2020); Wilkinson & Davies, The Aerobic Stability of Silage (2013).

Business reading: On a 3-hectare maize silage crop yielding 40 tonnes fresh weight per hectare, a shift from poor sealing (25% loss) to good sealing (10% loss) preserves roughly 18 tonnes of fresh silage. At a replacement cost of US$60 per tonne of fresh forage, that is about US$1,080 recovered per season from a few hours of extra labour and the cost of a proper plastic cover. That is the entire argument for doing compaction and sealing properly.

Table 2: Target Nutrient and Fermentation Benchmarks for Maize Silage

Parameter Target Range Action if Outside Range
Dry matter (DM)30-35%Below 28%: wilt longer before ensiling. Above 40%: chop finer, add water at ensiling
pH3.8-4.2Above 4.5: insufficient fermentation, check compaction and sugar content
Crude protein (% DM)7-9%Below 7%: harvest at earlier stage or supplement with protein concentrate
Metabolisable energy (MJ/kg DM)10.0-11.5Below 10: crop was too mature at harvest; use for maintenance feeding only
Neutral detergent fibre (NDF)40-50%Above 55%: low intake, blend with higher-energy feed
Lactic acid (% DM)4-7%Below 3%: poor fermentation, review chop length and sealing
Butyric acid (% DM)<0.1%Above 0.5%: clostridial fermentation, do not feed to lactating cows

â  SAFETY WARNING â Silage Gas and Mould Risk

Never enter a sealed pit, bunker or silo within the first 21 days of fermentation. Silage produces nitrogen dioxide, carbon dioxide and other gases that displace oxygen and can cause instant unconsciousness and death. In Zimbabwe, Kenya and South Africa there are recorded farm fatalities from entering freshly filled silage pits. Always ventilate for at least 30 minutes before entry, work in pairs with a rope and harness, and never enter alone. Separately, do not feed visibly mouldy or musty silage to dairy cows â mycotoxins pass into milk and can cause milk rejection at the processor and long-term herd health damage. Discard the spoiled layer; do not blend it into the ration.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Harvest at the right DM. For maize silage, target 30-35% whole-plant DM â check the milk line on the kernel; for grass, wilt 12-24 hours to 30% DM.
  • Chop length 1-2 cm for maize, 2-4 cm for grass. Longer chop traps air and raises pH; shorter chop risks rumen acidosis. Set the chopper and check it, do not assume.
  • Compact in thin layers. Fill in 15-20 cm layers and roll each layer with a heavy tractor. Target density above 700 kg fresh weight per cubic metre for maize.
  • Seal within 24 hours of starting to fill. Use a proper oxygen-barrier plastic (0.04-0.10 mm), overlap sheets by at least 1 metre, and weight the whole surface with tyres or sandbags â not just the edges.
  • Do not open for at least 21 days. Maize silage needs 21-30 days for stable fermentation. Opening early restarts aerobic spoilage across the whole face.
  • Feed out at 15-20 cm per day minimum. A slow feed-out face heats and spoils. Keep the face tight, vertical and clean; remove loose silage before each new cut.
  • Test every batch. Submit one sample per pit or bag for DM, pH and crude protein. A laboratory analysis costs far less than a season of feeding the wrong ration.
  • Record everything. Log date cut, DM at harvest, chop length, tonnes ensiled, date sealed, date opened, and any spoilage observed. Two seasons of records will tell you exactly where your losses come from.
