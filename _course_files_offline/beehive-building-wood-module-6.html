Module 6

Frame Making and Foundation

A frame is a small piece of wood that has to hold a comb full of honey — sometimes 3 kg of it — without sagging, breaking, or being glued in place by propolis. This module is how to make frames that last.

  • Cut and assemble frames that are square and true
  • Choose between wax foundation, plastic foundation and starter strips

Lesson 6.1

What a frame must do

A frame is the basic unit of a Langstroth hive. It has four jobs:

  1. Hold comb. Support a sheet of wax or a starter strip that the bees draw into comb.
  2. Support weight. A full brood frame weighs 2–3 kg. A full honey super frame weighs 2.5–3.5 kg. The frame must not sag or break under this.
  3. Maintain bee space. 35 mm centre-to-centre. Frames that are too close get propolised; too far apart and the bees build burr comb.
  4. Lift out cleanly. The beekeeper must be able to pull the frame without tearing comb or jamming it.

Every dimension is chosen to serve one of these four jobs. A frame that fails any of them costs you time, comb, or the colony.

The frame rule: square, bee-space correct, foundation properly fixed, spacing uniform. Four things. Miss one and the frame will annoy you every inspection for the life of the hive.

The top bar

The top bar has extended ends (10 mm longer on each side than the frame body) that rest on the box rabbet. Cut these ends carefully — they must be exactly the same width as the rabbet or the frame will not sit level.

Some top bars have a groove in the bottom centre for foundation. Others are plain. A grooved bar is better for wax foundation, a plain bar works with starter strips.

The side bar

The side bar has a notch at the bottom to accept the bottom bar. Cut this notch accurately — a loose notch makes the frame rattle and warp.

The top of the side bar has a small extension that sits against the top bar. This gives the frame its rigidity.

Assembly

  1. Glue and nail the top bar to both side bars (2 nails per joint).
  2. Fit the bottom bar, glued and nailed at each end.
  3. Check for square — the diagonals must be equal to within 2 mm.
  4. Do not paint or treat the frame. Bees touch every surface.
Use waterproof glue. Interior PVA glue fails in the humidity of a hive. Exterior-grade glue or polyurethane glue is required. Otherwise the frame falls apart in 6–12 months.

Lesson 6.3

Foundation — three options

Wax foundation

A thin sheet of pure beeswax, embossed with the base of worker cells. Gives bees a starting pattern so they draw straight, uniform comb.

  • Wired wax: has vertical wires embedded. Strongest. Recommended for brood frames.
  • Unwired wax: thinner, cheaper, only for super frames (honey comb), not brood.
  • Cost: $1–3 per sheet.
  • Downside: wax moth eats it in storage. Handle carefully — it cracks if cold.

Plastic foundation

A sheet of food-grade plastic, embossed with cell bases. Longer-lasting, doesn't crack, doesn't attract moths.

  • Cost: $0.80–2 per sheet.
  • Downside: some African bee races are slow to accept plastic. In dry conditions they may refuse to draw comb on it.

Starter strips

A 15–20 mm strip of wax at the top of the frame. Bees draw comb downward from it. There is no full sheet — the bees build the entire comb themselves.

  • Cost: lowest.
  • Downside: bees may build cross-comb or drone comb. Requires the beekeeper to check frequently in the first weeks.
  • Best for: African bees in areas where wax foundation is unavailable or expensive. Also the traditional method for top-bar hives.

Which to choose

  • Brood frames: wired wax foundation if available; starter strips if not.
  • Super frames (honey): unwired wax, or starter strips.
  • Large commercial apiaries: plastic foundation for durability.
  • Small apiaries: wax foundation.

Lesson 6.4

Waxing and wiring foundation

Wiring (for unwired wax foundation)

  1. Lay the side bars on a flat surface.
  2. Run horizontal wires across the frame (4 wires for brood, 3 for super), spaced evenly.
  3. Fix the wire to the side bars — with a staple or by wrapping around the side bar.
  4. Tension the wire firmly. Slack wire lets the comb sag.

Waxing the foundation in

  1. Place the sheet of foundation into the frame, seating the bottom edge in the bottom bar groove.
  2. For wired foundation: press the horizontal wires into the wax with a spur embedder or a warm knife.
  3. For unwired foundation with wire: heat a 12 V transformer tool or a warmed knife to melt the wax onto the wire at 2–3 points per wire.
  4. Check the foundation is flat and not sagging. A warped foundation produces crooked comb.

The correct tool is a spur embedder — a small wheel with points that presses the wire into the wax. They cost $10–30 and save enormous frustration. A warm knife works if you do not have a spur embedder.

Lesson 6.5

Frames in the box — spacing and management

Correct spacing

Frames hang at 35 mm centres. In a 10-frame box, this gives 8 mm gap at each end (bee space) and 35 mm between frames.

Spacing is set by the top bar contact points. Do not force extra frames into a box or spread them out — the bees will build burr comb in any gap larger than 10 mm.

The 9-frame alternative

Some beekeepers use 9 frames in a 10-frame box, wider apart. This produces thicker comb, easier to uncap, more honey per frame. But it requires a spacer tool and patience. For a beginner, stick to 10-frame spacing.

The three ways frames fail

  1. Sagging comb. Foundation not properly wired or attached. Comb bends under weight and eventually falls. Prevention: wire correctly, keep the hive level, don't overspray with starter strips too early.
  2. Propolis lockdown. Frames too close to the box wall (bee space too small). The bees propolise the frame to the wall. Prevention: correct rabbet dimension and correct frame length.
  3. Warped frame. The frame was built from unseasoned timber or not square. It warps as it dries. Prevention: seasoned timber, check square during assembly.
The frame rule: square, seasoned, waterproof glue, wired foundation, 35 mm spacing, bee space at the walls. Get all six and frames lift out cleanly for the life of the hive.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. What is the correct spacing between frames in a Langstroth hive?

Correct: 35 mm. Centre-to-centre. Bee space standard.

2. Why must waterproof glue be used for frames?

Correct: hive humidity. Interior glue fails in the moisture of a living colony.

3. Which foundation is strongest for brood frames?

Correct: wired wax. The vertical wires stop the comb sagging under brood weight.

4. What happens if frames are too close to the box wall?

Correct: propolis. Below 5 mm gap, the bees glue the frame in place.

5. Why is a warped frame a problem?

Correct: breaks bee space and comb. Warp is caused by unseasoned timber or out-of-square assembly.
