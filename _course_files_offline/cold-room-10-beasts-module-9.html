Module 9

Commissioning and Loading

The room is built. Now comes the ten days that decide whether it works: pressure test, vacuum, charge, pull-down test, first load, and the discipline that turns a finished room into a functioning cold chain.

Module 9 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Commission the refrigeration system safely and thoroughly
  • Run a pull-down test and document the result
  • First-load the room without overwhelming the unit
  • Set up the monitoring that catches problems in the first month
  • Handle the first carcass correctly from slaughter to chilled
  • Produce a commissioning record for your own cold room

Lesson 9.1

The ten-day commissioning sequence

Commissioning is not "turn it on and see if it works". It is a defined sequence with tests and records at each stage. Done properly, it catches problems while the installer is still on site. Done badly, it passes the defects on to you.

DayTaskEvidence
1Pressure test the refrigerant circuitRecorded pressure held for 24 hours
2Vacuum the system to under 500 micronsVacuum gauge reading, hold time
2Charge with the correct refrigerant weightWeight charged, from cylinder
3Functional test — compressor, fans, defrost, controlsWritten checklist
4Empty pull-down testTemperature log from ambient to target
5Fix any issues found. Repeat as needed.Updated log
6–7Load a partial load (5 carcasses) and record recoveryTemperature log
8–9Full load test (10 carcasses) and record recoveryTemperature log, time to recovery
10Hand-over, training, documentationSigned commissioning record
Never skip the pressure test or the vacuum. A system with moisture in it destroys the compressor in weeks. A system with a leak loses its charge and destroys the food. Both failures are entirely preventable and both are common when installers are rushed.

Lesson 9.2

The pull-down test

The pull-down test is where you prove the room can reach temperature with no product, then hold it. This is the base test.

Procedure

  1. Room empty, door closed, all lights off.
  2. Start the unit. Note the time and the starting temperature (typically 25–30 °C if warm).
  3. Log the temperature every 30 minutes until it reaches 2 °C and holds for 2 hours.
  4. Note the time from start to target. A well-sized unit on a well-built room takes 6–12 hours. If it takes 24+ hours, something is undersized or the envelope is failing.

What the numbers tell you

ResultInterpretation
Reaches 2 °C in 6–12 hours, holdsCorrect. System is well matched.
Reaches 2 °C in 12–20 hoursAcceptable but unit is at the limit. Watch the loaded test carefully.
Takes over 24 hoursProblem: undersized unit, envelope fault, or undercharged system. Do not proceed to loaded test until resolved.
Reaches 2 °C but cannot holdProblem: envelope leak (door gasket, penetrations), or door left open during test.

Lesson 9.3

The loaded test

The loaded test proves the room can handle real carcasses. This is where the sizing calculation meets reality.

Partial load (5 carcasses, ~1,250 kg)

  1. Room already at 2 °C.
  2. Introduce 5 warm carcasses (38 °C at surface, immediately after slaughter).
  3. Log the temperature every 30 minutes.
  4. Record the time to return to 2 °C.

Expected recovery: 6–10 hours for 5 carcasses in a well-sized room.

Full load (10 carcasses, ~2,500 kg)

  1. Room back at 2 °C.
  2. Introduce 10 warm carcasses.
  3. Log the temperature every 30 minutes for the first 4 hours, then hourly.
  4. Record the peak temperature and the time to return to 2 °C.

Expected: peak temperature rises to 8–12 °C during loading. Recovery to 2 °C in 12–18 hours. If recovery is longer, or the peak exceeds 15 °C, the unit is undersized or the loading practice needs to change (split the load, stagger carcasses in).

The loaded test rule: the loaded test is the moment of truth. Every number that matters — compressor duty cycle, recovery time, peak temperature — becomes visible. If the room passes this test, it will work in production. If it struggles, fix it now while the installer is still on site.

Lesson 9.4

First-load discipline

Most cold rooms that fail on their first real load fail because of overloading and loading practice, not because of any equipment fault.

The three loading rules

  1. Never load more than the design load. A 10-carcass room holds 10 carcasses. The eleventh causes a temperature rise that stresses the compressor and slows the whole room.
  2. Do not block the airflow. The evaporator blows air along the ceiling or wall. Carcasses hung too close to the evaporator block the circulation. Leave 300–500 mm clearance between the top of the hanging carcasses and the unit.
  3. Stagger warm loads if possible. If you must load 10 warm carcasses, hang them in two groups 30–60 minutes apart. This lets the unit recover partially between groups, avoiding a peak temperature.

The carcass temperature at entry

  • Ideal: carcass surface temp 15–20 °C at entry (after 2–4 hours of initial chilling or hanging in a cool area).
  • Acceptable: 25–30 °C (fresh off the kill floor).
  • Not acceptable: over 35 °C (hot carcass straight from the kill). This overloads the room and stresses the compressor.

Where possible, hang the carcass in a shaded, ventilated pre-chill area for 2–4 hours before entering the cold room. The pre-chill removes 30–50% of the heat load before it hits the evaporator.

Loading pattern

Hang carcasses from the far end of the room inward, not from the door inward. This keeps the near-door area clear for the next carcass and prevents the entrance zone from becoming a traffic-jam of warm carcasses blocking airflow.

Lesson 9.5

First-month monitoring

The first month tells you how the room actually behaves. Set up monitoring for the period.

What to record daily for 30 days

  • Room temperature at 6 a.m. and 6 p.m.
  • Compressor run time (approximate hours per day — install an hour meter if possible)
  • Door open count (an approximate tally)
  • External temperature (a cheap max-min thermometer outside)
  • Any alarm event, with time and duration
  • Any unusual noise, smell, or frost pattern

What to look for in the first month's data

PatternMeaningAction
Temperature rising slowly over the monthDoor gasket failing, or system slowly losing chargeCheck gasket; call refrigeration technician
Compressor running 20+ hours/dayUnit at or beyond capacityInvestigate envelope, loading practice, or unit sizing
Frequent alarms in afternoonPeak ambient heat loadShade condenser, check sun exposure
Ice on the evaporator not clearingDefrost cycle too short, or too infrequentAdjust defrost timer settings
Wet floor near doorCondensation on door or gasket failureCheck door seal, install strip curtain if missing

The 30-day review

At day 30, sit down with the log. Compute: average daily compressor hours, average room temperature, peak temperature, and number of alarm events. This is your baseline for the life of the room. Every future anomaly will be compared against it.

The monitoring rule: the first 30 days of records are the most valuable data the room will ever produce. They show you what "normal" looks like. Do not skip them.

Knowledge check — Module 9

1. Why must the refrigeration circuit be pressure-tested before charging?

Correct: find leaks. A system with a leak loses charge and destroys food. Testing first is the only safe order.

2. How long should an empty pull-down test take in a well-sized room?

Correct: 6–12 hours. From ambient to 2 °C. Longer means undersized or envelope fault.

3. What should the loaded test peak temperature not exceed?

Correct: 15 °C. Peak should be 8–12 °C. Above 15 °C means the unit is undersized or loading practice needs changing.

4. Why should carcasses be pre-chilled before entering the cold room?

Correct: remove heat load before the room. A hot carcass straight into the room overloads the unit and stresses the compressor.

5. What is the most valuable data the room will produce?

Correct: first 30 days. This defines the baseline. Every future anomaly compares against it.

Practical assignment — Module 9

Produce a commissioning record for your cold room.

  1. Your pressure test result — pressure, hold time, ambient temperature.
  2. Your vacuum record — microns achieved, hold time.
  3. Your refrigerant charge weight and type.
  4. Your empty pull-down log — time to reach 2 °C, and confirmation it held for 2 hours.
  5. Your partial load test (5 carcasses) — log and recovery time.
  6. Your full load test (10 carcasses) — log, peak temperature, recovery time.
  7. Your loading procedure as a written checklist for the staff.
  8. Your 30-day monitoring plan — what will be recorded, by whom, when reviewed.
  9. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest commissioning risk in your build and how it was resolved.

Marks are weighted toward points 6 and 9. The full load test is the moment of truth; the paragraph is where you commit to what you learned.
