Lesson 1

The Pegging Law — What Makes Groundnut Unique

In this lesson
  • Understand the pegging stage
  • Recognise what interrupts pegging
  • Protect the peg from damage

After flowering, groundnut sends a specialised stem — the peg — down from the flower into the soil. At the tip of the peg, the pod forms and fills. This is unique to groundnut. No other major crop forms its fruit underground.

Pegging usually begins 30–45 days after planting, peaks at 45–60 days, and continues until about 90 days. The pegs are fragile. Any of the following interrupts pegging and reduces pod number:

  • Soil compaction or crusting — the peg cannot penetrate
  • Very dry soil — the peg dries out and fails
  • Waterlogged soil — the peg rots
  • Weeding with deep hoeing — cuts the pegs
  • Low calcium in the pegging zone

Module 3 is essentially about protecting the peg. Every technique here exists to keep the pegging zone moist, loose, calcium-rich and undisturbed.

Lesson 2

Calcium and Gypsum at Pegging

In this lesson
  • Understand why groundnut has a unique calcium requirement
  • Apply gypsum at the correct rate and time
  • Recognise calcium deficiency

Groundnut pods absorb calcium directly from the soil around them, not through the roots. This means the calcium must be present at the pegging zone, not just anywhere in the soil profile. This is why groundnut responds to gypsum while other crops do not.

Gypsum application

  • Rate: 400–800 kg/ha for acidic soils; 200–400 kg/ha for neutral to alkaline soils
  • Timing: applied at flowering, broadcast over the rows, or just before flowering begins
  • Product: agricultural gypsum (calcium sulphate), not lime. Lime corrects pH but does not move down to the pegging zone.

Signs of calcium deficiency

  • Small pods with unfilled seed
  • Dark patches on the inside of the shell ("black heart")
  • Reduced pod set — many pegs but few pods
The rule: gypsum is the single most-skipped input in African groundnut farming. It is also the cheapest and most directly linked to yield. 400 kg/ha of gypsum at the right time can add 0.5–1 t/ha to a crop.
Lesson 3

Phosphorus, Potassium and Trace Elements

In this lesson
  • Apply phosphorus at planting for root and pod development
  • Apply potassium for pod fill and seed quality
  • Watch for common trace element deficiencies

Phosphorus

Apply DAP or TSP at planting: 100–150 kg/ha in the seed furrow, 5 cm below and to the side of the seed. Phosphorus drives root development and pod filling.

Potassium

Apply SOP or MOP at 80–120 kg/ha, split between planting and early pod fill. Potassium improves seed fill and shell strength.

Trace elements

  • Boron: deficiency causes "hollow heart" in seed. Foliar spray of 0.2% borax at flowering if suspected.
  • Molybdenum: supports nitrogen fixation. Foliar spray rarely needed if pH is above 6.0.
  • Iron: common deficiency in calcareous soils. Foliar iron chelate if leaves yellow between veins.

Organic alternatives

5–8 t/ha well-rotted manure, wood ash (for potassium), and bone meal (for phosphorus and calcium) can cover most needs in low-input systems.

Lesson 4

Water Needs by Stage

In this lesson
  • Match water to the four critical stages
  • Understand drought effects at each stage
  • Time irrigation for maximum benefit

Water need by stage

StageDays after plantingWater needDrought effect
Establishment0–20ModerateUneven stand, seed rot
Vegetative20–40ModerateSmall plants, fewer flowers
Flowering and pegging40–70CriticalFew pegs, low pod set
Pod fill70–110CriticalEmpty pods, aflatoxin risk
Maturity110+LowDelayed maturity

The two critical stages are pegging and pod fill. Water stress here is the direct cause of small pods and empty shells. If you have irrigation, water here first.

Waterlogging

Groundnut is more sensitive to waterlogging than maize or sorghum. Pods rot if the soil is saturated for more than 3 days. Ensure good drainage; use ridges if your soil is heavy.

Lesson 5

Weeding and the Critical Window

In this lesson
  • Time the two weedings before pegging
  • Weed without damaging pegs
  • Use mulch to reduce labour

Groundnut is a poor competitor against weeds, and weeds do the most damage in the first 6 weeks. After pegging begins, any deep cultivation damages pegs and reduces yield.

Critical weeding window: weeks 1–6

  1. First weeding: 2–3 weeks after emergence. Shallow hoe between rows, hand-pull within the row.
  2. Second weeding: 5–6 weeks after emergence. Before flowering starts and pegs form.

After 6 weeks, hand-pull any large weeds but do not cultivate. The pegs are forming.

Mulching

A 4–6 cm mulch of dry grass between rows reduces weeds by 50%, keeps the pegging zone moist, and keeps the soil loose. Excellent for groundnut, especially in dry areas.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. What is pegging?

Correct: pegs are stems that carry the pod into the soil. Unique to groundnut.

2. Why does groundnut need gypsum?

Correct: pods absorb calcium directly. The calcium must be in the pegging zone.

3. When should gypsum be applied?

Correct: at flowering. Before or as pegs begin to form.

4. What does drought during pegging cause?

Correct: pegs fail. Pegging and pod fill are the two most critical water stages.

5. When should the last weeding be done?

Correct: before 6 weeks. After pegging, cultivation damages the crop.

Module 3 capstone

Write your pegging and fertility plan: (1) gypsum rate and application date; (2) phosphorus and potassium plan; (3) water source and irrigation schedule for pegging and pod fill; (4) weeding dates; (5) mulch plan.
