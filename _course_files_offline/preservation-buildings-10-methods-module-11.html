Module 11 — Method 10: Oil, Salt and Brine Preservation

Water activity, salt, acid and the single greatest hazard in a home preserving kitchen — oil without acid.

Home › Preservation Buildings — 10 Methods › Module 11

Learning outcomes

  • Explain water activity and how salt, oil and brine reduce it.
  • Apply the correct salt concentration for dry-salting, brining and curing.
  • Preserve vegetables, fish, meat, olives and citrus safely.
  • Recognise — and avoid — the botulism hazard of oil-packed products.
  • Design a curing and salting room for safe, repeatable production.
  • Store and label these products to regulation.

1. What salt, brine and oil actually do

All three reduce water activity (aw) — the amount of water in the food that microorganisms can actually use. They do it by different mechanisms, and they are not interchangeable.

MethodMechanismaw achievedMain hazard
Dry saltingSalt draws water out of the food by osmosis0.75–0.85Under-salting in the centre of thick pieces
BriningSalt in solution moves into the food until equilibrium0.85–0.92Brine too weak; time too short
Salt + acid (pickle)Salt plus acid drops pH below 4.60.85–0.92, pH 3–4Acid not reaching the centre
Oil packing (with acid)Oil excludes air; the acid does the preservingpH below 4.6Safe — the acid is doing the work
Oil packing (no acid)Oil excludes air only — it does not reduce awUnchangedBotulism risk — never do this

The oil-packing hazard

Oil does not preserve food. It excludes air, which is exactly what Clostridium botulinum prefers — it is anaerobic. A garlic clove, a chilli or a herb covered in oil at room temperature is a textbook botulism risk. Cases in every decade come from home-made garlic-in-oil.

Rule: any oil-packed product must be either acidified (pH below 4.6), or refrigerated and used within days, or pressure-canned. Never store oil-packed garlic, herbs or vegetables at room temperature without acidification.

2. Salt preservation

Salt is the oldest preservative. There are three practical methods.

MethodSalt rateTimeProducts
Dry salt packing25–30 % of food weight1–4 weeksPreserved lemons, salt cod, salt pork, sauerkraut finish
Salt rub / dry cure2–3 % of meat weight, rubbed and turned3–14 daysBacon, ham, pancetta
Brine cureSalt in solution, 60–80 % saturation12 h – 5 daysFish, poultry, larger cuts

Preserved lemons — worked recipe

1 kg lemons, cut into quarters but not severed at the base.
Salt: 250 g (25 % of lemon weight).
Lemon juice: enough to cover, about 300 mL.
Optional: bay, peppercorns, cinnamon.

Method: pack salt into the cuts and between the quarters. Layer in a sterilised jar with the spices. Press down hard to release juice. Top with lemon juice to cover completely. Seal and leave 4 weeks at room temperature, turning the jar daily. Then refrigerate.

Storage: refrigerated, 12 months. The salt and acid together keep aw below 0.85 and pH below 4.0.

Dry-cured bacon — worked recipe

2 kg pork belly.
Salt: 45 g (2.25 % of meat weight)
Sugar: 25 g
Cure #1 (sodium nitrite 6.25 %): 5 g — follow the manufacturer's dose exactly
Black pepper, bay, juniper as desired.

Method: mix the cure. Rub all over the belly, including edges. Place in a non-reactive container, cover, refrigerate at 2–4 °C. Turn daily. Cure time: 7 days for a 4 cm thick belly.
After curing: rinse, pat dry, and either cold-smoke (Module 10) or hot-smoke, or leave unsmoked for a "green bacon".

Yield: about 1.6 kg after curing and drying — 20 % weight loss from water drawn out by the salt.

Nitrite is a poison in overdose

Cure #1 contains 6.25 % sodium nitrite. In its pure form, a teaspoon can kill an adult. Weigh it on a scale accurate to 0.1 g, use only the dose on the label, and store it in a clearly-labelled sealed container away from all other kitchen ingredients. Never substitute cure #1 for cure #2 or vice versa.

3. Brine preservation

Brining is salt in water. Strength is measured on a salinometer, by weight, or by the floating-egg test for a rough check (unreliable — use a proper measure).

ProductBrine strengthTimeNotes
Olives (bitter removal)10 % brine, changed weekly4–8 weeksLye method is faster but requires care
CapersDry salt, then 10 % brine4 weeksSalt draws bitterness; brine stores
AnchoviesDry salt, then oil pack6–12 monthsThe salt does the work; oil is for storage
Feta-style cheese7–10 % brine2 weeks – 6 monthsAcidified brine for longer storage
Corned beefBrine with 2.5 % nitrite cure5–10 daysRefrigerate throughout
Salt codDry salt, 25–30 %2–4 weeks, then dryTraditional method; drying completes the process

Brine strength calculation

How to make a 10 % brine

Brine % = salt weight / (salt + water weight) × 100

For 10 % brine in 20 L of water (20 kg):
Let S = salt weight.
S / (S + 20) = 0.10
S = 0.10 × (S + 20)
S = 0.10S + 2
0.90S = 2
S = 2.22 kg of salt

Do not make brine by "adding salt until it floats an egg" for anything you will sell. Weigh it.

4. Oil preservation — the safe way

Oil preservation works when combined with acid, salt or refrigeration. Three safe approaches:

  1. Acidified oil pack. Vegetables or herbs in vinegar or lemon juice first, then covered with oil. The acid does the preserving; the oil is only a barrier. pH must be below 4.6.
  2. Salted oil pack. Salted products (anchovies, preserved lemons) covered in oil. The salt does the work; the oil prevents oxidation and drying. Refrigerate.
  3. Refrigerated short-term. Fresh herbs, garlic or chilli in oil, stored in the fridge and used within 4 days. Not a preservation method — this is a fresh product.

Never store a fresh-garlic-in-oil jar on the shelf

Even acidified oil packs benefit from refrigeration. If in doubt about pH, test it with a meter. If you cannot test it, refrigerate it and treat it as short shelf life.

Buy a water activity meter if you will sell these products

aw and pH are the numbers regulators and buyers care about. A benchtop aw meter is expensive, but a local food-testing lab can measure it per batch at modest cost. Test each new product once, and every batch of cured meat and fish.

8. Storage, labelling and regulation

  • Cured meat and fish: refrigerated at 2–4 °C, or frozen for longer storage. Whole dry-cured hams may be stored cool and dry, wrapped in paper, not plastic.
  • Brine products: refrigerated, in the original brine; do not drain until use.
  • Oil-packed products: refrigerated; check the oil is clear and free of cloudiness before use.
  • Labels: product name, ingredients in descending order, net weight, business details, batch, best-before, storage instruction, allergen if any.
  • Regulation: salt-cured meat and fish products are usually food-processing activities subject to registration, inspection and, for products of animal origin, additional rules. Confirm with your local authority before selling.
  • Nitrite: limits and labelling vary; follow local rules. Keep the manufacturer's documentation on file.
  • Traceability: every batch record must show the raw material source, cure formulation, times and temperatures.
