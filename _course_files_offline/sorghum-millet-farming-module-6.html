Lesson 1

The Four Buyers Every Sorghum Farmer Should Know

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Name the four main buyer types for sorghum and millet
  • Match grain quality to the correct buyer
  • Understand the payment and specification of each buyer

Buyer 1 — The rural assembler

Buys small lots at farm gate, aggregates and re-sells to the urban market. Lowest price, immediate cash, no specification. Convenient for small lots, expensive for the farmer who has volume.

Buyer 2 — The urban miller

Buys grain for flour milling. Specification is modest: clean, dry, stone-free, one colour. Price 15–30% above assembler, payment on delivery or short credit. Volume requirement is real — often 5 t minimum per delivery.

Buyer 3 — The food processor

Buys for instant porridge, snack, or beverage manufacture. Specification is higher: uniform grain, food-grade cleanliness, moisture within 0.5% of target, sometimes a specific variety. Price 30–60% above assembler. Payment terms often 30 days.

Buyer 4 — The brewer or malting plant

Buys malting-grade sorghum. Specification is the highest: white low-tannin, high germination percentage, uniform, low broken-grain, tight moisture. Price 20–60% above food market. Payment via contract, often 30–60 days. Rejection on failed batches is real.

A farmer who can supply each buyer exactly what they want can sell a single crop into three different markets: the top grade to the brewer, the middle grade to the food processor, the working grade to the miller. The skill is in knowing the specification before the grain is harvested.

Lesson 2

Aggregation — Why Groups Sell Better Than Individuals

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand why buyers prefer volume over individual small lots
  • Set up or join a farmer group that aggregates for sale
  • Handle money, quality and disputes in a group sale

Most millers and food processors will not buy 500 kg lots. They will buy 20 tonnes. This single fact means a farmer with a good crop but no volume sells at assembler price and loses 20–40% of the potential revenue on every kilogram.

Aggregation solves this

A group of 20 farmers, each with 1 tonne, controls 20 tonnes. That volume is worth a contract negotiation. The group can approach a food processor directly, agree a price, and deliver in one truck.

Making it work

  1. Common quality standard — the group must agree moisture, cleanliness, and grain colour before pooling
  2. Single point of contact — a designated negotiator or committee, not 20 farmers each calling the buyer
  3. Written agreement — who brought what, at what grade, for what share of the sale
  4. One delivery — one truck, one invoice, one payment
  5. Transparent split — the buyer's money is divided according to grain delivered, not equally
  6. Dispute procedure — how to handle a rejected batch or a short payment

Agricultural cooperatives and farmer groups exist in most African countries and can often provide aggregation services, warehousing and contracts more cheaply than a group can do alone. Where a functioning cooperative exists, join it. Where it does not, form a small, tight group of trustworthy neighbours before trying to form something larger.

Lesson 3

Contracts and Out-Grower Schemes

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand what an out-grower contract covers
  • Read a contract for the risky clauses
  • Decide when a contract is worth signing and when it is not

What an out-grower contract typically covers

  • Quantity the farmer will deliver
  • Quality specification (variety, moisture, cleanliness)
  • Price (fixed, or formula-linked to a market index)
  • Delivery date and location
  • Input provision (seed, fertiliser) or not — and who pays
  • Payment terms
  • Penalties for failing to deliver or failing specification

Risky clauses to watch for

  • Input credit at inflated prices — the buyer provides seed and fertiliser at 40–60% above market, deducts from the crop price. Effectively borrows money at high cost.
  • One-sided rejection — the buyer can reject a batch without a clear standard and without a right of appeal.
  • No price floor — the price follows market but with a heavy discount clause that never triggers in the farmer's favour.
  • Exclusive delivery — the farmer must deliver the whole crop to the buyer even if the price collapses.
  • Delayed payment with no interest — 60–90 day payment, no late-payment penalty.

When a contract is worth signing

  • Inputs are provided at market price or below, with clear accounting
  • Price has a floor or is competitive at the time of signing
  • Specification is written and testable
  • There is a clear dispute resolution mechanism
  • Payment terms are reasonable (30 days or less, or with interest after)

Read the contract. If you do not understand a clause, ask. If you cannot get a clause changed, walk away — there is always another buyer.

Lesson 4

The Malting Business — Capital, Skill, Market

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand the basic stages of malting
  • Estimate the capital needed to start a small malting operation
  • Identify the market and the risks

The malting process in outline

  1. Steeping — soak clean white low-tannin sorghum in water for 24–36 hours
  2. Germination — spread in a thin layer, keep moist, allow 3–5 days of sprouting
  3. Kilning — dry and roast gently, controlling temperature and colour
  4. De-rooting — remove rootlets and defect grain
  5. Packing — for brewer, miller, or retail

Capital requirement

A micro-malting operation can start with $3,000–10,000: steeping tanks, germination floor space, a kiln (small gas or wood-fired, temperature-controlled), weighing and packing equipment, and working capital for the first grain purchase. Larger operations scale to $50,000+.

Skill and quality

Malting is unforgiving of poor temperature and moisture control. A batch that germinates unevenly, or is kilned at the wrong curve, will fail the brewer's specification and be rejected. The first 3–5 batches are learning batches. Do not sign a supply contract until you can reliably produce specification-grade malt.

Market

Buyers: the brewing industry (large commercial breweries, the growing craft brewing sector, and traditional sorghum beer producers), distillers, food processors using malt as an ingredient, and health-food markets where malt is sold as a sweetener or flour.

Payment: brewer contracts are often 30–60 days. Prices: $0.40–0.80/kg for finished malt in most African markets, versus $0.25–0.40/kg for raw grain.

Lesson 5

Costing a Dryland Cereal Crop

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Calculate the real cost per hectare of a sorghum or millet crop
  • Compare sorghum, millet and maize as competing uses of the same land
  • Decide next season's crop on numbers, not habit

Typical cost lines for 1 hectare of sorghum

Cost itemIndicative USD
Seed (improved, 5 kg/ha)$10–25
Manure / compost$0–40
Fertiliser (micro-dose + top-up)$40–90
Labour (land prep, sowing, thinning, weeding, harvest)$80–200
Striga pulling (2 passes)$10–30
Bird scaring (2 people × 2 weeks)$20–50
Threshing, drying, hermetic bags$30–60
Transport$10–40
Total cost per hectare$200–535

Revenue

At 2 t/ha saleable grain, at $0.30–0.50/kg = $600–1,000. Plus stover value $30–100/t = $90–500 (on 3–5 t/ha). Plus bran if milled.

Net margin per hectare: $200–700. Against a cost of $200–535, this is a solid but not spectacular return, which is why the difference between 1 t/ha (the African average) and 2.5 t/ha (achievable with management) is the whole business.

Comparison with maize

In good rainfall years, maize yields more per hectare and pays better. In drought years, maize fails and sorghum succeeds. Over five seasons including one or two droughts, sorghum outperforms maize as a risk-adjusted crop in most dryland areas. Farmers who grow both balance income across years; farmers who grow only maize accept the risk of a catastrophic failure every few seasons.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. Which buyer type pays the highest price for sorghum?

Correct: the brewer. 20–60% above the food market, if specification is met.

2. Why do groups sell better than individuals?

Correct: volume unlocks contracts. Millers buy 20 t, not 500 kg.

3. Which clause in an out-grower contract is a red flag?

Correct: input credit at 40–60% above market. Effectively borrowing money at a very high rate.

4. What type of sorghum does the malting market require?

Correct: white, low-tannin. Tannins interfere with germination and beer flavour.

5. Why do many dryland farmers grow both maize and sorghum?

Correct: risk balance. Sorghum is the drought insurance; maize is the upside.

Module 6 capstone

Build a one-page crop budget for the next sorghum or millet season. Include: (1) every cost line from land prep to delivery, with local prices; (2) your expected yield; (3) the price you will aim for and which buyer type that price is from; (4) your gross revenue and net margin; (5) whether you will sell raw, mill, or malt; (6) the one contract or buyer relationship you will establish before sowing.
