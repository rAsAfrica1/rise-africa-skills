â  SAFETY WARNING â Chemical and Biological Hazards in Silkworm Rearing

Do not spray insecticides, herbicides, or fungicides near rearing houses or mulberry plots. Silkworms are extremely sensitive to pyrethroids, organophosphates, and even household aerosols. A single drift event can kill an entire rearing batch within hours. Cases of total crop loss from nearby cotton or vegetable spraying are common in East and West Africa.

Mulberry leaf safety: Never feed leaves from plants sprayed within the last 21 days. Wash and dry leaves if contamination is suspected. Do not feed leaves from roadside or industrial-area mulberry.

Personal protection: Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling lime powder (used for tray disinfection) and when cleaning rearing houses. Lime burns skin and eyes. Ventilate enclosed rooms before re-entry.

Disease control: Pebrine, flacherie, and nuclear polyhedrosis virus spread fast in crowded trays. Burn or bury diseased larvae â do not compost. Disinfect trays with 2 % formalin or hot water between cycles. Formalin is a hazardous chemical: use outdoors, wear gloves and eye protection, and store locked away from children and food.

Food safety note: Silk pupae are sold as food in parts of Asia and increasingly in African urban markets. If you sell pupae for human consumption, follow national food hygiene regulations and do not use chemical treatments on cocoons intended for food use.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm market before eggs. Call at least three cocoon buyers and one reeler. Get a written price per kg. If no buyer exists within 100 km, do not start at scale.
  2. Secure mulberry first. A rearing box needs 500â650 kg of leaf per cycle. Plant or contract mulberry before you buy eggs.
  3. Build a dedicated rearing house. Separate room, screened windows, no chemical storage, easy to disinfect. Never rear silkworms in a kitchen or living room.
  4. Buy disease-free eggs. Source from a recognised grainage (e.g. ICIPE-supported centres in Kenya, or national sericulture stations). Cheap unknown eggs carry pebrine.
  5. Keep a daily record book. Temperature, humidity, leaf fed, mortality, and cocoon weight. Lenders and buyers trust records.
  6. Disinfect between cycles. Burn waste, wash trays, lime walls. Skipping this step is the number one cause of second-cycle failure.
  7. Mount on time. Move mature larvae to spinning frames when they stop eating and turn yellow. Late mounting reduces cocoon quality.
  8. Price per kg, not per basket. Weigh on a calibrated digital scale. Buyers who refuse weighing are usually underpaying.
  9. Reinvest 30 % of first-cycle revenue into trays, frames, and a second rearing house. Scale in steps, not in one leap.
