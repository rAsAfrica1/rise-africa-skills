Practical Reference

The DJ and event entertainment business in Africa is not a hobby market; it is a cash-flow market. A single well-booked wedding, corporate gala, or end-of-year party can gross more than a month of salaried work in many African cities. The operators who survive are not the best mixers â they are the best businesspeople. They know their cost per event, they price for profit, they protect their gear, they get deposits in writing, and they build a brand that promoters call first. This reference block gives you the hard numbers, the gear math, and the operating discipline to run entertainment as a business, not a side hustle.

â  SAFETY WARNING â ELECTRICAL, NOISE AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS

  • Electricity: Never run high-wattage sound and lighting from a single unfused socket or a daisy-chain of extension leads. Use RCD/earth-leakage protection, correctly rated cables, and a generator or inverter sized above your total load. Wet floors, outdoor events, and rain are lethal. Inspect every cable before every event.
  • Noise: Prolonged exposure above 85 dB damages hearing permanently. Wear musician's earplugs when setting up and during long sets. Position speakers away from young children and keep a sound-level limit agreed with the venue.
  • Batteries and chemicals: Lithium and lead-acid batteries can overheat, vent acid, or catch fire. Charge in a ventilated space, never under bedding or in a closed vehicle in the sun. Do not short terminals. Dispose of old batteries and bulbs through a licensed recycler â not in household waste or open pits.
  • Fog and haze fluids: Use only water-based, manufacturer-approved fluids. Never substitute kerosene, oil, or unknown mixtures. Keep the machine away from asthmatics and ventilate the room.
  • Fire and crowd: Keep a fire extinguisher at the rig, keep emergency exits clear, and secure all trusses, stands, and cables so they cannot fall into a crowd. Never overload a venue beyond its safe capacity.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Price for profit, not for popularity. Calculate cost per event (transport, crew, fuel, wear, power, commission) and add a minimum 55% margin before you quote.
  2. Take a 50% non-refundable deposit. No deposit, no booking. Issue a written contract with date, time, venue, duration, fee, deposit, cancellation terms, and overtime rate.
  3. Confirm the venue before you quote. Check power supply, load capacity, access, parking, noise limits, and whether the client or venue supplies sound and lights.
  4. Carry backup for every critical item. Spare cable, spare microphone, spare laptop or USB, and backup power. One failure can cost you the booking and your reputation.
  5. Protect your gear. Use cases, covers, and a locked, dry storage space. Insure or self-insure against theft, damage, and transport accidents.
  6. Track every event in a record book. Client, fee, deposit, costs, profit, and what worked. This data drives your pricing and your marketing.
  7. Build a repeatable brand. Consistent logo, social media presence, testimonial photos, and a simple one-page rate card. Ask every satisfied client for a review and a referral.
  8. Reinvest 20â30% of profit into gear and skills. Upgrade sound and lights before you upgrade your lifestyle. Capacity is what raises your fee ceiling.
