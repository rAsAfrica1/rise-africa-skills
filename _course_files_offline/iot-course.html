â  SAFETY WARNING â Electrical, Chemical & Food-Safety Hazards

IoT nodes combine mains power, batteries, water, and food-contact environments. Treat every one of the following as non-negotiable:

  • Mains wiring: Never wire a 220â240 V relay or pump contactor yourself unless you are a licensed electrician. In many African jurisdictions this is a legal requirement, and it is a life-safety requirement everywhere.
  • Batteries: Lithium (Li-ion/LiFePO4) cells can vent or catch fire if shorted, overcharged, or punctured. Use a proper charge controller, fuse every positive lead within 20 cm of the battery terminal, and never charge a swollen cell.
  • Food contact: Sensors placed inside cold rooms, milk tanks, or grain silos must have food-safe housings. A cracked probe leaching into milk or produce is a contamination event, not a data problem.
  • Chemicals: If your node triggers dosing of chlorine, sanitiser, or agrochemicals, fit a physical failsafe (mechanical timer or manual override). Software bugs must never be able to overdose a food or water stream.
  • Water + electricity: Use IP65+ enclosures and drip loops for any outdoor or washdown installation. GFCI/RCD protection is mandatory on any circuit near water.
  • Cold-chain integrity: An IoT monitor is an early-warning tool, not a compliance record. Keep a manual temperature log as backup for regulators and buyers.

Quick Reference Checklist

Run through this before you buy a single component. It will save you more money than any discount you negotiate.

  1. Define the business decision, not the sensor. Write down the exact action you will take when a threshold is crossed â "call the driver," "start the pump," "move the stock." If no action exists, don't install the sensor.
  2. Map power first. Calculate daily energy draw (sensor + MCU + radio Ã duty cycle) and size solar + battery for three days of autonomy. Grid-tied nodes still need surge protection and a UPS.
  3. Walk the site with a signal meter. Never trust a coverage map. Test Wi-Fi RSSI or GSM signal at the exact mounting point, at the exact height, in the wet season.
  4. Get three supplier quotes in writing. Include freight, duties, SIM/data bundles, and a spare unit. Budget 15â20% of hardware cost for spares and consumables.
  5. Design for offline. The node must buffer at least 7 days of readings locally and re-send on reconnect. Assume the network will drop.
  6. Set alert thresholds with hysteresis. A single threshold causes alert storms. Use e.g. alert at 8Â°C, clear at 6Â°C.
  7. Plan calibration and maintenance. Schedule sensor checks monthly, recalibration annually, and battery replacement at 70% of rated cycle life.
  8. Document the install. Photograph every connection, label every cable, and store the config file in two places. The person maintaining this in two years may not be you.
