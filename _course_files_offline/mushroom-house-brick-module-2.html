Module 2

Siting, Orientation and Site Drainage

A mushroom house on a bad site cannot be fixed. Water arrives from uphill, contamination arrives from neighbours, and heat arrives from the sun. Every one of those is a siting decision.

Module 2 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Select a mushroom house site by the five environmental criteria
  • Orient the house to control temperature swings through the day
  • Design a drainage system that keeps the house dry, including the wet pad around it
  • Plan the clean/dirty routes on the site before the walls go up
  • Recognise the specific contamination risks that make a site unsuitable
  • Produce a marked site plan for a mushroom house

Lesson 2.1

The five site criteria

A mushroom house is a controlled-environment building. The site must help control the environment, not fight it.

1. Level or gently sloped ground

The house floor must be level and dry. Slope the site away from the house on all sides. On a steep slope, cut a level pad and provide a retaining wall at the cut — do not build on sloping ground and fill underneath.

2. Well-drained soil

Water table must be at least 800 mm below the floor. If the site holds water after rain, do not build. A mushroom house in wet ground is a contamination magnet — wet walls grow mould, wet floors grow bacteria, and wet substrate fails.

3. Away from other agricultural buildings

Mushroom houses should be at least 30 m from livestock pens, poultry houses, compost heaps, or any source of airborne spores. Mushroom contamination is caused by competing moulds, and livestock areas are full of them.

4. Shaded from afternoon sun, exposed to morning sun

The house absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night. Afternoon sun pushes the interior above the mushroom's tolerance. Morning sun warms the house after a cool night and encourages fruiting. Orient the long axis east-west and shade the west wall.

5. Accessible for substrate delivery

Fresh substrate arrives as straw bales, sawdust bags or compost. A truck or a wheelbarrow must reach the substrate storage and pasteurisation area without crossing the clean grow-room side.

The siting rule: if any of the five criteria fail, fix it or find another site. Mushrooms do not tolerate a bad site the way vegetables do. Contamination compounds over every cycle.

Lesson 2.2

Orientation and the daily temperature cycle

The mushroom house holds a stable interior temperature through the day. Orientation is the first line of that stability.

The long axis

Run the long axis of the house east-west. This presents the two long walls to the equator-facing and pole-facing directions, both of which receive high-angle sun that a modest roof overhang can shade. The two short walls face east and west, where low-angle morning and afternoon sun arrives. Only the short walls suffer, so the heat load is reduced.

The roof overhang

Extend the roof overhang by 600–800 mm on all sides. This shades the walls for most of the day in low latitudes, reduces heat gain, and keeps rain off the walls. On a brick house, the roof overhang is more valuable than roof insulation for the walls.

The thermal mass cycle

Brick walls absorb heat from the walls' exterior during the day and release it slowly at night, holding the interior temperature within a narrow band. For this to work, the walls must be shaded — a hot brick wall in full afternoon sun becomes a radiator inside the house at 9 p.m.

Lesson 2.3

Drainage — the wet pad and the dry pad

A mushroom house has two distinct areas: the dry side (grow room, spawn run, packing) and the wet side (substrate prep, pasteurisation, wash-down). Each has its own drainage.

The grow-room floor

  • Slope 1:100 toward a floor drain inside the grow room.
  • Drain routed to a grease trap and soakaway outside.
  • Floor material: smooth, sealed concrete, coved at the wall junction so no crevice remains.
  • Drain strainer to catch substrate fragments — a drain blocked by spent substrate is a real risk.

The pasteurisation pad

  • Sloped toward a separate drain that does not feed back into the grow room.
  • Wash-down water from pasteurisation is hot and organic — it must not pool near the grow room.
  • Concrete pad, sealed, with a grease trap if the substrate has grease content (uncommon — straw does not).

The perimeter

  • V-drain around the entire house, 200 mm deep and 300 mm wide, carrying surface water away.
  • Discharge point at least 10 m from the house, into a soakaway or existing drainage.
  • Do not let roof runoff fall onto the perimeter — install gutters and downpipes that discharge into the drain.

The 300 mm rule

The floor of the house must be at least 300 mm above the surrounding ground. This single decision prevents 90% of water ingress problems. If the site does not allow it, raise the pad with compacted rubble and a concrete slab.

Lesson 2.4

The clean/dirty route

Contamination control in mushroom growing is a physical layout problem, not a hygiene problem. If clean and dirty material share any part of the path, contamination happens eventually.

The clean/dirty rule

Dirty material (fresh substrate, spent substrate, contaminated bags) must never cross the path of clean material (pasteurised substrate, spawn-inoculated bags, harvested mushrooms).

Footwear and clothing

  • Boot wash at every transition from dirty to clean.
  • Different boots for the pasteurisation room and the grow room — do not mix.
  • Clean coats, changed at the clean/dirty transition.
  • Hand sanitiser at the grow-room entrance.
Contamination is cumulative. A single dirty boot in the grow room does not ruin the crop. Ten dirty boots over a season does. The clean/dirty route is a discipline, not a one-time feature.

Lesson 2.5

Site risks that make a location unsuitable

Some sites cannot be fixed. Do not build a mushroom house on any site that meets one of the following conditions:

  • Within 30 m of a poultry house or livestock pen. Airborne spores from these buildings overwhelm the mushroom house.
  • Downwind of a compost heap. Compost is a mushroom contaminant nursery.
  • On ground that floods once every 3 years. Mushroom houses cannot be mopped after a flood and returned to production.
  • On a site with no vehicle access. Substrate arrives in bulk. Carrying straw bales 200 m by hand is not viable.
  • In a frost pocket. Low-lying ground where cold air collects drops 5–8 °C below the surrounding area on frosty nights. A mushroom house at 12 °C when it should be 20 °C will not fruit.
  • Where the water source is unreliable. Mushrooms need a steady, clean water supply for humidity, misting, and wash-down. No water, no mushrooms.
The negative test: walk the site on a wet day, on a hot day, and on a windy day. If any of the six conditions above appear — or are likely — do not build there.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. What should the long axis of the mushroom house align with?

Correct: east-west. Reduces heat load by presenting only the short walls to punishing low-angle sun.

2. What is the 300 mm rule?

Correct: floor 300 mm above ground. The single decision that prevents 90% of water ingress problems.

3. Why must the mushroom house be at least 30 m from livestock?

Correct: airborne spores. Competing moulds from livestock overwhelm the mushroom grower.

4. What is the clean/dirty route rule?

Correct: never cross. A physical layout rule. Any crossing means eventual contamination.

5. Which of these is NOT a site disqualifier for a mushroom house?

Correct: near a vegetable garden. Vegetables do not produce airborne spores harmful to mushrooms. All other listed sites are disqualifying.

Practical assignment — Module 2

Produce a marked site plan and drainage scheme for a mushroom house.

  1. A plan drawing to approximate scale, north marked, showing the house footprint, the pasteurisation room, the substrate storage, and the dispatch area.
  2. Assessment of the five site criteria (level, drained, distance from livestock, orientation, access). State each as pass or fail.
  3. The clean/dirty route diagram with the pasteurisation room marked as the clean/dirty transition point.
  4. The floor level plan — how much the house floor will sit above the surrounding ground, and how you will achieve it.
  5. Drainage plan — floor drains inside, perimeter drain outside, discharge point.
  6. Roof overhang dimension and shade plan for the west wall.
  7. Footwear and clothing transition points marked on the plan.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) naming the biggest site risk and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 3 and 8. The clean/dirty route is the discipline that keeps the house productive; the paragraph is where you commit to a risk decision.
