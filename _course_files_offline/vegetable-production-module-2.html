Lesson 1

Why Tomato Justifies the Risk

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Understand the tomato market — why it always has a buyer
  • Read the yield range between poor and good management
  • Accept that tomato is a skill crop, not a luck crop

Tomato is the highest-grossing vegetable crop for smallholders almost everywhere in Africa. It is also the crop most likely to fail. Both facts are true, and both matter.

The market is universal: households, market vendors, restaurants, hotels, processors, and supermarkets all buy tomato. The price is real: $0.30–1.50/kg depending on the season, with peaks at $2+ during dry-season shortages in some urban markets. The yield range is wide: 10 t/ha under poor management, 40–80 t/ha under good management with irrigation and staking.

What separates the two? Not seed, not fertiliser, not luck. Management of the transplanting date, the trellising, the irrigation rhythm, and the disease-pest system. All four are taught in this course. The first three in this module, the fourth in Module 4.

Lesson 2

The Tomato Nursery — Site, Media, Sowing

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Build a nursery bed or seedling tray that produces strong transplants
  • Sterilise the media and protect against damping-off
  • Time the nursery so the transplant date matches your field readiness

Site

Near water, on flat or gently sloping ground, in partial shade for the first week (a shade net or a palm-frond roof). Full sun after day 7. Sheltered from heavy rain.

Media

A loose, fertile, well-drained mix. The classic recipe: 3 parts well-rotted compost, 1 part sand, 1 part sieved topsoil. Sterilise the topsoil by pouring boiling water over it and letting it cool — this kills the damping-off fungi that kill tomato seedlings in week one.

Trays or beds

Seedling trays with individual cells are the best — they avoid root disturbance at transplanting. Where trays are unavailable, a raised nursery bed 1 m wide, 15 cm high, is used. Sow in rows 10 cm apart, 1 cm deep, 3 cm between seeds. Cover with a fine layer of soil and a light mulch of dry grass.

Water and shade

Water twice a day with a fine rose (not a hard stream). Shade for 5–7 days. Remove shade gradually. Germination in 5–8 days; transplant at 3–4 weeks when seedlings have 4–5 true leaves and are 10–15 cm tall.

Timing

Work backwards from your target transplant date. Tomato nursery = 3–4 weeks. So if you want to transplant on 15 March, sow on 15–20 February.

Lesson 3

Transplanting — Timing, Depth, Water

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Transplant without killing the seedling
  • Space correctly for the variety
  • Water deeply at transplant — the one non-negotiable step

When to transplant

Late afternoon, or on a cloudy day. Never in the heat of midday. The seedling needs 12 hours of cool, moist conditions to recover before the sun hits it.

Depth

Plant 2–3 cm deeper than the seedling was in the tray — up to the first set of true leaves. Tomato stems produce roots along the buried section, giving the plant a stronger root system. This single technique increases yield by 10–20%.

Spacing

Determinate (bush) varieties: 60 × 45 cm. Indeterminate (vining) varieties that will be staked: 75 × 45 cm. In each case one plant per station. Plant population: 25,000–30,000 per hectare.

Water

Water each transplant with 500 ml–1 L immediately after planting. If the soil is dry, water the planting hole before setting the seedling. A transplant that wilts and does not recover within 24 hours is dead.

After-care in the first week

Water daily for the first week. If the sun is strong, shade each transplant with a small leaf or a piece of paper for the first 2 days. Do not fertilise during week one — the seedling is focused on root establishment, not leaf growth.

Lesson 4

Staking, Pruning and Support

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Decide between staked and unstaked tomato
  • Stake correctly for the variety
  • Prune to direct energy to fruit, not leaves

Staked vs unstaked

SystemYieldLabourFruit quality
UnstakedLowerLowGround contact — rot and soil splash
Staked40–60% higherHigh (staking + weekly tying)Cleaner, better price, less disease

For the market-fresh trade, stake. For processing tomato (paste, sauce), leave unstaked — mechanical harvest is common and staking does not pay back.

Staking method

Wooden stakes 1.5–2 m tall, driven 30 cm into the ground at each plant, or a trellis of horizontal wires between strong end-posts. Tie the tomato stem to the stake with soft twine or strips of cloth — never wire, which cuts the stem. Tie every 20–30 cm as the plant grows.

Pruning

Indeterminate varieties produce side shoots (suckers) in the leaf axils. Removing these directs the plant's energy into the main stem and fruit. Pinch them out with fingers or a knife when they are 2–5 cm long, weekly. Determinate varieties do not need pruning — leave all side shoots on.

Stop the main stem (remove the growing tip) two to three weeks before the end of the season to force the plant to concentrate on ripening existing fruit rather than producing more.

Lesson 5

Leafy-Green Beds — the Beginner Crop Done Well

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Set up a leafy-green bed for continuous harvest
  • Time plantings for weekly supply
  • Harvest cut-and-come-again without killing the plant

Leafy greens — kale, spinach, Swiss chard, collards, amaranth — are the fastest cash in vegetable farming. They germinate in 5–10 days, are ready for first harvest in 30–45 days, and continue producing for 2–4 months with weekly harvests.

Bed preparation

Raised beds 1 m wide, 20–30 cm high, spaced 40 cm apart. Mix in well-rotted compost at 5 kg per m². Rake level.

Spacing and sowing

Broadcast sow thinly, or drill in rows 20 cm apart. Thin to 15–20 cm between plants within 3 weeks. Thinned plants can be eaten or sold as baby greens.

Staggered planting

The mistake almost every beginner makes: planting all the greens on one day. Four weeks later, the entire bed is ready and most of it spoils unsold. The correct method is to plant 1/6 of the bed every 10 days. Six plantings in two months gives continuous harvest and continuous supply to buyers.

Cut-and-come-again harvest

Harvest the outer leaves only, using a sharp knife or scissors. Leave the growing tip and the inner 4–6 leaves to keep producing. Do not cut the whole plant — you kill it. A well-managed kale plant produces for 3–4 months with weekly harvests.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. When should you transplant tomato seedlings?

Correct: 4–5 true leaves at 3–4 weeks. Younger is delicate; older is stunted.

2. What is the correct transplanting depth for tomato?

Correct: 2–3 cm deeper. Tomato stems root along the buried section, giving a stronger root system.

3. When is the best time of day to transplant?

Correct: late afternoon or cloudy. The transplant needs cool, moist conditions to recover before sun.

4. Why stake tomato plants?

Correct: 40–60% higher yield and cleaner fruit. Staked fruit is off the ground, less rot, better presentation.

5. How should leafy-green beds be staggered?

Correct: 1/6 every 10 days. Continuous supply, no gluts, no waste.

Module 2 capstone

Draw your nursery and transplant plan for the next tomato and leafy-green crop. Include: (1) your nursery sowing date and expected transplant date; (2) your media mix and whether you will sterilise the topsoil; (3) your field layout (rows, spacing, path widths); (4) whether you will stake and why; (5) your staggered leafy-green planting calendar for the next 6 weeks.
