Module 8

Costing the Build and the Kill

Two numbers decide whether your cold room business survives. The first is what it costs to build and run the room. The second is what it costs to put a kilogram of saleable meat on the shelf. Get both wrong and no price is high enough.

Module 8 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Build a complete capital cost sheet for a 10-beast cold room
  • Calculate the running cost per month and per kg of meat chilled
  • Work out the true cost of a kill day
  • Set a meat price from cost, not from guessing
  • Identify the three line items that most often blow the budget
  • Produce a written cost model for your own cold room

Lesson 8.3

The cost per kill day

A kill day is a discrete production event. Every kill day has its own cost that must be recovered from the meat sold from that kill.

Direct costs per kill day (10 beasts)

LineIndicative USD
Live animals (10 × 400 kg × $2.00/kg)8,000
Transport of live animals200–500

Water and consumables30–60

Cleaning and sanitising15–30

Direct cost per kill day$8,525 – $9,190

Saleable weight per kill day

  • 10 beasts × 250 kg carcass = 2,500 kg carcass weight
  • Boneless yield 57%: 1,425 kg saleable boneless meat
  • Plus offal, bones, fat: another 300–400 kg of secondary product
  • Total saleable weight: ~1,750 kg

Cost per saleable kg

$8,800 ÷ 1,750 = $5.03/kg. That is the cost of the meat as it leaves the cold room, before any profit.

The reality check: if you paid $2.00/kg live and your cost per saleable kg is $5.03, then every kilogram you sell below $5.03 is a loss. This is the number that most small butchers never calculate — and it is the number that decides whether the business makes money.

Lesson 8.4

Pricing from cost, not from hope

The three costs

  • Direct cost per kg (kill day) — $5.03/kg in the example
  • Overhead cost per kg — monthly running cost ÷ monthly kg sold. At $800/month and 7,000 kg/month = $0.11/kg.
  • Total cost per kg — $5.14/kg in the example

The three price levels

  • Break-even price — $5.14/kg. Below this, you lose money.
  • Target price — 15–25% above break-even for the standard product. $5.90–6.40/kg.
  • Premium price — 40–60% above break-even for branded, packaged product sold to hotels and supermarkets. $7.20–8.20/kg.

Why most butchers lose money without knowing

They look at the price they paid for the animal. They think "I paid $2.00/kg, I sell at $6.00/kg, I make $4.00/kg." But they never did the division. In reality:

  • Dressing loss: 45% of live weight is not saleable meat
  • Chilling shrink: another 4%
  • Boning loss: another 30–40%
  • Offal and bones sell at lower price or not at all

The true cost is 2.5–3.5× the live purchase price. Every butcher who has not done this arithmetic is pricing against the wrong number.

The pricing rule: always price from cost per saleable kg, not from live purchase price per kg. These two numbers differ by a factor of three, and confusing them is the most common cause of failed butcheries.

Lesson 8.5

The three budget killers

1. Underestimating the envelope

Panels, door, vapour barrier, floor insulation. This line is 40–50% of the capital cost, and it is the one most often under-quoted. A cheap envelope means a high running cost forever.

2. Ignoring the backup power cost

Backup power is 20–30% of the capital cost in unreliable-grid areas, and 30–60% of the monthly running cost in generator-only sites. Business plans that leave this out show a profit that does not exist.

3. Assuming full yield

Small butchers assume they will sell every kilogram. Real-world: 5–15% is lost to trim, waste, discount, spoilage, theft. Price to cover 90% of theoretical yield, not 100%.

Knowledge check — Module 8

1. What is the correct contingency percentage for a cold room capital budget?

Correct: 15–25%. First-time builders always underestimate. Budget with a real contingency or run out of money halfway.

2. What is the largest variable in monthly running cost?

Correct: power cost. A grid-reliant room pays $80–250/month. A generator-reliant room pays $150–400 just in fuel.

3. Why does cost per saleable kg come out at 2.5–3.5× the live purchase price?

Correct: multiple losses. Dressing (55% yield), chilling (96%), boning (57%) — combined, only 30–40% of live weight becomes saleable prime meat.

4. What is the correct pricing approach?

Correct: cost-based pricing. Matching neighbours means matching their mistakes. Guessing means losing money without knowing.

5. What yield percentage should you price to cover?

Correct: 85–90%. Trim, waste, discount, spoilage, theft — every butchery loses 5–15%. Price to cover the real yield, not the theoretical one.

Practical assignment — Module 8

Build a full cost model for your cold room.

  1. Your complete capital cost sheet, with each line filled by a real supplier quotation (or an indicative figure clearly marked). Include 15% contingency.
  2. Your monthly running cost, with each line estimated from real local prices.
  3. Your direct cost per kill day, using your local live animal price and your slaughter cost.
  4. Your cost per saleable kg, with the arithmetic shown (dressing %, shrink %, boning %).
  5. Your break-even price, target price, and premium price.
  6. A comparison against two local butchers' current prices.
  7. One paragraph (150 words) stating whether your break-even price is above or below the local market price, and what that means for the business.

Marks are weighted toward points 4 and 7. The cost per saleable kg is the single most important number; the paragraph is where you decide whether the business works.
