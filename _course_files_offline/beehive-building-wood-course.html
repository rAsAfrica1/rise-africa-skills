â  SAFETY WARNING â Hive treatment, preservatives and chemicals

Timber preservatives and hive treatments can contaminate honey and kill bees. Treat this as a food-safety issue, not a carpentry issue.

  • Never treat the inside of a hive box, frame, top bar or feeder with any preservative, varnish, oil or pesticide. Beeswax and propolis absorb chemicals and transfer them directly into honey.
  • Only apply preservative to external surfaces â roof, stand legs, outer walls â and allow full curing (minimum 14 days, longer in humid conditions) before bees enter.
  • Do not use creosote, pentachlorophenol, chromated copper arsenate (CCA) or any arsenic-based timber treatment on hives. These are banned for food-contact use and are hazardous to the operator.
  • Wear gloves, eye protection and a respirator when applying any preservative. Work outdoors or in forced ventilation. Never eat, drink or smoke during application.
  • Keep all chemicals in original labelled containers, locked away from children and away from honey extraction and storage areas.
  • If you suspect honey contamination, do not sell it. Discard and clean the hive. Contaminated honey is a legal liability and destroys buyer trust permanently.
  • Follow your national pesticide and veterinary authority regulations. In most African jurisdictions, hive treatment chemicals are regulated under agricultural or food-safety law.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm your regional standard first. Measure a working hive from a local beekeeper or check your national beekeeping authority before cutting. A 5 mm error in bee space costs you a whole production run.
  2. Season timber to under 15% moisture content. Green timber shrinks after assembly, opens joints, and warps frames out of alignment. Air-dry for 3â6 months depending on species and climate.
  3. Cut all boxes to identical external dimensions. Interchangeability between brood boxes and supers is what makes a hive a system, not a pile of boxes. Jigs pay for themselves by box ten.
  4. Hold bee space at 6â9 mm everywhere. Check top bar to top bar, frame to wall, and box to box. This single dimension decides whether bees build straight comb or burr comb.
  5. Use hardwood for frames, softwood only for roofs and stands. Frames carry load and are handled constantly. Roofs need lightness and weather resistance, not strength.
  6. Treat only external surfaces and cure fully. No preservative ever touches the inside of a hive. 14-day minimum cure before bees enter.
  7. Fit galvanised sheet to every roof. Untreated timber roofs fail in two rainy seasons. This is the cheapest insurance in the whole build.
  8. Price the hive against the buyer's honey revenue, not your timber cost. A hive that produces 15â25 kg of honey per year at local market price is a revenue asset. Price it as one.
