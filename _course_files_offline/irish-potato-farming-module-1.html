Lesson 1

Why Potato Is the Highest-Value Staple

In this lesson
  • Explain why potato out-earns every cereal per hectare
  • Understand the 90–120 day cycle
  • See the cash-flow advantage

Irish potato yields 20–40 t/ha under good management — 10x what maize yields per hectare, at 2–3x the price per kg. A single hectare can gross $4,000–12,000 in 3–4 months. No cereal comes close.

The trade-off is risk. Potato is the most input-sensitive and disease-sensitive crop in African smallholder farming. Late blight can destroy a field in 10 days. Bacterial wilt is permanent in the soil once present. Seed quality decides 40–60% of yield.

Farmers who succeed with potato are the ones who treat it as a business from day one: certified seed, blight prevention, proper hilling, careful harvest, and storage or immediate sale.

Lesson 2

Site Selection — Cool, Well-Drained, Fertile

In this lesson
  • Understand potato's three environmental requirements
  • Choose a site that avoids the two big killers: heat and waterlogging
  • Avoid soil with bacterial wilt history

Temperature

Potato needs cool conditions. Optimum growing temperature is 15–22°C. Above 25°C, tuber formation slows; above 30°C, it stops. In tropical Africa, potato is grown at high altitude (1,500–3,000 m) or in the cool season in medium-altitude areas.

Soil

Well-drained, loose, deep loam or sandy loam. pH 5.0–6.5 (slightly acid suppresses common scab). Never plant in heavy clay or waterlogged soil — tuber rot will destroy the crop.

Rotation

Never plant potato after potato, tomato, pepper or eggplant. These share diseases including bacterial wilt and late blight. A 3-year rotation with cereals or legumes is the standard.

Waterlogging risk

If a site holds water after rain, do not use it for potato. Raised beds or ridges help, but a naturally waterlogged site is a lost cause.

The rule: a site that has grown potato or tomato within the last 3 years, or that holds water after rain, is not a potato site. Choose carefully before you plant.
Lesson 3

Varieties — Table, Processing, Seed

In this lesson
  • Distinguish table, processing and seed potato varieties
  • Match variety to altitude and market
  • Understand seed classes

Table varieties

Chosen for appearance, taste, cooking quality. Common in Africa: Shangi, Tigoni, Kenya Karibu, Unica, Dutch Robijn, Rosita. Mature 90–120 days. Market price varies by season.

Processing varieties

Chosen for high dry matter and low sugar — needed for chips and crisps. Higher specification, contract buyers, premium prices. Examples: Markies, Taurus, Lady Rosetta.

Seed varieties

Grown specifically for seed multiplication under certification. Requires isolation from other potato and strict virus control. Very high margin but also very high discipline.

Seed classes

  • Pre-basic: tissue-culture derived, disease-free, most expensive
  • Basic (E, A, B): multiplication stages, decreasing price
  • Certified: what farmers usually buy for commercial production
  • Farmer-saved: cheaper but accumulating virus; use only 1 season
Lesson 4

Yield Reality and the African Gap

In this lesson
  • Read the yield range between poor and good management
  • Identify the five factors that close the gap
  • Set a realistic first-season target

African average potato yield is 5–10 t/ha. Achievable with certified seed, timely blight control, adequate fertility and proper hilling: 20–40 t/ha. Under irrigation and good management, 50+ t/ha is recorded.

Five factors that close the gap

  1. Certified seed. The single biggest factor. Poor seed costs 40–60% of yield.
  2. Timely blight control. Late blight can kill 10 t/ha in a week if untreated. Weekly scouting from emergence.
  3. Hilling (earthing up). Prevents greening and provides space for tubers. Done twice: at 20–25 cm and at 40–50 cm.
  4. Fertility. Potato is a heavy feeder. 400–600 kg/ha of compound NPK split across the season.
  5. Water. Critical at tuber initiation and tuber bulking. 500–700 mm over the season.
Lesson 5

The Three Markets

In this lesson
  • Name the three main potato markets
  • Match grade to buyer
  • Plan which market you are growing for

Market 1 — Fresh table market

Households, market vendors, supermarkets, hotels. $0.20–0.60/kg depending on season. This is the bulk market. Grade for uniformity and freedom from blemishes.

Market 2 — Processing market

Chip and crisp manufacturers, restaurants, food processors. $0.35–0.80/kg but strict specifications: high dry matter, low sugar, correct size, specific varieties. Often through contracts.

Market 3 — Seed market

Other farmers and seed distributors. Certified seed sells for 2–3x food price. Requires isolation, virus control, and inspection. Highest margin, highest discipline.
