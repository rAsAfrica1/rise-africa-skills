Lesson 1

Site and Soil Requirements

In this lesson
  • Choose the right site for yam
  • Understand soil depth and drainage
  • Plan rotation to control nematodes

Soil

  • Deep: at least 60 cm of loose soil, ideally 1 m+
  • Well-drained: yam rots in waterlogged soil
  • Fertile: high organic matter, good structure
  • pH 5.5–6.5: slightly acid is best
  • Texture: sandy loam to loam is ideal; heavy clay restricts tuber growth

Climate

Yam needs 1,000–1,500 mm of rainfall over the season, with a distinct dry season for harvest. High altitude reduces yield. Best in the lowland humid and sub-humid tropics.

Rotation

Never follow yam with yam, and avoid planting after other root crops (cassava, sweet potato) — they share nematodes. Best rotation partners: cereals (maize, sorghum, rice), legumes (cowpea, groundnut), and fallow.

The rule: yam after yam builds nematodes and depletes the soil. Rotate with a cereal or legume for at least one season between yam crops.
Lesson 2

Land Preparation — Deep and Loose

In this lesson
  • Prepare deep, loose soil
  • Incorporate organic matter
  • Use hand, animal or tractor methods

Primary tillage

  • Hand: dig to 30–40 cm with a hoe or fork. Very labour-intensive. Practical up to 0.3 ha.
  • Animal-drawn plough: 20–30 cm depth. Common in West Africa.
  • Tractor plough: 30–40 cm. Best for larger areas. Requires access to a tractor and capital.

Secondary tillage

Harrow to break clods and level. Yam needs fine soil for smooth tuber growth — clods cause misshapen tubers.

Organic matter

Incorporate 10–20 t/ha of well-rotted manure or compost. This is the single biggest improvement you can make to soil for yam. It improves structure, water holding, and reduces nematode pressure.

Soil amendments

  • Lime: if pH is below 5.5, apply agricultural lime 1–2 t/ha, 3–6 months before planting
  • Wood ash: 2–4 t/ha, provides potassium, raises pH slightly, suppresses nematodes
  • Neem cake: 1–2 t/ha, suppresses nematodes as it breaks down
Lesson 3

Ridges, Mounds and Flat Planting

In this lesson
  • Choose between ridges, mounds and flat planting
  • Build ridges at correct dimensions
  • Understand the yield differences

Mounds

Traditional West African method. Circular mounds 30–50 cm high and 60–100 cm wide. Plant 1–3 setts per mound. Good drainage, easy to control nematodes by mound location rotation. Labour-intensive.

Ridges

Continuous ridges 30–50 cm high, spaced 1 m apart. Good for larger areas. Similar yield to mounds. Easier to manage with mechanisation.

Flat planting

Used in deep, well-drained soils with low waterlogging risk. Lower yield but easier and faster. Not suitable for heavy or poorly drained soils.

Soil depth matters

Yam tubers grow 60–100 cm long, sometimes 2 m. Ridges and mounds must have deep, loose soil underneath. A shallow mound gives short, stunted tubers.

Lesson 4

Spacing and Plant Population

In this lesson
  • Space for correct plant population
  • Adjust spacing for sett size and market
  • Calculate seed yam required

Standard spacing

  • Between ridges/mounds: 1 m
  • Within ridge: 30–50 cm
  • Plant population: 10,000–13,000 plants/ha

By market

  • Food yam (large tubers): 1 × 50 cm, ~13,000 plants/ha
  • Seed yam (many small tubers): 1 × 30 cm, ~20,000 plants/ha
  • Rapid multiplication: 75 × 30 cm, ~30,000 plants/ha

Seed yam required

  • At 13,000 plants/ha and 150 g per sett = 1,950 kg/ha
  • At 20,000 plants/ha and 60 g per sett = 1,200 kg/ha

For 1 hectare of food yam, budget 1.5–2 tonnes of seed yam — the single biggest cost line.

Lesson 5

Planting — Depth, Orientation, Timing

In this lesson
  • Plant setts at the correct depth and orientation
  • Time planting for the rainy season
  • Handle the sprouting instinct

Depth

Plant setts 10–15 cm deep. Too shallow and tubers green or dry; too deep and emergence is delayed.

Orientation

Yam setts have a "head" and a "tail". The head (cut end or the top of the whole tuber) should face upwards. This is the end that sprouts and where the tuber head forms. Getting orientation wrong delays emergence by 2–3 weeks.

Timing

Plant at the start of the rainy season, when soil is moist to 20–30 cm. In West Africa:

  • Forest zone: March–April
  • Guinea savannah: April–May
  • Sudan savannah: May–June

Late planting reduces yield by 30–50% because the crop runs out of rains before tuber bulking finishes.

Mulching

Mulch around the setts at planting with dry grass or crop residue. Reduces weed competition, conserves moisture, and keeps soil cooler during establishment.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. What soil depth does yam need?

Correct: 60 cm minimum. Tubers can grow 1–2 m long.

2. What rotation rule applies to yam?

Correct: rotate with cereal or legume. Cassava shares nematodes.

3. What sett orientation is correct?

Correct: head up. The head is where the sprout and tuber head form.

4. What spacing for food yam production?

Correct: 1 × 50 cm. ~13,000 plants/ha, allows large tubers to form.

5. What does organic matter do for yam soil?

Correct: all three. Manure or compost at 10–20 t/ha is the single biggest soil improvement.
