Module 1: The Broiler Business - Capital Tiers and Profit Plan

Broiler production is the fastest route to cash in African poultry. A day-old chick reaches 2.2 kg live weight in 35 to 42 days under good management. That is five to six cycles per year on the same house. No other livestock enterprise turns capital this fast. But speed cuts both ways: a mistake in week one costs you the whole cycle. This module gives you the numbers before you spend a shilling, naira, or rand.

We cover three capital tiers, real feed conversion figures, and a profit model you can adapt to your market. Everything here is built for African conditions: heat, feed cost volatility, and informal market pricing. Read it once, then run the numbers for your own farm before you buy a single chick.

Why Broilers, and Why Now

Africa's poultry meat demand is rising faster than any other animal protein. Per capita consumption in sub-Saharan Africa sits around 4 to 5 kg per year, against a global average above 15 kg (FAO, 2023). Urban demand for affordable protein is the driver. Broilers fit because they convert feed to meat more efficiently than any alternative: a modern broiler needs roughly 1.6 kg of feed to produce 1 kg of live weight. A local dual-purpose chicken needs 3 to 4 kg. That gap is your margin.

The business is not "keeping chickens." It is converting purchased feed into sellable meat at a spread. Your job is to protect that spread. Feed is 65 to 70 percent of your cost. Every point of feed efficiency you gain drops straight to profit.

The Three Capital Tiers

Do not start bigger than your cash flow and your management skill can carry. Mortality punishes beginners hardest. Start at the tier your experience, labour, and market access justify. You can climb tiers cycle by cycle.

This is where most African broiler businesses start. You use an existing structure, a shed, or a simple open-sided house with a corrugated roof. Deep litter on the floor. Feeders and drinkers by hand. You sell to neighbours, local butchers, and roadside buyers.

Realistic output: 100 birds, 5 percent mortality, 1.7 FCR, 2.2 kg average live weight at 42 days. That is 209 kg of live meat. Sell at farm gate and you avoid transport and middlemen. Your profit per cycle is small but repeatable, and it funds your climb to Tier 2.

Tier 2: Semi-Commercial (500â2,000 birds)

Here you become a real business. You need a purpose-built house with proper ventilation, a brooder section, and a reliable water supply. You hire at least one worker. You keep records because your margins depend on catching problems in day one, not day ten.

Capital needed: $1,500 to $6,000 (KES 200,000â800,000 / NGN 1.2Mâ4.5M / ZAR 27,000â110,000). This covers house construction or upgrade, feeders and drinkers, a small generator or solar for lighting and water pumping, and working capital for one cycle of 500 to 1,000 birds.

Tier 2 is where feed efficiency starts to matter more than bird price. You are buying 25 kg bags or bulk feed, and every 0.1 point of FCR on 1,000 birds is roughly 220 kg of feed saved per cycle. At KES 55/kg that is over KES 12,000 per cycle. Protect it.

Tier 3: Commercial (5,000+ birds)

This tier supplies processors, supermarkets, and institutional buyers on contract. You need climate control for heat stress, automated feeding and drinking lines, and a formal veterinary relationship. Feed comes by the tonne, often from your own mill or a bulk supplier.

Capital needed: $12,000 and up (KES 1.6M+ / NGN 9M+ / ZAR 220,000+). At this scale, a 0.05 improvement in FCR across 50,000 birds per cycle is worth millions of shillings a year. You also get price leverage: bulk chick and feed contracts, and the ability to negotiate fixed supply deals that stabilise your margin.

Biosecurity is not optional. Newcastle disease and avian influenza can wipe out an entire flock in days. WOAH lists Newcastle disease as endemic across much of Africa. Follow these rules without exception:

  • Vaccinate on schedule. Newcastle (day 7, booster day 21), Gumboro (day 14), and follow your vet's local protocol. Never skip a booster.
  • Keep a footbath at every house entrance and use it. Change the disinfectant every two days.
  • Never let traders, other farmers, or dead-bird collectors walk into your house. Meet them at the gate.
  • Use a single age group per house. Do not mix ages. All-in, all-out.
  • Rest the house 10 to 14 days between cycles with full clean-out and disinfection.
  • Report sudden high mortality to your vet or veterinary authority immediately. Do not sell or move sick birds.

One disease outbreak costs more than a year of profit. Biosecurity is cheaper than treatment.

Africa Context: Inputs, Prices, and Suppliers

Your numbers depend on where you farm. Chick and feed prices vary widely across the continent. Here is what to watch and who supplies what.

Kenya (KES): Day-old broiler chicks run roughly KES 90 to 120 each. Feed (broiler starter, grower, finisher) sits around KES 50 to 60 per kg. Key suppliers: Kenchic, Kukuchic, and Tumaini hatcheries; feed from Unga, Pembe, and Nutrimix. KARI and the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation publish local cost guides.

Nigeria (NGN): Chicks cost NGN 600 to 800 each depending on season and hatchery. Feed is NGN 350 to 420 per kg, and has been volatile with naira movement and import costs. Suppliers: Zartech, Obasanjo Farms, and Amo Farms for chicks; Premier Feeds, Vital Feeds, and Chikun for feed. Plan for feed price swings by buying a cycle's feed upfront where cash allows.

South Africa (ZAR): A more consolidated market. Chicks from Astral, Rainbow, and Country Bird. Feed from Astral, Meadow Feeds, and Epol. Prices are more stable but margins are tighter because you compete with large integrators. Your edge is local, fresh, and halal or niche supply.

Across all three, the rule is the same: lock your chick and feed supply before you commit to a cycle. A late chick delivery or a feed gap mid-cycle destroys FCR and your profit.

Your Profit Plan: Five Steps Before You Spend

  1. Pick your tier honestly. First-timers start at Tier 1. If you have never managed a flock to slaughter, do not start at 500 birds.
  2. Write your cost per bird. Chick + feed + meds + litter + labour Ã· number of birds. If you cannot state this number, you are not ready.
  3. Confirm your market before the chicks arrive. Who buys 950 birds in week six? Get a verbal commitment or a contract. Unsold birds eat profit.
  4. Set your FCR and mortality targets. Target FCR 1.7 or better and mortality under 5 percent. Weigh feed and count birds weekly. Measure or guess.
  5. Plan the next cycle's cash. Hold back enough from each sale to buy the next batch of chicks and feed. Never spend the whole cycle income.
