Practical Reference

This reference section turns the six modules into working numbers you can take to the field, the bank and the buyer. Drought-resistant crops are a commercial play, not a subsistence fallback. Millet, sorghum and amaranth hold value because they tolerate heat and moisture stress, mature on short rainfall windows, and trade into both informal food markets and formal processing channels. The figures below are drawn from published FAO and ICRISAT data and from regional market observations across Southern and East Africa. Treat them as planning baselines, then replace them with your own farm records after your first season.

The commercial implication is straightforward: on a fixed water budget you can plant more hectares of millet, sorghum or amaranth than maize, and you carry lower failure risk in a dry year. That is a revenue-diversification decision, not a yield-maximisation decision. Build your margin per hectare, not your tonnage per hectare.

Indicative gross margin per hectare, rain-fed production, Southern Africa baseline
Crop Yield (t/ha) Farm-gate price (USD/t) Variable cost (USD/ha) Gross margin (USD/ha)
Pearl millet 1.2 260 180 132
Sorghum 1.8 230 210 204
Amaranth (grain) 0.9 520 240 228
Hybrid maize (comparison) 3.5 200 420 280

Source: Compiled from FAO FAOSTAT yield and producer price series (2022-2023 averages, Southern Africa) and ICRISAT Sorghum and Millet Economics working data. Amaranth price reflects premium niche and processing demand; maize comparison shown for risk context only.

Read the table carefully. Maize still wins on gross margin in a good year, but it carries the highest variable cost and the highest failure probability. Millet and sorghum deliver lower absolute margin with far lower downside. Amaranth wins per hectare if you can access a processor or a premium retail buyer, because the price is driven by nutrition demand, not bulk commodity demand. The strategy that works for most African agribusinesses is a split: 60% sorghum or millet for volume and food security contracts, 20% amaranth for margin, 20% maize only where you have irrigation or reliable rainfall.

Sorghum, millet and amaranth grain are highly susceptible to Aspergillus mould and aflatoxin contamination when stored above 13% moisture in warm, humid conditions. Aflatoxin is a confirmed human carcinogen. Never sell, mill or feed mouldy, musty or discoloured grain. Dry to safe moisture before storage.

If you use pesticides, herbicides or fumigation tablets (including phosphine): always wear gloves, a respirator or dust mask, eye protection and long sleeves. Never enter a fumigated store or silo for at least the full label waiting period. Phosphine tablets release highly toxic gas â keep them away from dwellings and children.

Store treated seed separately from food grain and label every container. Wash hands and clothing after handling any agro-chemical. If anyone shows dizziness, vomiting or breathing difficulty after exposure, move them to fresh air and seek medical help immediately. Follow the label and your national regulator (e.g. the Pesticides Control Board or equivalent) at all times.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • 1. Match crop to rainfall window. Confirm your 20-year seasonal rainfall before choosing millet, sorghum or amaranth. Do not plant sorghum on a 350 mm site.
  • 2. Source certified or improved open-pollinated seed. Buy from a registered agro-dealer and keep the receipt. Seed quality is your single biggest yield lever.
  • 3. Test soil and correct pH and phosphorus first. Drought tolerance does not compensate for depleted soils. Apply basal fertiliser or manure at planting.
  • 4. Plant on the first reliable rain, in rows. Row planting with correct spacing makes weeding, spraying and harvesting cheaper and faster.
  • 5. Control weeds in the first 45 days. Early weed competition removes more yield than drought in most seasons. Budget for two weedings.
  • 6. Dry grain to below 13% moisture before storage. Use a moisture meter. Store on pallets in a clean, ventilated, rodent-proof store.
  • 7. Secure a buyer before harvest, not after. Contract with a miller, aggregator or processor and agree grade, moisture and price in writing.
  • 8. Record every cost and sale. Your gross margin per hectare is the number that decides whether you scale, switch crop or change market.
