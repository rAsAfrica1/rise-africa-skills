Module 4

The Block Hive — Adapting to Concrete Block

Concrete block is what most African builders have on hand. It is cheaper per unit than fired brick, but hollow, weaker in tension, and it holds water if not sealed. This module adapts the brick hive design to concrete block.

By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Choose the correct block type and fill specification
  • Adapt the wall thickness and cavity dimensions for block construction
  • Seal hollow block cores so bees do not nest inside them

Lesson 4.1

Block types and what each does for a hive

Concrete block comes in a range of shapes and sizes. For a hive, the choice matters.

Block typeSize (typical)Suitability for hive
Solid block390 × 190 × 190 mmExcellent — no cavities for bees to enter
Hollow block (2-core)390 × 190 × 190 mmGood — cores must be filled or sealed
Hollow block (3-core)390 × 190 × 190 mmAcceptable — more cores to fill
Lightweight / aerated block390 × 190 × 190 mmAvoid — low strength, absorbs water
Clay brick220 × 110 × 75 mmExcellent but slow to lay

For a hive, solid block is best if it is available. If not, hollow block is fine provided every core in every block that faces the hive cavity is filled with mortar or concrete.

Why hollow cores must be filled

  • Bees nest in unfilled cores. A hollow block core becomes a secondary cavity. Bees will find it, use it, and you cannot inspect it.
  • Cores hold water. Moisture from rain or rising damp collects in block cores, keeping the hive interior damp.
  • Cores reduce the effective wall mass compared to solid block, reducing thermal stability.

Filling every core with a 1:4 mortar or a lean concrete mix is the correct practice. It is tedious, but it is the difference between a hive and a decorative wall.

The block rule: a hollow block hive is only acceptable if every core that faces the cavity is filled. Otherwise, use solid block or fired brick.

Lesson 4.2

Wall thickness and cavity dimensions

Wall thickness

A single 190 mm block wall is thinner than a 230 mm brick wall. For thermal mass, a 190 mm block wall alone is marginal in extreme climates. Two options:

  • Single 190 mm block wall. Acceptable in moderate climates (average daytime temp under 30 °C).
  • Cavity wall — two 190 mm skins with 50 mm air gap. Excellent thermal performance. More material, more labour, but the correct choice in hot climates or cold highlands.

Cavity dimensions

The internal cavity dimensions do not change from the brick hive. Standard KTBH-style cavity: 900 × 450 × 300 mm internal. What changes is the wall thickness around it.

Wall typeWall thicknessExternal hive footprint
Brick (single)230 mm1,360 × 910 mm
Block (single)190 mm1,280 × 830 mm
Block cavity wall190 + 50 + 190 = 430 mm1,760 × 1,310 mm

Lesson 4.3

Laying block for a hive

Block is faster to lay than brick because the units are larger. But the discipline is different:

Mortar

  • Joint thickness: 10–12 mm (thinner than brickwork).
  • Mix: 1:4 cement-to-sand for the walling, 1:3 for core filling.
  • Bedding: full bed of mortar. Do not spot-bed — hollow spots hold water.

Laying steps

  1. Start from the corners. Get the first corner block dead level, plumb, and square.
  2. String a line between corners for the first course.
  3. Lay the first course with a full mortar bed.
  4. Fill the cores of the first course with mortar.
  5. Continue up, each course staggered by half a block.
  6. Fill cores every 2–3 courses, before they become inaccessible.
  7. Keep the cavity clean — mortar droppings inside the hive cavity must be cleaned out.

The bar rest

Block units are 190 mm wide, wider than the 20 mm bar rest requires. Cut a bar rest into the top of the two long side walls: 20 mm wide, 15 mm deep. Use an angle grinder or a masonry chisel.

Alternatively, cast a concrete lintel on top of the walls with the bar rest pre-formed. This gives more precise bar spacing.

Keeping the cavity clean

Mortar drops into the cavity as you lay. If left, they harden and can never be removed without damaging the wall. Clean the inside face of every course with a wet sponge before the mortar sets.

Do not skip the daily cavity clean. A hive with a mortar-encrusted interior cannot be cleaned properly later, and the bees will refuse to work cleanly inside it.

Lesson 4.4

Sealing and finishing the interior

Why seal

Block is porous. The interior face of the cavity must be sealed to:

  • Prevent bees from attaching comb to a rough, gritty surface (which tears when lifted).
  • Prevent moisture from rising through the block into the hive.
  • Create a smooth, cleanable surface if the hive needs to be scrubbed out at any point.

Sealing options

  • Cement render, then whitewash. Traditional. 10 mm render, cured, then lime wash. Cheap, effective, food-safe.
  • Cement render, then mason's waterproof coating. Better water resistance. Use a product rated food-safe when cured.
  • Waterproof masonry paint. Fastest. Must be food-safe when cured — check the label.

Never use bitumen, tar, or oil-based sealants. They release fumes and are not food-safe.

Ventilation

Block hives hold moisture. Provide ventilation slots at the top of the walls (200 × 10 mm, meshed) opposite each other. Without these, the interior stays damp and mouldy.

Curing

Like all masonry, block requires 28 days of curing before the bees can move in. Wash the interior with clean water after curing, dry fully, then place the hive.

Lesson 4.5

Roof, entrance and stand for a block hive

Roof

  • Metal roof on a timber frame. The standard. Light, easy to lift, weatherproof.
  • Concrete slab roof. Heavier, requires two people to lift, but very durable. Only for a hive that is inspected infrequently.
  • Overhang: 200 mm minimum on all sides.
  • Ventilation gap: 20 mm between the top bars and the underside of the roof. Bee space.

Entrance

Cut or cast into one end wall: 300–400 mm wide, 15–20 mm high, at the base of the wall. Some designs use a removable entrance bar that can be adjusted seasonally.

Stand

Block hives are heavy. Do not attempt to lift one onto a stand. Build the hive on a raised concrete plinth — 300–400 mm above ground — cast as part of the base slab. The hive sits directly on the plinth.

Access for harvest

Since the hive cannot be moved, the roof must be removable in place. Two people lift it off, set it aside, then replace after harvest. Make the roof light enough to lift easily — a metal roof on a light timber frame is standard for this reason.

The block-hive rules: solid block or filled hollow block; render and seal the interior; 28-day cure; ventilation slots at the top; roof overhang 200 mm; built on a plinth, not a stand. Miss any and the hive will either damp, mould, or become uninspectable.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. Why must hollow block cores be filled in a block hive?

Correct: bees and water. Unfilled cores become uninspectable secondary cavities and moisture traps.

2. Why is a cavity wall sometimes preferable to single-block wall for a hive?

Correct: thermal stability. The cavity wall adds mass and air insulation, giving the colony a more stable interior.

3. What is the correct joint thickness for block work?

Correct: 10–12 mm. Thinner than brickwork because block is more dimensionally consistent.

4. What is the correct treatment for the interior of a block hive?

Correct: render + food-safe coating. Seals the porous block, prevents comb attachment on rough surfaces, and creates a cleanable interior.

5. Why does a block hive sit on a plinth instead of a stand?

Correct: weight. A block hive weighs as much as a small car. The plinth is cast as part of the base.

Practical assignment — Module 4

Produce a complete block hive specification for your own site.

  1. Block type chosen (solid, hollow, other) and why.
  2. Wall design — single block wall or cavity wall — with a cross-section sketch showing dimensions.
  3. Core-filling plan: which blocks are filled, when in the laying sequence, with what mix.
  4. Bar rest design — cut into the top block or pre-formed in a cast lintel. State which.
  5. Interior finishing — render material, sealant product, food-safety confirmation.
  6. Ventilation plan — how many slots, where, what mesh.
  7. Roof design with overhang dimension and metal covering.
  8. Site plan showing the hive on its plinth, drainage, and orientation.
  9. Two supplier quotations for the block and the render material.
  10. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest risk in your block-hive build and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 10. The wall design is the structural decision; the paragraph is where you commit to a specific risk control.
