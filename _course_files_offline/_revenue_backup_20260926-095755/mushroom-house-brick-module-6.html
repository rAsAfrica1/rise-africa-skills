Module 6

Air Exchange, CO₂ and Fresh Air Without Contamination

CO₂ is the parameter most first-time growers ignore and the one that most often produces unsaleable mushrooms. This module is how to control it without wrecking the temperature and humidity you worked to achieve.

Module 6 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Explain why CO₂ matters for mushroom shape and quality
  • Set CO₂ targets for each growth stage
  • Calculate the correct air exchange rate for your room
  • Filter incoming air without losing humidity or heat
  • Distribute air evenly without creating draughts
  • Balance CO₂ flush with temperature and humidity

Lesson 6.1

Why CO₂ matters

The mushroom mycelium and fruiting bodies both respire — they consume oxygen and produce CO₂, just like animals. In a closed room, CO₂ builds up. This is a problem because:

  • High CO₂ produces long stems and small caps. This is called "etiolation" or "stretching". The mushroom tries to reach fresh air by growing a long stem, and the cap stays small. The result is an unsaleable product.
  • Very high CO₂ (> 2,000 ppm) suppresses fruiting entirely. The fungus stays in the vegetative state and never produces mushrooms.
  • CO₂ also affects taste. Mushrooms grown at high CO₂ have less flavour and shorter shelf life.

The signal

If your mushrooms are getting long stems and small caps, it is almost never a variety problem. It is a CO₂ problem. Increase fresh air exchange.

The CO₂ rule: below 800 ppm during fruiting. Above that, mushrooms stretch. Above 2,000 ppm, fruiting stops. The signal that you have a CO₂ problem is long stems and small caps.

Why spawn run is different

During spawn run the mycelium is colonising the substrate. It grows fastest with elevated CO₂. Many growers deliberately seal the room during spawn run and let CO₂ build. Only when the substrate is fully colonised do they introduce fresh air to trigger pinning.

This is the one time high CO₂ is desirable. Every other stage requires flushing.

Why pinning needs a CO₂ drop

In nature, the mushroom triggers fruiting when the mycelium reaches the surface of the substrate and encounters fresh air. A drop in CO₂ (from the high level inside the substrate to the atmospheric level at the surface) is the trigger signal. In a mushroom house, we simulate this by deliberately reducing CO₂.

Some growers also induce pinning with a temperature drop (cold shock). Combined with the CO₂ drop, this reliably initiates fruiting.

Lesson 6.3

Calculating air exchange

The basic calculation

Air exchange rate is expressed as air changes per hour (ACH). For a mushroom house:

  • Spawn run: 1–2 ACH (minimum — CO₂ accumulates intentionally)
  • Pinning: 4–6 ACH
  • Fruiting: 4–8 ACH
  • Very active fruiting: 8–12 ACH

Calculating fan size

Fan volumetric rate = Room volume × ACH

For a room 5 × 4 × 3 m = 60 m³, at 6 ACH = 360 m³/hour. A 100 mm axial fan moves 200–300 m³/hour; a 150 mm fan moves 500–800 m³/hour. So a single 150 mm fan is enough for this room.

Fan type

  • Axial fan — moves air in a straight line. Good for direct wall or ceiling mounting. Most cost-effective.
  • Centrifugal fan — moves air at higher pressure. Used when ductwork must be long or has bends.
  • In-line duct fan — used inside ductwork. Good for filtered systems.

Inlet and outlet placement

  • Inlet: low on one wall, opposite the fan. Screened. Adjustable damper.
  • Outlet: high on the fan wall. Screened. Adjustable damper.
  • The fan draws air out (extraction), and fresh air enters passively through the inlet.
  • Do not place the inlet directly opposite the outlet at the same height — that creates a straight draught and does not exchange the whole room's air.

Lesson 6.4

Filtering incoming air

Fresh air carries spores. Every spore that enters the mushroom house is a contamination risk. Filtration is mandatory for a commercial operation.

Filter types

  • Insect screen (1 mm mesh). Keeps out flies, wasps, moths. Does not stop spores.
  • Pre-filter (G4 grade). Catches larger particles. Extends the life of the HEPA filter.
  • HEPA filter (H13 grade). Catches 99.95% of particles above 0.3 microns. Stops almost all fungal spores. This is the standard for a mushroom house.

The filter arrangement

Outside air │ ▼ INSECT SCREEN (1 mm) │ ▼ PRE-FILTER (G4) │ ▼ HEPA FILTER (H13) │ ▼ INLET DUCT TO GROW ROOM

Cost

  • Insect screen: minimal
  • Pre-filter: $20–60, replace every 1–3 months
  • HEPA filter: $80–300, replace every 6–12 months
  • Filter housing: $100–400

Positive pressure

The room should be under slight positive pressure — more air entering than leaving. This pushes air out of any leaks, so contaminants cannot enter through cracks. A small excess of fresh air intake over exhaust achieves this.

Lesson 6.5

Balancing CO₂, temperature and humidity

Every fresh air exchange also brings heat and moisture changes. The three parameters fight each other.

The conflicts

ActionEffect on CO₂Effect on tempEffect on humidity
Increase air exchangeLowers CO₂ (good)Moves temp toward outside temp (may be good or bad)Lowers humidity (bad — dries the room)
Decrease air exchangeRaises CO₂ (bad)Holds temp more stableRaises humidity (may cause blotch)
Run humidifierNo effectSlight cooling (evaporation)Raises humidity (good)
Run heaterNo effectRaises temp (good in cool)Lowers humidity (bad)
Run coolerNo effectLowers temp (good in hot)Raises humidity (may be too much)

The control strategy

  1. Set the temperature with the heater or cooler. Hold at species target (±1 °C).
  2. Set the humidity with the humidifier. Hold at 85–92%.
  3. Set the CO₂ with the air exchange fan. Run on a timer or on a CO₂ sensor.
  4. Coordinate them. If air exchange drops humidity, run the humidifier more. If it drops temperature, run the heater more. Do not run air exchange just before harvest (mushrooms lose moisture).

The CO₂ controller option

A CO₂ sensor + controller automatically runs the fan when CO₂ exceeds the target. Cost $200–600. Worth it for a commercial operation, because it optimises both CO₂ and energy use.

Manual control

Without a CO₂ controller, run the fan on a timer. Common settings:

  • Spawn run: fan off or 15 min per hour
  • Pinning: fan 30–45 min per hour
  • Fruiting: fan 45–60 min per hour

These are starting points. Adjust based on mushroom quality (long stems = increase; dry caps = decrease).

The CO₂ rule: below 800 ppm during fruiting. Fresh air in, exhaust out, through HEPA filters. If mushrooms have long stems and small caps, you have a CO₂ problem. Fix it with air exchange, not with a different variety.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. What is the CO₂ target during fruiting?

Correct: under 800 ppm. Above this, mushrooms produce long stems and small caps — unsaleable.

2. What is the signal that CO₂ is too high?

Correct: long stems, small caps. The fungus stretches to reach fresh air.

3. What filter grade is required for mushroom house incoming air?

Correct: HEPA H13. Catches 99.95% of particles above 0.3 microns, including most fungal spores.

4. What is the recommended air exchange during spawn run?

Correct: 1–2 ACH. Spawn run benefits from high CO₂. Only reduce CO₂ when it is time to fruit.

5. Why run the room at slight positive pressure?

Correct: contaminants cannot enter through leaks. Slight positive pressure is standard in clean rooms and mushroom houses.
