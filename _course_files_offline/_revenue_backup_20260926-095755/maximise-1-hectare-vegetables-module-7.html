7. Harvest, Post-Harvest and Marketing

This is where the money is made or lost. A perfect crop harvested badly, graded poorly, or sold to the wrong buyer earns less than a mediocre crop handled well. Up to 40% of vegetable value in Africa is lost between the field and the customer — almost all of it avoidable.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end · course completion

Lesson 7.1 — Harvest timing and maturity

Harvest too early and you lose weight and flavour. Harvest too late and you lose quality, shelf life, and often the whole crop to rot.

Tomato (fresh)Colour break — 10–30% pink, firmEvery 2–3 daysSoft fruit, splits, cannot transport

Onion50–70% tops fallen, necks soft7–14 days after top fallThick necks, bulb rot in store, poor storage life

Kale / rapeLeaves 20–30 cm, tender, before stem hardensWeekly for 6+ monthsTough, bitter, woody leaves

Herbs (basil, mint, coriander)Before flowering, morning after dew driesWeekly, cut-and-come-againBitter, bolted, low oil content

Three timing principles:

  1. Harvest at the correct physiological stage, not the correct market stage. If the market wants green tomato, pick at colour break, not at full green — flavour develops after picking.
  2. Harvest in the cool of the day. Before 9am or after 5pm. Field heat is the enemy of shelf life.
  3. For leafy greens, harvest early morning. They are turgid and crisp, then move them into shade immediately.
The maturity rule: Write the maturity index for each crop on a card and keep it in your pocket during harvest week. Pickers who guess cost you 20% of your crop value.
Practical exercise 7.1: Harvest 20 tomato fruits at colour break and 20 at full red. Store both in identical crates in shade. Compare firmness and marketable fruit at day 5 and day 10.

Lesson 7.2 — Harvest technique and handling

Bruises do not show at harvest. They show 4 days later as rot at the market — and the buyer blames you.

The 10 handling rules:

  1. Use sharp, clean tools. Secateurs or a sharp knife. A torn stem is an open door for rot.
  2. Do not pull fruit off the plant. Cut the stalk with a short piece attached, or twist gently if the variety allows.
  3. Never throw produce into a crate. A 1 m drop bruises tomato, pepper, and cabbage. Place, do not toss.
  4. Harvest into clean, shallow crates. Deep sacks crush the bottom layer.
  5. Never harvest into fertiliser or chemical sacks. Contamination and residue risk. Use clean plastic crates or woven baskets with a liner.
  6. Keep produce in shade from the moment it is picked. A 30-minute exposure to direct sun can raise fruit temperature by 10°C and cut shelf life in half.
  7. Move to a shaded collection point immediately. Never let full crates sit in the field.
  8. Do not mix damaged with healthy produce. One rotting fruit infects the whole crate.
  9. Keep harvested produce off the ground. Ground contact means soil, moisture, and disease.
  10. Harvest the right volume for the day. Do not harvest more than you can cool, grade, and sell the same day.

Field heat — the invisible killer:

  • Produce harvested at 30°C in full sun respires 3–5× faster than produce at 15°C.
  • Every hour of delay in cooling shortens shelf life by roughly one day for leafy greens.
  • The cheapest cooling is shade plus a ventilated shed. The second cheapest is harvesting in the cool hours.
Never leave harvested vegetables in a closed vehicle in the sun. Interior temperatures reach 50–60°C within 20 minutes. A full load can be worthless before you reach the market.
Practical exercise 7.2: Harvest one crate of tomato into a clean crate in shade, and one into a sack left in the sun for 1 hour. Grade both at day 4. Count marketable fruit.

Lesson 7.3 — Washing, grading and packaging

Grading is the fastest way to increase your price. The same crop, sorted and packaged properly, sells for 20–50% more than a mixed, dirty pile.

Washing — when and when not:

  • Wash: leafy greens, carrots for fresh market, beetroot, radish, herbs (gently).
  • Do not wash: onion and garlic for storage (moisture causes rot), tomato for storage (wounds and moisture), anything you will store for more than a week.
  • Wash water: clean water with a small amount of food-safe sanitiser (or 1 teaspoon of bleach per 10 L). Change the water often — dirty water spreads disease across the whole batch.
  • Dry thoroughly after washing. Wet produce rots.

Grading standards:

CropGrade 1 (premium)Grade 2 (standard)Reject / process
TomatoUniform size, firm, no blemish, correct colour stageSlight size variation, minor blemishCracked, soft, diseased, sunburnt
OnionUniform, tight skin, no sprouting, dry neck, 60–80 mmSmaller or larger, minor skin damageThick neck, soft, sprouting, diseased
CabbageFirm, tight head, 1.5–2.5 kg, 2 wrapper leavesLoose head, small or large, trimmedSplit, rotting, insect damage
CarrotStraight, uniform, no fork, no crack, deep colourSlight fork or curve, minor crackSplit, forked, woody, green shoulder
PepperUniform, glossy, thick wall, no blemishMinor blemish or shape variationSoft, sunscald, blossom-end rot
HerbsClean, whole leaves, no stem, uniform bunchMixed size, some stemYellowed, wilted, flowering

Packaging options:

PackagingBest forAdvantageWatch out for

Labeling that raises price:

  • Your farm name and a phone number.
  • Product name and weight (e.g. "Tomato Grade 1 — 10 kg").
  • Harvest date. Buyers trust dates.
  • Any claim you can back up — "no spray within 7 days", "grown without synthetic pesticide". Never claim what you cannot prove.
The grading rule: Grade before you leave the farm, never at the market. Grading in the field takes 30 minutes and raises the price of the whole load. Grading at the market, in a rush, in front of the buyer, costs you the premium and wastes the reject.
Practical exercise 7.3: Take one crate of mixed tomato to market as-is and one graded crate the same week. Record the price received for each. The difference is your grading profit, and it is usually 20–40%.

Lesson 7.4 — Storage and cooling

Storage is what lets you sell when the price is good instead of when everyone else is selling. Onion is the classic example: harvested in a glut at $0.30/kg, sold 3 months later at $0.80/kg.

CropStorage tempHumidityStorage life
Onion (cured)0–5°C65–70%3–6 months
Garlic (cured)0–5°C60–70%4–8 months
Carrot0–2°C95%4–6 months
Beetroot0–2°C95%4–5 months
Cabbage0–2°C95–98%3–6 months
Tomato (mature green)12–15°C85–90%2–4 weeks
Tomato (ripe)10–12°C85–90%5–10 days
Pepper7–10°C90–95%2–3 weeks
Kale / leafy greens0–2°C95%1–3 weeks
Basil10–12°C90%5–7 days (chill-sensitive — never below 8°C)
Coriander / mint0–2°C95%1–2 weeks

Never store these together:

Ethylene producersEthylene-sensitive (keep away)
Ripe tomato, banana, apple, avocado, onionLeafy greens, carrot, cucumber, cabbage, herbs

Ethylene is a ripening gas. Storing a ripe tomato next to kale turns the kale yellow in 2 days.

Onion curing — the step almost everyone skips:

  1. Stop irrigation 2 weeks before harvest.
  2. Lift bulbs and leave them in the field to dry 7–14 days until the tops are completely brown (if rain threatens, dry under cover).
  3. Move to a shaded, well-ventilated store and cure a further 2–3 weeks.
  4. Trim tops to 2 cm and roots close. Do not cut into the bulb.
  5. Store in mesh sacks or shallow layers on slatted shelves. Never in sealed bags.

Low-cost cooling options:

  • Evaporative cool chamber (zero-energy cool chamber): a double-walled box with wet sand between the walls. Cuts temperature 8–12°C and raises humidity. Costs $50–$200 and needs no electricity. Perfect for leafy greens on a small farm.
  • Shaded, ventilated shed: insulated roof, high vents, no direct sun on the walls. The single best cheap investment for post-harvest.
  • Cool room: $2,000–$8,000 for a small unit. Worth it only once you are selling 500 kg+ per week to premium buyers.
The storage rule: Only store crops that are designed to store — onion, garlic, carrot, beetroot, cabbage, cured pumpkin. Never try to store tomato, leafy greens, or herbs for more than a few days without cooling. Sell them fresh and fast.
Practical exercise 7.4: Cure 50 kg of onion properly this season. Weigh the sacks at harvest, at 1 month, and at 3 months. Record shrinkage and the price difference between selling now and selling in 3 months.

Lesson 7.5 — Finding buyers and pricing

Farming ends when the money arrives, not when the harvest does. A great crop with no buyer is a loss.

ChannelPriceVolumePaymentRequirements
Village / roadside stallHighest per kgVery lowCashTime, location, small quantities
Local open market (selling yourself)HighLow–mediumCash dailyTransport, market fee, full day, spoilage risk
Market trader / broker (selling at farm gate)Low–mediumHighCash or short creditAccept their price, no control
Restaurants and hotelsHighMedium, consistentWeekly or monthly invoiceConsistent quality, weekly delivery, grading, invoice
Schools, hospitals, institutionsMediumHigh, contracted30–60 daysTender process, paperwork, reliability

Processors (tomato paste, dried herbs)LowVery highOn deliveryVolume, correct variety, delivery to factory
Direct box scheme / neighboursHighLow–mediumPrepaidMarketing, packing, delivery route

How to approach a new buyer:

  1. Go in person, in the morning, before service — never at lunch or dinner rush.
  2. Bring a graded sample. Not a sack of mixed produce — a clean tray of your best.
  3. Ask questions first: what do you buy, how much per week, what quality, what price, what day do you want delivery?
  4. Offer a trial: "I will bring 20 kg next Tuesday. If you are happy, we agree a weekly order."
  5. Deliver on time, every time. Reliability is worth more than price to a chef.
  6. Follow up with a phone call after the first delivery. Ask what could be better.

Pricing correctly:

Never price by guessing what the market will pay. Price from your cost of production.

Pricing rules:

  • Know your break-even. Write it on the wall. Never sell below it, even to clear a glut — dumping teaches buyers your price is negotiable downward.
  • Ask the buyer's price first. Then decide.
  • Price the grade, not the crop. Grade 1 and Grade 2 are different products with different prices.
  • Consistency beats a one-off high price. A restaurant paying $0.50/kg every week for a year is worth more than a market day at $1.20/kg once.
  • Never supply more than the buyer asked for. Extra volume forces them to discount it, and it damages your price.
The buyer rule: One confirmed buyer before planting is worth more than 10 buyers you hope to find at harvest. Sign a simple written agreement — variety, quantity, quality standard, price range, delivery day, payment terms. One page is enough.
Practical exercise 7.5: This week, visit 5 potential buyers — 3 restaurants or hotels, 1 school, 1 market trader. Ask each the same 5 questions and write the answers in a notebook. Bring one graded sample. One confirmed weekly order changes your whole farming year.

Lesson 7.6 — Records, costing and your whole-hectare plan

A farm without records is a hobby. Records tell you which crop actually makes money, which one only looks good, and where your money is leaking.

Notice the pattern. Herbs earn $5.00 per m² — four times tomato — but with a tiny area and a small, specific market. Tomato earns the largest total because of area and volume. Kale earns steady weekly cash. Onion earns the best return because it stores and you choose when to sell. This is why a whole-hectare plan uses all of them together.

The 1-hectare plan:

BlockCropAreaWhy
1Tomato (staked)0.30 haHighest total revenue — but only with a confirmed buyer
2Onion0.20 haStores 3–6 months; you sell when the price is good
3Kale + rape0.20 haWeekly cash flow from week 8 onward, for 6+ months
4Carrot + beetroot0.15 haReliable, low water, stores well, low risk
5Pepper0.10 haHigh value per kg, extends the season after tomato
6Herbs (basil, coriander, mint)0.05 haHighest margin per m² — restaurant market only

Stagger the planting:

  • Plant kale and rape first — they give cash within 8 weeks.
  • Plant the tomato nursery next, transplant 4 weeks later.
  • Sow onion nursery early and transplant at 6–8 weeks.
  • Plant herbs last, once you have confirmed the restaurant orders.
  • Split tomato into 2 plantings 3 weeks apart so you are not harvesting 10 tonnes in one week.

The five rules of a profitable hectare:

  1. Never plant without a confirmed buyer for at least 50% of the crop.
  2. Always have a fast cash crop growing — kale, rape, or herbs.
  3. Always have a storage crop — onion, carrot, beetroot — so you can wait for price.
  4. Never grow more than 0.3 ha of any one crop in your first two years.
  5. Record everything. After one season, your records will tell you which block to expand and which to drop.
Practical exercise 7.6: Draw your own 1-hectare plan on paper today. Mark each block, crop, area, planting date, expected harvest date, and expected buyer. Then start a notebook with the six record tables above. That notebook is your farm's memory — and it is worth more than any single season's profit.
