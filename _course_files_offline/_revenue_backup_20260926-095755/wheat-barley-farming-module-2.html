Lesson 1

Wheat Varieties — Bread, Durum, Feed

In this lesson
  • Distinguish bread wheat, durum wheat, and feed wheat
  • Match variety to market and environment
  • Know the rust-resistance ratings

Bread wheat

High gluten, high protein (11–14%). Used for bread, biscuits, noodles. Common African varieties: KARI, Robin, Njoro BW, Danda'a, Kubsa, most CIMMYT-derived lines.

Durum wheat

Harder grain, higher protein, yellow pigments. Used for pasta, couscous. Higher price (10–30%). More demanding on soil and water. Grown in Ethiopia, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, South Africa.

Feed wheat

Any high-yielding wheat that does not meet milling spec. Prices 20–40% below milling.

Rust resistance is critical

Wheat stem rust (Ug99 race) and yellow rust have devastated crops across Africa. Only plant varieties with documented resistance to the current rust races in your area. Ask your research institute for the updated variety list every 2 years.

Lesson 2

Barley Varieties — Malting, Feed, Food

In this lesson
  • Distinguish malting from feed barley
  • Meet the malting specification
  • Choose high-yielding feed varieties

Malting barley

Specific varieties bred for malting. Two-row barley is preferred over six-row in most markets. Key traits:

  • Low protein (10.5–12.5%) — high protein reduces malt extract
  • High germination (95%+)
  • Uniform grain size (above 2.5 mm)
  • Low moisture (below 12.5%)
  • Thin husk

Only varieties on the malting-approved list of the local brewery can be sold as malting barley. Feed barley cannot be sold into the malting market, even if it looks similar.

Feed barley

Any high-yielding barley. Protein 11–14%, no strict germination or size requirements. Prices track maize and feed wheat.

Food barley

Hull-less varieties for porridge and traditional foods. Small niche market in Ethiopia and the Middle East.

Lesson 3

Seed Sources and Quality

In this lesson
  • Know the seed classes
  • Test germination
  • Treat seed for smuts and bunts

Seed classes

  • Breeder seed: from the breeding programme
  • Pre-basic: first multiplication, disease-free
  • Basic: second multiplication
  • Certified (C1, C2): what most farmers buy
  • Farmer-saved: only for 1–2 seasons

Seed rate

  • Wheat: 100–150 kg/ha (irrigated); 120–160 kg/ha (rain-fed)
  • Barley: 80–120 kg/ha

Seed treatment

Wheat and barley seed must be treated against smuts (loose smut, covered smut), bunts, and soil-borne fungi. Common products: carboxin, triadimenol, or a combination fungicide. Follow the label rate exactly.

Germination test

Count 100 seeds, wrap in damp cloth, keep warm for 5–7 days. Above 85% germination is acceptable.

Lesson 4

Land Preparation for Cool-Season Crops

In this lesson
  • Prepare a fine, level seedbed
  • Manage residue from the previous crop
  • Level the field for irrigation

Tillage

  • Conventional: plough 20–25 cm deep, harrow to a fine, level tilth
  • Conservation: minimum tillage with residue retained — improves water holding and reduces erosion
  • Bed planting: raised beds for irrigated wheat in some systems, especially in South Asia and increasingly in Africa

Residue management

Retain residue if possible. It improves soil organic matter and reduces evaporation. But chop and spread evenly — large clumps interfere with seeding.

Land levelling

Critical for irrigated wheat. A 5 cm variation in field level causes 20–40% yield loss by uneven water distribution. Laser levelling is worth the investment for commercial wheat.

Soil pH

Wheat and barley prefer pH 6.0–7.5. Below 5.5, apply lime 6 months ahead. Above 8.0, some micronutrient deficiencies (zinc, iron) may appear.

Lesson 5

Rotations and Soil Health

In this lesson
  • Design a rotation that breaks disease cycles
  • Use legumes to fix nitrogen for wheat
  • Prevent the build-up of soil-borne pathogens

Why rotation matters

Continuous wheat builds up soil-borne diseases (take-all, crown rot, nematodes) and depletes soil nitrogen. A rotation with a legume breaks disease and fixes nitrogen.

Best rotations for African wheat

  • Wheat–legume (faba bean, chickpea, lentil): the classic rotation. Legume fixes 40–100 kg N/ha for the following wheat.
  • Wheat–maize (double crop): in irrigated systems where the season allows two crops.
  • Wheat–fallow: in dryland systems, fallow stores water for the next wheat crop.
  • Wheat–barley–legume: three-year rotation, breaks disease, improves soil.

Fallow and green manure

A 6–12 month fallow (with weeds controlled) stores water and nitrogen for the following wheat. Green manure crops (vetch, clover) can be grown and ploughed in for the same purpose.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. What is durum wheat used for?

Correct: pasta and couscous. Higher price than bread wheat.

2. What protein content does malting barley need?

Correct: 10.5–12.5%. Too high reduces malt extract.

3. What is the wheat seed rate for irrigated systems?

Correct: 100–150 kg/ha. Rain-fed slightly higher.

4. Why is land levelling critical for irrigated wheat?

Correct: uneven water causes 20–40% yield loss. Laser levelling pays back quickly.

5. Which rotation breaks wheat disease?

Correct: wheat after legume. Fixes nitrogen and breaks disease cycles.
