Module 10

Repair, Service and the Long Run

A cold room is a machine with a 15-year life if serviced, and a 3-year life if not. This module is the maintenance discipline that turns the capital you spent into a decade of service.

Module 10 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Set a preventive maintenance schedule for every component
  • Diagnose the five most common cold room failures
  • Detect refrigerant leaks before they become emergencies
  • Replace a door gasket and recognise when it needs replacing
  • Service the evaporator, condenser and controls
  • Decide between repair and replace for major components

Lesson 10.1

The preventive maintenance schedule

Most cold room failures are not sudden. They build slowly over weeks: a gasket hardening, a coil frosting, a charge leaking. A quarterly and annual inspection catches them while they are still cheap to fix.

The maintenance rule: $500 per year in preventive maintenance saves $3,000+ per year in emergency repairs and lost stock. The single most common cause of premature cold room failure is skipped maintenance.

Lesson 10.2

The five most common failures

1. Loss of refrigerant charge

Symptoms: Room warms slowly; compressor runs continuously; frost forms on only part of the evaporator; suction pressure low.

Cause: A leak. Common leak sites: flare fittings, brazed joints, schrader valves, or a damaged pipe.

Action: Do not simply top up. Find the leak first. A system that loses charge twice in a year has a leak that must be repaired. Topping up is expensive and eventually destroys the compressor.

2. Dirty condenser

Symptoms: High discharge pressure; compressor hot; high running cost; reduced capacity.

Cause: Dust, insects, leaves, or lint blocking the condenser coil.

Action: Clean the coil with a soft brush and compressed air or water (with unit off). Monthly if in dusty or dirty environment.

3. Iced evaporator

Symptoms: Airflow reducing; room warming despite compressor running; visible ice on the coil.

Cause: Defrost cycle failing, or door left open, or a gasket leak letting in moist air.

Action: Manual defrost (turn off, let melt, do not chip); check defrost timer settings; check door gasket.

4. Failed door gasket

Symptoms: Condensation on door frame; ice or water at the base; running cost rising.

Cause: Hardening, tearing, or shrinkage of the gasket.

Action: Replace. A gasket kit costs $50–200 and takes an hour to fit. Do not postpone — every day of leaking gasket wastes power and risks the crop.

5. Failed compressor

Symptoms: No cooling; compressor trips the breaker; loud noise; burnt smell.

Cause: Often the consequence of one of the above (running with low charge, dirty condenser, running continuously). A compressor rarely fails on its own.

Action: Replace. If the unit is over 10 years old, replace the whole system. If under 5, replace just the compressor. Either way, ask why the compressor failed — if you do not fix the underlying cause, the new compressor fails too.

Lesson 10.3

Refrigerant leak detection

The three detection methods

  1. Soap bubble test. Mix water and dish soap; brush onto every joint and fitting. Bubbles form where gas escapes. Cheap, effective, but requires visibility and slow leaks may be missed.
  2. Electronic leak detector. A handheld device that senses halogenated refrigerant in the air. $100–500. Fast, sensitive. Moves along joints, valves, pipe runs.
  3. Pressure decay test. Isolate the system, note pressures, wait 24 hours. Any pressure drop indicates a leak. Requires the system be stopped and valved off.

Common leak sites

  • Flare fittings — every 6 months check tightness
  • Schrader valves — often overlooked
  • Brazed joints — usually reliable but can crack
  • Pipe wall — vibration or abrasion against a support
  • Service valves — packing deterioration over time

Finding and fixing a leak

  1. Isolate the system and confirm there is a leak (pressure decay).
  2. Pressurise with dry nitrogen (never oxygen — it explodes with refrigerant oil).
  3. Use soap bubbles or electronic detector at every joint.
  4. Once found, repair: re-flare, re-braze, or replace the component.
  5. Replace the filter-dryer. A leak repairs alone does not clean the system; moisture may have entered.
  6. Pressure test again. Hold for 24 hours.
  7. Vacuum, recharge, verify performance.
Never charge a system with a known leak. You are buying refrigerant that will escape. A tiny leak that loses 100 g of refrigerant every 3 months costs 5× more over a year than fixing it once.

Lesson 10.4

Door gasket replacement

Signs a gasket needs replacing

  • Visible tears or cracks in the rubber
  • Hardening (gasket no longer flexes)
  • Shrinkage causing gaps at the corners
  • Paper test fails: place a strip of paper in the door, close it, and pull. If the paper slides out with no resistance, the gasket is not sealing there.
  • Visible condensation at the door frame

Replacement procedure

  1. Buy the correct gasket for the door. Manufacturer or universal fit.
  2. Open the door and pull the old gasket out of its groove. It may be screwed, pressed, or glued.
  3. Clean the groove. Any old glue or dirt affects the new gasket's seal.
  4. Soak the new gasket in warm water for 10 minutes to soften it — helps it seat.
  5. Press or screw the new gasket into the groove, working from one corner round.
  6. Close the door and check the seal by paper test at multiple points.
  7. Adjust hinges if needed to bring the door square to the frame.

Cost and frequency

  • Gasket kit: $50–200 depending on door size
  • Frequency: every 1–2 years, or whenever signs appear
  • Labour: 1–2 hours

This is the single highest-return maintenance item. A $100 gasket that stops a leaky door saves $100–300 in electricity per year and protects the crop.

Lesson 10.5

Repair vs replace

At some point every major component fails. The decision to repair or replace follows three rules.

Rule 1 — Repair if the system is under 8 years old and the failure is isolated

  • Compressor failed but rest of system is sound → replace compressor
  • Fan motor failed → replace fan
  • Controller failed → replace controller
  • Door gasket worn → replace gasket

Rule 2 — Replace if the system is over 12 years old and the failure is major

A 12-year-old system that loses its compressor is near the end of its economic life. A new compressor on an old system rarely lasts — the rest of the system is also aged. A new system is more efficient, has a warranty, and lasts another 12+ years.

Rule 3 — Replace immediately if the refrigerant is banned

Older refrigerants (R-22, R-12) are being phased out under the Montreal Protocol. If your system uses a banned refrigerant and needs service, replace the whole unit with one using an approved refrigerant (R-404A, R-134a, R-448A, or a natural refrigerant).

The long-run rule: a cold room is a 15-year asset if you service it. The annual service cost is 3–5% of the capital cost. Budget it and pay it. Every year you skip, the eventual repair bill grows.

Knowledge check — Module 10

1. What is the single most common cause of premature cold room failure?

Correct: skipped maintenance. Failures build slowly over weeks. Quarterly inspection catches them.

2. If the system loses refrigerant, what must you do before recharging?

Correct: find the leak first. Charging a leaking system wastes refrigerant and eventually destroys the compressor.

3. How often should door gaskets be replaced regardless of appearance?

Correct: every 1–2 years. Gaskets harden and shrink, even if they look fine. The paper test confirms.

4. When should a failing system be replaced rather than repaired?

Correct: age, refrigerant, multiple failures. A compressor on an old system rarely lasts — the rest is also aged.

5. What is the recommended annual maintenance budget for a cold room?

Correct: 3–5%. $500/year in preventive maintenance saves thousands in emergency repairs and lost stock.

Practical assignment — Module 10

Produce a maintenance plan for your cold room.

  1. Your monthly maintenance checklist with tasks and who is responsible.
  2. Your quarterly technician visit scope, with the specific items to be checked.
  3. Your annual replacement schedule (gasket, filters, other consumables).
  4. Your leak detection procedure and the tools you have available.
  5. Your gasket replacement procedure and where the spare gasket will be stored.
  6. Your repair-or-replace decision rules for compressor, controller, evaporator, condenser.
  7. Your maintenance budget line item, as a percentage of capital cost.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest maintenance risk in your operation and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 1 and 7. The schedule is the discipline; the budget is what makes it real.
