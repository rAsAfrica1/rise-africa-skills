1. Planning Your Hectare

One hectare is 100 × 100 metres — roughly the size of a football pitch. It is enough to feed a family of six well, and to generate $8,000–$25,000 per year in vegetable sales if it is properly planned. This module shows you how to think about the whole hectare as a system, not a random collection of beds.

Module 1 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 1.1 — How to measure and map your hectare

You cannot plan what you have not measured. Before buying a single seed, walk the plot with a tape measure. Note:

  • Total area. 1 hectare = 100 m × 100 m = 10,000 square metres.
  • Slope. Where does water flow? Which corner is lowest? Water pools there.
  • Soil type by zone. Dig 10 holes across the plot. Test each one by hand (see lesson 1.3).
  • Water source. Where is the borehole, dam, river or tank? How far from the middle of the plot?
  • Existing shade. Which areas are shaded by trees in the morning or afternoon?
  • Access paths. How do you currently walk from the gate to the furthest corner?

Draw a rough map on paper. Mark every tree, every rock, every slope, every drainage channel. This map is the foundation of everything that follows.

The map rule: Any successful farmer can draw a rough map of their plot from memory. If you cannot, you do not yet know your land well enough to farm it properly. Draw the map this week.
Practical exercise 1.1: Walk your hectare with a tape measure. Draw a scale map (1:200) on paper. Mark north, water source, trees, rocks, slopes, and any existing structures. This is your base map.

Lesson 1.2 — Soil preparation — the foundation of every crop

Soil is not just "dirt". It is a living ecosystem of minerals, organic matter, air, water, and billions of microorganisms. Your job as a vegetable farmer is to build and protect that ecosystem.

Soil typeFeel testVegetables that work
SandyGritty, falls apart, drains fastCarrots, beetroot, sweet potato, onions
Loam (ideal)Smooth, holds shape, crumbles when squeezedAlmost everything
ClaySticky, holds shape when wet, cracks when dryCabbage, broccoli, kale
SiltSmooth, floury when dry, slippery when wetLeafy greens, brassicas

Soil preparation for a hectare of vegetables:

  1. Clear the plot. Remove grass, weeds, rocks, and old crop residue. Cut tall weeds first with a slasher, then dig out roots with a fork.
  2. Deep dig or plough. Break the soil to 25–30 cm depth. Do this when soil is slightly moist — not bone dry, not waterlogged.
  3. Test the soil. Send 3 samples (from different zones) to a lab. Cost: $15–$40 per sample. Ask for pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium.
  4. Add organic matter. Spread 5–10 tonnes of compost or well-rotted manure per hectare. Yes — that much. This is the single most important investment.
  5. Mix. Work the compost into the top 15 cm with a fork or rotavator.
  6. Level. Rake level so irrigation water flows evenly. Uneven beds = dry spots and wet spots.
  7. Rest for 1–2 weeks before planting. This lets the soil settle and microorganisms multiply.
Practical exercise 1.2: Prepare one 10 m × 10 m test plot this weekend. Clear it fully, dig it deep, add 50 kg of compost. This is your practice bed before you tackle the whole hectare.

Lesson 1.3 — Water availability — the hard constraint

Vegetables need water. Not "some water" — 40–80,000 litres per hectare per day at peak season. Without reliable water, no amount of planning saves the crop.

Water sourceCapacity (L/hour)Can supportCost
River / stream (dry season)5,000–20,0000.5–2 hectaresFree / pumping cost
Borehole (deep)3,000–15,0000.3–1.5 hectares$1,500–$6,000 to drill
Dam / pondDepends on size0.5–3 hectares$500–$5,000 to build
Municipal / piped1,000–5,0000.1–0.5 hectaresUsage fees
Tank / rainwater onlyVariable0.05–0.2 hectaresFree but limited

How to calculate your maximum irrigable area:

  1. Measure your water source output in litres per hour (fill a 20 L bucket, time it).
  2. Multiply by how many hours you can pump per day.
  3. Divide by crop water need (average 5,000 L per hectare per hour of irrigation time).
  4. Example: borehole 8,000 L/h × 6 hours = 48,000 L/day ÷ 5,000 = 9.6 hectares. Wait — that is wrong. Let me redo: 48,000 L/day supports about 0.75 hectares of tomato at peak season.

Actually the correct calculation: tomato needs ~50,000 L/ha/day at peak. So 48,000 L/day supports about 0.96 hectare. Just under 1 hectare. Perfect for our target.

The water rule: Never plant more than your water source can reliably supply in the worst month of the dry season. Plan for a 20% shortfall. If your borehole gives 48,000 L/day in the wet season, assume 40,000 L/day at the end of the dry season when the water table is lowest.
Practical exercise 1.3: Measure your water source. Calculate your maximum irrigable area at 50,000 L per hectare per day. If it is under 1 hectare, either reduce your planting area or find more water before you commit.

Lesson 1.4 — Site layout — beds, paths, shade, storage

A hectare is a small factory. Every square metre must earn its keep. The layout determines how fast you can work and how much you can grow.

Standard 1-hectare layout:

ZonePercentagePurpose
Production beds65%Where crops grow — 6,500 m²
Paths and access15%Walking and wheelbarrow access — 1,500 m²
Water infrastructure5%Tank, pump, pipes, drip lines — 500 m²
Compost and manure area5%Composting, storage — 500 m²
Nursery and seedling area3%Seedlings for transplant — 300 m²
Storage shed / tool room2%Tools, harvest staging — 200 m²
Living / office / security5%If on-site — 500 m²

Bed layout rules:

  • Bed width: 1 m — reachable from both sides.
  • Path width: 40 cm between beds.
  • Main access paths: 1.2 m wide every 20 m.
  • Bed orientation: East–West for maximum sunlight, or up-slope for drainage.
  • Raised beds: 20 cm high in wet areas, 10 cm high in dry areas.
Practical exercise 1.4: Sketch your 1-hectare layout on paper. Mark the 7 zones. Show paths. Show where the water tank sits. Show the nursery. This is your master plan.

Lesson 1.5 — Crop selection — what sells in your area

Do not grow what you like. Grow what the market is already buying. Here are the top-performing vegetables in African markets:

Tomato75–90$0.80–$2.00Very highMedium

Cabbage75–100$0.40–$0.90HighEasy

Carrot90–120$0.50–$1.00MediumMedium
Beetroot75–100$0.60–$1.10MediumEasy

Lettuce45–60$0.80–$1.50HighMedium
Rape (broccoli family)60–75$0.50–$1.00MediumEasy

Rule of thumb for a 1-hectare mixed vegetable farm:

  • 40% long-cycle, high-value: tomato, onion, green pepper
  • 40% medium-cycle, reliable: cabbage, carrot, beetroot
  • 20% quick-cycle cash flow: kale, spinach, lettuce, rape

The 20% quick-cycle crops give you weekly cash flow while the 40% high-value crops are still growing.

The market rule: Spend a full day at your local market before planting. Watch what sells out first and what stays on the table. Ask 5 traders what they cannot get enough of. Plant that.
Practical exercise 1.5: Visit your local market for one full day. Interview 5 vegetable traders. Write down their top 3 crops by demand, their buying prices, and where they source from. This is your planting plan.

Lesson 1.6 — Crop rotation and intercropping

Growing the same crop in the same bed year after year destroys the soil. It depletes specific nutrients, encourages pests, and reduces yield. Rotation and intercropping solve this.

FamilyCropsMain nutrient need
BrassicasCabbage, kale, broccoli, rapeNitrogen
SolanaceaeTomato, pepper, eggplantPotassium
AlliumsOnion, garlic, leekSulphur, potassium
RootsCarrot, beetroot, radishPhosphorus
LegumesBeans, peas, groundnutsFix their own nitrogen
Leafy greensSpinach, lettuce, amaranthNitrogen

Rotation rules:

  1. Never follow a crop with another from the same family. Tomato → pepper is bad. Tomato → cabbage is good.
  2. Follow heavy feeders with light feeders. Cabbage → carrot is ideal.
  3. Include a legume every third season. Beans fix nitrogen, improving soil for the next crop.
  4. Keep a rotation record. Note which crop was in each bed for the last 3 seasons. Plan the next 3.

Intercropping (growing 2+ crops together):

  • Maize + beans: beans fix nitrogen, maize gives structure.
  • Cabbage + lettuce: cabbage is slower, lettuce fills the space between.
  • Tomato + basil: basil repels some tomato pests.
  • Onion + carrot: onion smell repels carrot fly.

What NOT to intercrop:

  • Two heavy feeders (both will be stunted)
  • Two tall crops (they shade each other)
  • Same-family crops (share diseases)
Practical exercise 1.6: Divide your hectare into 12 blocks (a 3×4 grid). Plan a 3-year rotation for each block: which crop family goes in which block in year 1, 2, 3. Post this plan in your tool shed.
