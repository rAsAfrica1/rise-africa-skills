Lesson 1

Knowing When to Harvest

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Judge the correct harvest time by variety, market and weevil pressure
  • Understand the trade-off between yield and quality
  • Plan harvest against the buyer's schedule

Sweet potato harvest timing is a balance between yield, market and pest pressure.

By variety

  • Early varieties: 90–100 days. Suitable for quick cash and drought-prone areas.
  • Medium varieties: 100–120 days. The standard for most African smallholders.
  • Late varieties: 120–150 days. Higher yield but longer risk exposure.

By market

  • Fresh table market: harvest at full maturity for maximum size and sweetness.
  • Processing: slightly earlier is often preferred — root flesh is firmer and dry matter higher.
  • OFSP institutional: harvest at peak beta-carotene content, which is close to full maturity.

By weevil pressure

In weevil-heavy areas, harvest earlier than the variety would suggest. A 4-month crop with 10% weevil damage beats a 5-month crop with 40%.

Signs of readiness

  • Vines begin to yellow and dry at the tips
  • Skin of sample roots is firm and does not slip easily
  • Roots have reached their full size for the variety
  • The soil is starting to crack around the ridges

Lesson 2

Harvesting Without Damage

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Lift roots without cutting or bruising them
  • Handle with care from field to packhouse
  • Minimise the visible and invisible damage that shortens shelf life

Lifting technique

  • Water the ridges lightly 1–2 days before harvest if the soil is very dry. This softens the soil and makes lifting easier.
  • Use a fork or hoe to loosen the soil around the ridge, then lift the roots by hand.
  • Never pull the vine to extract roots — you break the roots and leave some in the ground.
  • Cut roots free from the vine with a clean cut, leaving 1–2 cm of stalk attached.

Handling

  • Place roots gently in crates or baskets — never throw or drop.
  • Do not wash at the field. Lightly brush off loose soil; washing shortens storage life.
  • Keep roots in the shade between field and storage.
  • Sort immediately: marketable, processing, and damaged/reject.

Shelf life

Fresh sweet potato stored under typical African tropical conditions (25–30°C) lasts 2–4 weeks at best without curing. With proper curing and cool storage it lasts 2–5 months.

Lesson 3

Curing — the Step Most Farmers Skip

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand what curing does and why it matters
  • Set up a curing room or area
  • Cure for 4–10 days at the right temperature and humidity

Curing is a controlled post-harvest treatment that heals minor wounds and thickens the root skin. It is the difference between 3-week storage and 3-month storage.

How curing works

At 28–30°C and 85–90% relative humidity for 4–10 days, the root forms a corky layer (periderm) beneath wounded areas. This seals the wound and blocks rot organisms. The skin also thickens across the whole root, reducing moisture loss.

How to cure without a curing room

  • Simple method: pile roots in a shaded, well-ventilated shed at 28–30°C for 5–7 days. Cover lightly with a damp cloth to raise humidity.
  • Evaporative cooling chamber: roots in crates lined with damp sand or cloth, in a shaded structure. Effective and cheap.
  • Do not cure in direct sun. Sun causes sunscald and shortens storage life.
  • Do not cure in a sealed, unventilated room. Roots respire and need airflow.

After curing

Move roots to cool, dark, well-ventilated storage. Ideal storage temperature is 12–15°C and 85–90% humidity. In practice, most smallholder storage is at ambient temperature (18–25°C) and humidity varies — that is why curing is critical, because cured roots tolerate imperfect conditions much better.

Lesson 4

Storage Options

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Compare traditional, improved and cooled storage
  • Choose the method that fits your volume and market
  • Monitor stored roots for damage and losses

Traditional storage

Pit storage, heap storage in the field, or shade under trees. Cheap, simple, losses 20–40% over 3 months. Still used where modern methods are unavailable.

Improved ventilated storage

A simple shed with slatted shelves, good airflow, and shaded. Roots cured then stored in shallow crates. Losses 10–20% over 2–3 months. Construction cost $200–1,500. Best entry-level improvement.

Cold storage

Cold room at 12–15°C. Losses 5–10% over 4–6 months. Capital cost $3,000–15,000+. Worth it only at commercial scale (10+ tonnes stored regularly). Off-season prices can justify the investment if you are a consistent producer.

Sand storage

Roots layered in dry, clean sand in a cool, dark place. Traditional method used in many African countries. Extends storage by weeks if the sand is dry and the location is cool.

Storage monitoring

  • Check weekly for rot, weevil, or moisture
  • Remove any damaged roots immediately
  • Keep the storage area rodent-free
  • Rotate stock: first in, first out
Lesson 5

Processing — Flour, Chips, Puree

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Turn glut or off-grade roots into higher-value products
  • Understand the flour, chip and puree processes
  • Decide which product fits your market

Sweet potato flour

Process: wash, peel, slice thinly, dry (sun 2–3 days or solar dryer 1 day), mill to flour. Yield: 4–5 kg fresh root → 1 kg flour. Storage life 6–12 months if dry. Uses: composite flour for baking (10–30% substitution), porridge, weaning foods. Price: 3–6x fresh root per kg.

Sweet potato chips / crisps

Process: wash, peel, slice (2–3 mm for crisps, 5–8 mm for chips), blanch, fry or oven-bake, salt, pack. Yield: 3–4 kg fresh → 1 kg finished chips. Price: 6–10x fresh root. Requires frying oil, packaging, and food-safety compliance.

Sweet potato puree

Cooked, mashed, and packaged sweet potato. Uses: baby food, weaning, institutional feeding programs (especially OFSP for vitamin A). Shelf life requires refrigeration or canning. Price: 4–8x fresh root.

OFSP puree for institutional buyers

The fastest-growing value-added market in OFSP. Schools, clinics, and nutrition programs buy puree in bulk for feeding schemes. Requires food-safety certification and often consistent weekly supply.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. When is sweet potato ready to harvest?

Correct: yellowing vines, firm roots, per variety. Harvest in weevil-heavy areas earlier than maximum.

2. What is the correct curing temperature?

Correct: 28–30 °C, 85–90% humidity. Wounds heal, skin thickens, storage life triples.

3. How long does curing take?

Correct: 4–10 days. Longer for larger roots.

4. How much does curing extend storage life?

Correct: 3 weeks to 3 months. Curing is the difference between a quick sale and a stored crop.

5. How many kg of fresh root make 1 kg of flour?

Correct: 4–5. The 4:1 to 5:1 ratio makes flour a real margin product from glut roots.
