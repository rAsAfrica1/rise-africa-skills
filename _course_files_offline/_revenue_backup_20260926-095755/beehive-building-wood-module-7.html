Module 7

The Bee Space and Why It Decides Everything

You met bee space in Module 1. This module is the full treatment — every measurement, every consequence, and the tools and habits that keep your hive within the range the bees demand.

Lesson 7.1

The three bee space measurements

Bee space is not one number. It is a range, and it applies in three different ways.

MeasurementRangeWhere it applies
Comb-to-comb spacing (comb centre to comb centre)35 mmFrame spacing in Langstroth, bar spacing in top-bar
Comb face to parallel surface6–9 mmFrame side bars to box wall; frame top to inner cover
Floor-to-comb clearance6–9 mmBottom of comb to hive floor

Comb-to-comb (35 mm)

This is the spacing between the centres of two adjacent combs. It allows bees to pass between them without touching both sides — exactly the gap they would leave in a wild colony.

In Langstroth: 35 mm between frame centres. In top-bar: 35 mm between bar centres. In masonry hive: 35 mm between top-bar notches.

Comb-to-wall (6–9 mm)

The gap between the face of the comb and the nearest parallel surface — a wall, a frame side bar, a floor, an inner cover. Between 6 and 9 mm, bees leave it empty. Below 6 mm, they propolise it shut. Above 10 mm, they build comb in it (burr comb).

Floor-to-comb (6–9 mm)

The gap between the bottom of the comb and the hive floor. Same rule — 6 to 9 mm is the empty zone. Too small and bees propolise; too large and they build comb on the floor.

The three-number rule: 35 mm between combs. 6–9 mm everywhere else. These three measurements decide whether your hive works or fights you at every inspection.

Lesson 7.2

What happens when bee space is wrong

Too narrow (under 6 mm)

Bees fill the gap with propolis — a sticky, resinous substance they collect from tree buds and sap. Propolis has three functions: it seals draughts, it sterilises surfaces, and it glues things together.

For the beekeeper, propolis is a nuisance. Frames become glued to walls. Inner covers become glued to boxes. Every inspection takes longer, damages comb, and stresses the colony.

Too wide (over 10 mm)

Bees build burr comb — irregular comb in the gap. Burr comb is a problem because:

  • It wastes bee effort (they build comb that will be destroyed).
  • It jams frames together during inspection.
  • It creates unnecessary drone comb (which uses resources without producing honey).
  • It makes the hive harder to work.

The cumulative cost

A hive with 1 mm too little bee space throughout takes 10–15 minutes longer per inspection. Over a season of 20 inspections, that is 4 hours of extra work. Plus the damaged comb, the irritated bees, and the higher risk of rolling the queen.

A hive built to correct bee space takes 3–5 minutes per inspection. Clean lifts. Calm bees. That difference compounds over years.

Lesson 7.3

Measuring bee space in a built hive

The tools

  • Steel rule or tape measure — 300 mm with 1 mm graduations.
  • Feeler gauge or a set of known shims — optional, for measuring gaps.
  • A piece of 8 mm dowel or a wooden matchstick — 8 mm is the centre of bee space. If the dowel slides into the gap with a slight friction, the space is correct.

What to measure

  1. Frame-to-frame spacing: measure from the centre of one frame top bar to the centre of the next. Should be 35 mm.
  2. Frame-to-wall gap: measure the clearance between the outermost frame side bar and the box interior wall. Should be 6–9 mm.
  3. Frame-to-inner-cover: measure the clearance between the top of the frame top bar and the underside of the inner cover. Should be 6–9 mm.
  4. Frame-to-floor: measure the clearance between the bottom of the frame and the hive floor. Should be 6–9 mm.

The 8 mm test

Cut a piece of wood 8 mm thick. Slide it into every gap that should be bee space. If it slides freely, the gap is over 8 mm. If it does not fit, the gap is under 8 mm. If it slides with slight friction, the gap is correct.

This one test takes 15 minutes and tells you everything you need to know about a built hive.

Lesson 7.4

Correcting bee space errors

The errors and their fixes

ErrorSymptomFix
Frame side bars too longFrames jam against box wall; propolisTrim 5–10 mm off each side bar with a plane or saw
Frame side bars too shortFrames shift; bees build comb on the wallsAdd a thin shim or spacer to the rabbet, or replace the frames
Rabbet too deepFrames sit low; comb nearly touches the floorAdd a thin strip of wood to raise the rabbet depth
Rabbet too shallowFrames sit high; top bar does not sit flushDeepen the rabbet carefully, or replace the box
Frame spacing too tight (under 35 mm)Frames propolised togetherRemove one frame from the box; use a spacer tool to re-space
Frame spacing too wide (over 40 mm)Burr comb between framesAdd extra frames, or use a spacer tool to space evenly
Top bar too wide in top-bar hiveBar rest gap too small; bars glued inRe-cut bar rest wider, or re-plane the bar to width
Top bar too narrowBars rattle; bees build burr combAdd a shim on the bar rest, or replace the bars

The spacer tool

A simple metal or wooden spacer that fits between the frame top bars. When you re-insert frames after inspection, the spacer holds each at exactly 35 mm. Cost $5–20. It eliminates 90% of spacing errors.

When to fix

Fix bee space errors during the build, not after. If a frame does not fit correctly, cut a new one. If the rabbet is wrong, re-cut it. Fixing after the bees have built comb means destroying comb and stressing the colony.

Lesson 7.5

Imperial and metric — working with both

Bee space was measured in the imperial system in 1852. Modern hives are built in metric. The conversions matter because a "close enough" metric approximation is often too far off.

The key conversions

ImperialMetricUse
3/8 inch9.53 mmTraditional bee space upper limit
5/16 inch7.94 mmTraditional bee space lower limit
1 3/8 inch34.93 mmFrame spacing (comb centre to comb centre)
19 inches482.6 mmFrame top bar length
7/16 inch11.11 mmRabbet depth (Langstroth standard)

The metric roundings

Most modern hive plans round to the nearest millimetre:

  • Bee space: 8 mm (was 9.53)
  • Frame spacing: 35 mm (was 34.93)
  • Frame top bar: 483 mm (was 482.6)
  • Rabbet depth: 11 mm (was 11.11)

These roundings are within tolerance. The important thing is that every part of the hive is consistent with every other part. If your frames are 35 mm spacing but your box is built to imperial, small mismatches accumulate.

The rule

Pick one system. Build every hive in your apiary to the same system. Do not mix metric and imperial in the same hive or the same apiary. A hive that is off by 2 mm from another hive in the same apiary is a hive whose parts will not interchange.

The measurement rule: pick one standard (metric is fine), build everything to it, and check bee space with the 8 mm test on every new hive. This single discipline separates an apiary that runs smoothly from one that fights the beekeeper.

Knowledge check — Module 7

1. What is the correct frame-to-frame spacing in a Langstroth hive?

Correct: 35 mm. This is the comb-to-comb spacing that lets bees pass between combs.

2. What happens if bee space is under 6 mm?

Correct: propolis. Bees seal gaps under 6 mm. This makes inspection difficult and damages comb.

3. What is the 8 mm test?

Correct: dowel test. 8 mm is the centre of bee space. Simple, fast, and definitive.

4. What is the correct response to a frame whose side bars are too long?

Correct: trim the side bars. A quick fix that restores bee space and stops propolis buildup.

5. Why must every hive in an apiary be built to the same measurement system?

Correct: interchangeability. In an apiary, a super from one hive must fit any other. Mixed standards break this.

Practical assignment — Module 7

Audit the bee space in one hive in your apiary.

  1. The three bee space measurements you tested (frame-to-frame, frame-to-wall, frame-to-floor), with actual figures for each.
  2. The 8 mm test result at every frame in the hive.
  3. Any errors found and how you corrected them.
  4. Photographs showing a frame sitting in the box, with the 8 mm dowel in place at the wall gap.
  5. Your hive's overall assessment — is it within bee space throughout, or does it need corrections?
  6. Your plan to correct any remaining errors.
  7. One paragraph (150 words) on why bee space matters more than any other measurement in a hive, and what you will do differently on your next build.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 7. The dowel test tells the truth about a hive; the paragraph is where you commit to building better.
