Module 12 — Mushroom Business: Costing, Packing and Marketing

From bags on the shelf to money in the bank — the numbers, packaging and customers that make it work.

1. Costing — the number that decides everything

Mushroom growing has a reputation for high margins. In practice, margins depend entirely on knowing your cost per kilogram. Calculate it for every batch.

Cost lineIncludeCommonly forgotten
SubstrateStraw or sawdust, bran, gypsum, limeTransport to the site
SpawnPer kg of substrate, at your spawn rateShipping; contamination losses in spawn itself
Bags and consumablesBags, clips, gloves, alcohol, filtersThe small stuff that adds up
EnergyPasteurisation, lighting, air exchange, cooling, heatingHeat-up time, not just running time
WaterSubstrate wetting, cleaning, mistingWastewater disposal if charged
LabourPrep, inoculation, harvest, packing, deliveryYour own time at a real rate
PackagingPunnets, bags, labels, cartonsCartons and delivery boxes
DeliveryVehicle, fuel, ice packs, cool boxesTime spent delivering
Building shareRent, depreciation, maintenanceRepairs to doors, seals, filters
Losses5–15 % of production lost to contamination, spoilage, cullsThe green bags and the unsold mushrooms

Worked costing — 20-bag weekly batch, oyster mushrooms

Inputs per week:
Substrate (100 kg wet): 18
Spawn (8 % rate, 8 kg): 22
Bags, clips, consumables: 8
Energy (pasteurisation, lights, air exchange): 20
Water and cleaning: 5
Labour (harvest, pack, deliver — 8 h): 48
Packaging (60 punnets at 0.20): 12
Delivery: 10
Building share: 8
Total weekly cost = 151

Output per week:
Expected yield: 30 kg marketable mushrooms.
Losses (culls, spoilage): 2 kg, deducted.
Net saleable: 30 kg.

Cost per kg = 151 / 30 = 5.03

At 8/kg retail: margin = (8 − 5.03) / 8 = 37 %.
At 6/kg wholesale: margin = (6 − 5.03) / 6 = 16 % — too thin.

The lever: increase yield per bag, not price. Every extra 0.1 kg per bag raises margin by 3–4 %.

3. Packaging

Mushrooms are 90 % water and they continue to respire after harvest. Packaging must allow gas exchange while protecting the mushrooms from bruising and moisture loss.

PackSizeBest forNotes
Perforated punnet + film lid200 g, 250 g, 400 gRetail, supermarkets, delisMust be perforated — sealed film sweats and rots
Paper bag250 g – 1 kgFarmers' markets, direct salesBreathes naturally; shows mushrooms nicely
Kraft box with vent holes500 g – 2 kgRestaurants, bulk directStackable, protective, still breathable
Vacuum packBulkWholesale, restaurantsOnly if you will cook or process quickly; sealed packs sweat if held long
Shallow crate + liner2–5 kgMarket, wholesaleHarvest into crate, no repacking; best for fresh quality

Never pack warm mushrooms in sealed plastic

Warm mushrooms in a sealed bag sweat within minutes, the condensation causes slime, and the pack spoils. Cool the mushrooms to 4 °C first, then pack into breathable material.

Label contents

  • Product name and species (e.g. "Oyster mushrooms — Pleurotus ostreatus").
  • Net weight.
  • Your business name and address.
  • Batch code and packing date.
  • Storage instruction: "Keep refrigerated below 4 °C. Use within 5 days".
  • Best-before date.
  • Any allergen declaration if applicable.

4. Cooling and delivery

Mushroom shelf life is set by how quickly and how well you cool them after harvest.

StepTimingMethod
HarvestCool morning; before the room is mistedTwist and pull; trim base; straight into clean crates
Pre-coolWithin 1 hour2–4 °C cool room; shallow crates so cold air reaches all mushrooms
Grade and packIn the cool roomSort by size; discard damaged or over-mature
Cold storageUntil delivery2–4 °C, 85–90 % RH, dark; shelf life 5–10 days
DeliverWithin 24 hInsulated box or cool vehicle; ice packs; keep out of sun

The value of a cold chain

Mushrooms at 20 °C: shelf life 1–2 days. Spoilage losses 20–30 %.
Mushrooms at 4 °C: shelf life 5–10 days. Spoilage losses 5–8 %.

On a 30 kg weekly crop at 8/kg:
Without cooling: 25 % loss = 7.5 kg lost = 60 lost revenue per week.
With cooling: 6 % loss = 1.8 kg lost = 14 lost revenue per week.
Difference = 46 per week, or 2 400 per year. That is far more than the running cost of a small cool room. Cold chain is not an expense — it is the largest single margin improvement available.

Restaurants are the sweet spot for small growers

A single restaurant that buys 2–4 kg per week at 8–10/kg gives you a reliable customer, a fixed delivery day and honest feedback on quality. Ten such customers is 20–40 kg per week — most of a small operation — with better margins than wholesale and less work than a market stall.

6. Pricing

ApproachWhen
Cost plus marginAlways as the floor — never sell below cost plus a real margin
Market comparisonCheck what mushrooms sell for at your target market and channels
Premium positioningFresh, locally grown, exotic species, organic — charge above commodity price and deliver on the claim
Volume contractOnly at prices that still make a real margin; never as a loss leader
Bundle / boxFor subscription customers; sets higher average value per sale

Pricing decision — worked example

Cost per kg = 5.03. Target margin 40 % of selling price.

Price = 5.03 / (1 − 0.40) = 8.38
Round to 8.50/kg retail.

Restaurant price: 7.50/kg (12 % below retail) — still 33 % margin.
Wholesale price: must be above 6.50/kg to make a real margin.

If your cost is above 6.50/kg, wholesale is not a viable channel. Focus on direct and restaurant sales.

8. Scaling

StageWeekly productionFocus
Start5–10 kgLearn the process; one species; one channel
Established20–40 kgAdd a second flush schedule; build a customer list; refine cost per kg
Commercial small50–100 kgAdd a second room; stagger crops; hire part-time harvest labour
Commercial200+ kgAutomate air exchange and humidity; add species; add drying or processing for surplus

Do not scale before the numbers work

Scaling a business with a thin margin makes the loss bigger, not the profit. Prove cost per kg and price at 20–40 kg per week before you invest in a second room. The commonest failure in mushroom growing is scaling before the numbers are proven.

9. Closing — course review

You have now designed, built and learned to run a brick mushroom house: the shell, insulation, air exchange, shelving, lighting, harvesting, scheduling, contamination control, substrate preparation, pest and disease management, and the business.

The next steps in your operation are usually one of:

  • Add a species. Once you have one species reliable, adding a second — shiitake, lion's mane, milky — opens new markets at higher prices.
  • Value-add. Dried mushrooms, powders, pickled mushrooms, mushroom salt. Uses surplus and culls; commands a higher price per kg.
  • Add a second room. Enables a true year-round staggered schedule and reduces contamination risk.
  • Teach. A working mushroom house is an excellent teaching site; workshops and training generate income with little extra capital.

The habit that matters most

Weigh every harvest and calculate cost per kg every month. The number tells you what is really working. Without it, you are guessing about the one thing that decides whether the business survives.
