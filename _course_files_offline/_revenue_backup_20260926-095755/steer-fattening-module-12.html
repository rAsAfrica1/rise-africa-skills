Compliance, Welfare and Your Complete Batch Plan

The last module covers the things that can close a business rather than merely cost it money: registering properly, identifying and moving cattle legally, medicine records and withdrawal periods, welfare in handling and transport, and manure and neighbours. Lesson 12.114 min

Registering the Business and the Records an Inspection Wants

In this lesson
  • Identify the five registrations to ask about in your country
  • Know which office answers which question
  • Keep the record set an inspection will actually ask for

Everything in this course so far has been about producing a finished animal profitably. This module is about the rules that decide whether you are allowed to, and whether you can prove it. These rules are the difference between a business that scales and one that gets stopped.

Two honest warnings before we start. First, the detail differs in every country and frequently by province or district, so this lesson teaches you what categories of thing to ask about and who to ask, not what your answer will be. Second, this is not legal advice. Where the answer matters — and for slaughter, movement and medicines it does — get it from the office that enforces it, and get it in writing.

The five registrations to ask about

  1. Business registration. Whatever your country's basic trading registration is: sole trader, business name, cooperative, or company. Ask what the threshold is; many people trade informally for years and then find they cannot invoice a formal buyer, open a business account, or bid for a supply contract.
  2. Tax registration. Income tax and, above a turnover threshold, value added tax or its equivalent. Formal buyers such as abattoirs and supermarkets frequently cannot buy from an unregistered seller, or must withhold tax if they do.
  3. Livestock or holding registration. Many countries require the premises where livestock are kept to be registered and issued a holding number. That number is often what links an animal to you in the traceability system.
  4. Local authority permission. Keeping cattle in numbers is frequently subject to local by-laws, particularly near residential areas. Ask before you build, not after a complaint.
  5. Environmental or water permission. Where you have significant effluent or you draw water from a river or borehole, there may be a separate permit.

Make it one visit, with a list

The single most efficient thing in this lesson: go to your district veterinary or livestock office once, in person, with a written list of questions, and come away with the forms and the phone numbers. That office generally knows the answers to movement, disease, slaughter and holding registration, and is generally willing to explain them. People lose months guessing at things they could have been told in twenty minutes.

The record set an inspection actually asks for

Across most jurisdictions the same handful of records are requested. You have already been building most of them in Modules 1, 7 and 8; this is the compliance view of them.

  • An animal register. Every animal, its identification, the date it arrived, who it came from, and the date and destination when it left.
  • Movement documents. Permits or clearance certificates in and out, filed and kept for whatever period your country requires.
  • A medicine record. Covered in Lesson 3, and usually the record most closely inspected.
  • Feed purchase records. Invoices identifying the lot, which is what lets a feed problem be traced.
  • Mortality records. Deaths, dates, and cause where known. An unexplained cluster of deaths is exactly the thing a veterinary officer will ask about.
  • Staff records, where you employ anyone, including terms and any training given.

Keep them where they can be found

A record you cannot produce on the day is the same as no record. One box file, one book per category, kept in one place that is not the feed store. Write in ink. If you keep anything on a phone, photograph the paper too, because phones are lost and broken far more often than box files are.

Lesson 12.216 min

Identification, Movement Permits and Proving Where an Animal Came From

In this lesson
  • Choose and apply an identification method
  • Plan movement permits into your sale calendar
  • Demand the right paperwork from a seller

Traceability is the ability to say, with paper to back it, where an animal came from and where it went. Every country's system differs, but the reasons behind them are the same everywhere: controlling disease outbreaks, controlling stock theft, and giving meat buyers confidence about origin. For a fattener who buys animals from other people, traceability is not bureaucracy. It is the thing that protects you from buying someone else's problem.

Whatever your country requires officially, do one extra thing for yourself: photograph every animal on arrival from both sides, with the tag number visible, and file it with the purchase record. It costs nothing and it has resolved more ownership disputes than any argument ever has.

Movement permits

Most countries in the region require a permit or clearance to move cattle between areas, and frequently restrict movement in and out of disease control zones entirely. Foot and mouth control zones are the common case: a movement that is routine in one district may be prohibited in another, and the restriction can be imposed at short notice during an outbreak.

The practical consequences for a fattener are concrete:

  • A permit is usually needed before the animal moves, not afterwards. Buying first and asking later is how animals end up stranded.
  • Movement out of a restricted zone may be refused entirely, which can strand a finished batch away from its market at exactly the wrong moment.
  • Permits commonly have a validity window and a stated route or destination. Moving late, or somewhere else, can invalidate it.
  • Some permits require a recent dip or veterinary clearance. That has to be scheduled before the sale date, not on it.

Build this into your batch calendar. When you plan a sale window in Module 9, add the permit lead time in your district and work backwards from it.

Buying: what to demand from the seller

Module 2 taught you to spot the warning signs of undocumented stock. Here is the paperwork side of it.

  1. A receipt naming the seller, with their identification details, the date, the price, and the animals described with their identification numbers.
  2. The clearance or movement document your district requires for the purchase.
  3. Confirmation of where the animal has been, particularly whether it has come from a restricted zone.
  4. The seller's own record of any treatment given, which matters for Lesson 3. An animal treated shortly before sale may carry a withdrawal period you do not know about.

If any of that is refused, walk away. An animal you cannot prove you own is an animal you cannot legally sell, and in a theft investigation the person holding the animal is the person answering the questions.

Your own animal register

One book, one line per animal, and it stays with the business for as long as your country requires.

Lesson 12.316 min

Medicines, Records and Withdrawal Periods

In this lesson
  • Keep a medicine record that satisfies an inspection
  • Calculate the earliest legal slaughter date from a label
  • Store, dose and dispose of medicines correctly

Module 7 covered recognising disease and knowing when to call a vet. This lesson covers the legal and food-safety side of the same subject, which is where a fattener is most likely to cause real harm without meaning to. Meat from an animal slaughtered inside a withdrawal period is not safe food, and in most jurisdictions the carcass is condemned and the seller is liable.

What a withdrawal period is

A withdrawal period is the minimum time that must pass between the last dose of a medicine and slaughter, so that residues fall below the legal maximum. It is set for each product by the licensing authority, it is printed on the label and in the package insert, and it is not a recommendation.

Several things about it catch people out:

  • It varies enormously between products treating the same condition. Two antibiotics for the same infection can have very different withdrawal periods, and that difference may matter more to you than the price.
  • It is counted from the last dose, not the first. A three-day course extends the clock by three days.
  • It can differ by route. The same active ingredient injected and applied on the skin may carry different periods.
  • Off-label use changes everything. If a vet prescribes a product outside its labelled use, the labelled withdrawal period no longer applies and the vet must specify one. Ask for it in writing.
  • Some products carry a withdrawal period for milk as well as meat. Not your main concern in a fattening pen, but relevant if you ever hold breeding stock.

Calculating the earliest safe slaughter date

  1. Find the date of the last dose.
  2. Read the withdrawal period for meat from the label of the exact product used, including the concentration.
  3. Count forward that many days from the last dose. That is the earliest legal slaughter date.
  4. Write that date in the animal register and on a board in the pen, not only in your head.
  5. Leave yourself margin. If the date is tight against your sale window, feed on rather than cutting it fine.

Dosing by weight, and why underdosing is the greater sin

Almost every medicine is dosed per kilogram of body weight. Guessing weight in a fattening pen is a poor idea in both directions. Overdosing wastes money and can be toxic; underdosing fails to clear the infection and contributes directly to antimicrobial resistance, which is a genuine public-health problem and not an abstract one. Use the girth tape from Module 6 and dose to the measured weight, rounding up to the nearest sensible increment rather than down.

Storage and disposal

  • Store medicines as the label says. Many need refrigeration, and a product that has been warm is not a product with a known potency.
  • Check expiry dates before use and dispose of expired product properly rather than using it up.
  • Never decant into an unlabelled bottle. The label carries the withdrawal period, and a bottle without one is a bottle you cannot legally rely on.
  • Sharps go in a sealed puncture-proof container, not a bin. Ask your vet where to take them.
  • Keep the medicine cabinet locked, away from feed, and away from children.

Antimicrobial use is increasingly regulated

Many countries in the region are tightening rules on veterinary antimicrobials, particularly on the routine use of antibiotics for growth promotion rather than treatment. Assume the direction of travel is toward more restriction and better records, not less. Ask your veterinary office what currently applies, use antibiotics for treatment on veterinary advice rather than as a routine ration additive, and keep the records — they are increasingly the thing that lets you sell to a formal buyer at all.

Lesson 12.416 min

Welfare in Handling, Loading and Transport

In this lesson
  • Use the flight zone and point of balance to move cattle calmly
  • Decide honestly whether an animal is fit to travel
  • Load and transport in a way that protects welfare and carcass value

Welfare and margin are not in conflict in a fattening pen; they are the same subject seen from two angles. Rough handling costs you gain, bruising, downgraded carcasses and injured people. Everything in this lesson is defensible both as decency and as arithmetic.

How cattle actually see and move

Cattle have very wide, close to panoramic vision with a blind spot directly behind them, and they see depth poorly. That explains most of what frustrates people about moving them.

  • The flight zone is the space around an animal within which it moves away from you. Step inside it and the animal moves; step out and it stops. Working the edge of that zone moves cattle calmly; charging into the middle of it makes them run or turn back.
  • The point of balance is roughly at the shoulder. Move behind it and the animal goes forward; move in front of it and the animal goes back. Almost every failed attempt to push an animal forward involves someone standing in front of its shoulder.
  • Shadows, glare, puddles, dangling chains and changes in floor colour all read as obstacles. Cattle balk at them, the handler shouts, and everything gets worse. Fixing the race is faster than fixing the cattle.
  • They follow each other. Move animals in small groups rather than singly. A single animal separated from the group is stressed and dangerous.

Handling rules that protect gain and people

  1. Move slowly and quietly. Shouting and stick use are signs the facility is wrong, not that the cattle are difficult.
  2. No electric goads as routine practice. Where used at all it must be rare, brief and never on the face, udder or genitals — and many buyers and markets now prohibit them outright.
  3. Never twist tails. It causes real pain and can break the tail.
  4. Give non-slip footing everywhere cattle walk. A slipped animal bruises the carcass and may never walk properly again.
  5. Never work cattle alone in a confined space. Have an escape route in every pen and race. Cattle kill people every year, and it is almost always at handling.
  6. Keep the routine consistent, as Module 6 taught. Cattle that are handled calmly and regularly load in a fraction of the time.

Fitness to travel

Before any animal is loaded, decide honestly whether it is fit to travel. An animal that is not fit to travel includes, at minimum, one that cannot bear weight on all four limbs or walk unaided, one that is severely emaciated, one with a fresh untreated injury or a prolapse, one that is heavily pregnant or has given birth very recently, and one that is inside a medicine withdrawal period. Loading an unfit animal is a welfare offence in most jurisdictions and the carcass will usually be condemned anyway.

A downer animal — one that cannot rise — is never dragged, never lifted by a limb or the tail, and never loaded. Call your vet. The decision is between treatment on the spot and humane emergency slaughter on the spot, and it is a decision for a vet, not a haulier with a deadline.

Loading and the journey

  • Load in the cool of the day where you can, and never leave a loaded vehicle standing in the sun.
  • Do not overload, and do not underload. Overcrowded animals go down and get trampled; too much space means they are thrown about. They should be able to stand comfortably without being able to fall far.
  • Non-slip floor, bedded. Partition long loads.
  • Do not mix animals from different groups, and do not load horned with polled animals together, on the same vehicle if it can be avoided.
  • Withhold feed only as your abattoir or buyer requires, and keep water available for as long as possible before loading. Extended water deprivation is both a welfare problem and a direct cause of shrink.
  • Drive smoothly. Most bruising happens at corners, braking and on rough ground, not at speed.
  • Know your country's rules on maximum journey time, rest and watering, and carry the movement paperwork with the load.

Shrink and bruising, in money

Module 9 covered shrink as a marketing issue. Here is the welfare link stated plainly: the same practices that reduce fear and rough contact — calm handling, short waits, non-slip floors, smooth driving, water until loading — are the practices that reduce weight loss in transit and bruise trim at the abattoir. The buyer pays you for what arrives in good condition. Welfare is not a cost you absorb for decency's sake; it is most of the difference between the weight you loaded and the weight you are paid for.

Lesson 12.514 min

Manure, Effluent, Flies and Neighbours

In this lesson
  • Value your manure against local fertiliser prices
  • Compost and site a heap so it does not pollute
  • Control flies mechanically and keep neighbours on your side

A pen of cattle produces a great deal of manure and, if it rains, a great deal of contaminated run-off. Handled well, that is fertility you would otherwise buy. Handled badly, it is flies, smell, a polluted watercourse, a complaint to the council, and eventually a reason you cannot expand.

Manure is an asset with a value you can calculate

Cattle manure carries nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, plus organic matter that improves soil structure and water holding. The nutrient content varies widely with diet, bedding and how it was stored, so the honest approach is not to work from a published figure but to ask your extension service what local composted cattle manure typically tests at, and then to price it against the cost of the equivalent bagged fertiliser locally. For many fatteners, doing that arithmetic once changes manure from a nuisance into a second product.

There are three realistic destinations, and it is worth deciding which is yours before you build the heap:

  1. Onto your own land. Cheapest and usually best if you have crops, especially if you are growing your own silage from Module 11. The nutrient loop closes on your own farm.
  2. Sold or given to neighbouring crop farmers. Often the easiest arrangement, and it makes the neighbours who would otherwise complain about the smell into people with a reason to tolerate it.
  3. Into a biogas digester. Real, established technology, and it produces both gas and a digestate that still carries most of the nutrients. It needs capital and consistent management, so treat it as a Year 3 decision rather than a Year 1 one.

Composting, and why raw manure is not the same product

Fresh manure applied directly can burn crops, carries weed seeds and viable pathogens, and is unpleasant to handle. Composting fixes most of that. The practical method is a heap, turned, kept damp but not waterlogged, and left long enough to heat and then to cool and mature. A properly working heap heats noticeably in the centre, and that heat is what kills weed seeds and most pathogens.

  • Site the heap on a level pad, away from any well, borehole or watercourse, and downhill from nothing you care about.
  • Mix in bedding, spoiled hay or crop residue. Pure manure is too wet and too dense to compost well.
  • Turn it. A heap that is never turned composts only in the middle.
  • Cover it in the rainy season. Rain leaches out exactly the nutrients you are trying to keep and creates the run-off problem below.

Effluent and water

This is the part with legal consequences. Run-off from a cattle pen is high in nutrients and organic matter, and in a watercourse it strips out oxygen and kills fish. Contaminating a river or a neighbour's water supply is an offence almost everywhere, and it is also the most likely way a small operation attracts official attention.

  • Slope pens so run-off goes to a collection point, not to the boundary.
  • Have a collection pit or a vegetated buffer strip between the yard and any watercourse.
  • Never site the yard, the heap or the pit uphill of a well or borehole.
  • Never let contaminated water reach a stream, drain or road culvert.
  • Ask the local authority or water authority what applies at your scale before you build, and get it in writing.

Flies

Flies are the most common cause of complaints, and they breed in wet, undisturbed manure — around trough edges, under fences, in spilled feed. Control is mostly mechanical: remove wet manure regularly, fix leaking troughs and pipes so there is no permanently damp patch, spread or compost rather than leaving piles, and clean up spilled feed. Chemical fly control is a supplement to those things, never a substitute, and any product used around cattle must be labelled for it and must be recorded exactly like a medicine, with its own withdrawal period where one applies.

Neighbours

The practical advice is short and it works. Talk to the people around you before you expand, not after they complain. Tell them what you are doing and when the smelly jobs will happen. Give the nearest neighbours manure for their gardens. Deal with a complaint the day you get it. A single unmanaged complaint is how a by-law gets enforced against you, and by-law enforcement is far harder to undo than a wet corner of a pen is to fix.

The Complete Batch Plan: Putting Modules 1 to 11 Together

In this lesson
  • Assemble the eleven sections of a complete batch plan
  • Build a costing page where every line is dated and sourced
  • Write a contingency page with triggers and pre-decided actions

This is the final project, and it is the point of the whole course. Everything you have built module by module now goes onto one document that a buyer, a lender or a business partner could read and take seriously — and more importantly, that you can hold yourself to when the batch is running and the decisions get hard.

A plan that is only optimistic is useless. The test of this document is not whether it shows a profit. It is whether the numbers in it are your own, dated, and sourced, and whether it says honestly what happens when things go wrong.

Break-even stated honestly

Give your break-even twice: once on a live-weight basis, and once on a carcass-weight basis across the full 50 to 58 percent dressing range, exactly as Module 8 required. A single break-even figure quoted on a carcass basis with an optimistic dressing percentage assumed is the most common way a fattening plan lies to its own author.

The contingency page

One page, five scenarios, and for each one a trigger you would actually recognise and an action you would actually take.

  1. Feed price rises sharply mid-cycle. Trigger: your cost of gain, recalculated at today's prices, approaches the value of a kilogram of live weight. Action: stated in advance, not improvised.
  2. Disease outbreak or a movement restriction is imposed. Trigger: notification. Action: who you call, and what happens to the sale window.
  3. A death or a serious injury in the pen. Trigger: the event. Action: vet, record, and how the loss allowance absorbs it.
  4. Theft. Trigger: a count that does not reconcile. Action: who is called, and what your identification records let you prove.
  5. The sale price at your window is below your walk-away price. Trigger: three buyer quotes below it. Action: feed on, or sell at a loss, decided in advance using cost of gain rather than on the day using hope.

What to do after the batch

The plan is not finished when the cattle are sold. Reopen it, write the actual figure beside every estimate, and mark each line as better, worse or as expected. The gap between the plan and the outcome is the most valuable teaching material you will ever have, and it is the only one that is specific to your own cattle, your own feed and your own market. Do that three times and you will not need a course.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. An animal completes a three-day antibiotic course on 10 March. The product label states a meat withdrawal period of 28 days. What is the earliest legal slaughter date?

Withdrawal is counted from the last dose, not the first and not from recovery. The date should be written in the animal register and on a board in the pen.

2. You are trying to move a steer up a race and it keeps turning back. You are standing just in front of its shoulder. What is happening?

The point of balance sits roughly at the shoulder. In front of it means back, behind it means forward. Standing in the wrong place is behind most failed attempts to move cattle.

3. Which of these animals may be loaded for transport to an abattoir?

The others are all unfit to travel. The finished steer is clear of its withdrawal period with margin to spare and is fit, so it may be loaded.

4. Why is it a serious problem to site a manure heap uphill of a borehole?

Nutrient- and pathogen-rich leachate moves downhill with water. Contaminating a water supply is an offence almost everywhere and is one of the fastest ways a small operation attracts enforcement.

5. Your finished batch plan shows a break-even of 41 CU per kg on a carcass basis, assuming a 58 percent dressing percentage. What is the honest correction?

Quoting one break-even at the most optimistic end of the dressing range is the commonest way a fattening plan misleads its own author. Break-even is stated on a live-weight basis and across the whole 50 to 58 percent range.

Module 12 capstone

Final project. Write the complete costed plan for one fattening batch from purchase to sale, using the eleven-section structure in Lesson 6 and every worksheet you have built from Modules 1 to 11. It must contain: your batch size justified under all four limits; your maximum buying price per kilogram; the full ration with its balancing working and the step-up table; expected ADG and FCR with days on feed; the complete costing page with every line dated and attributed to a named source; break-even stated on a live-weight basis and on a carcass-weight basis across the full 50 to 58 percent dressing range; total feed tonnage with the month of peak cash need; three named buyers with their price basis and deductions, and your walk-away price; the registrations and permits that apply to you with their lead times, obtained from your own district office; your medicine record sheet with the withdrawal discipline written into it; your effluent and manure plan; and the one-page contingency sheet with five scenarios, each with a trigger and a pre-decided action. Then put a date on the front page and file it, because the second half of this project is writing the actual figures beside the estimates once the batch is sold.
