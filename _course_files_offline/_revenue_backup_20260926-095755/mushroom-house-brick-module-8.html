Module 8 — Lighting, Harvesting and Crop Scheduling

Light for pinning, hands for picking, and a calendar that keeps mushrooms on the shelf every week.

Learning outcomes

  • Set light intensity and photoperiod correctly for your species and stage.
  • Harvest at the right moment, by the right method, for maximum yield and shelf life.
  • Manage the between-flush period to set up the next flush.
  • Build a staggered crop schedule that delivers a steady weekly harvest.
  • Handle, cool and pack mushrooms so they reach the customer in good condition.
  • Keep records that show yield per bag and cost per kg.

1. Light — how much, for how long

Mushrooms do not photosynthesise, but many species use light as a signal. Light tells the mycelium it has reached the surface and it is time to fruit. Too little light gives long stems and small caps. Too much light dries the surface and can inhibit pinning.

Species / stageLight intensityPhotoperiodNotes
Oyster — spawn run0–50 luxDark or dimColonisation is faster in the dark
Oyster — pinning and fruiting200–500 lux12 h on / 12 h offLight is essential for pinning; more light = darker caps and shorter stems
Shiitake — fruiting500–1 000 lux12 h / 12 hNeeds more light for good cap colour and thick flesh
Button / Agaricus50–200 lux12 h / 12 hTolerates and prefers lower light; direct light causes browning
EnokiVery low, 20–50 lux8–10 hHigh light gives short, dark, undesirable stems
Milky / reishi300–800 lux12 h / 12 hReishi needs light for spore formation and colour

How many light fittings do you need?

Target 300 lux at the bag surface for oysters.
A 20 W LED batten typically produces about 2 000 lumens spread over 4 m² of floor at 2 m height.
1 lux = 1 lumen / m².
300 lux needed = 300 lumens / m².
For a 40 m² growing room with bags on 3 tiers:
Floor area = 40 m². Effective bag area ≈ 3 × 40 = 120 m² of surfaces receiving light (but light is measured at the surface, not summed).

Practical approach: fit LED battens along the aisles at 2.2 m height, spaced 2 m apart. Two rows of 10 fittings per 20 m of aisle. Measure with a lux meter app at the bag surface and adjust spacing until you read 250–350 lux. Do not guess.

Measure, then adjust

A phone lux meter is not laboratory-grade, but it is consistent enough to compare positions in your own room. Walk the aisles with the phone at bag height and find the dark spots. Move or add fittings until every bag is within 100 lux of your target.

Lighting hardware

  • Use IP65 or better sealed LED fittings. Standard fittings fail within months in 90 % RH.
  • Choose a neutral to cool white (4 000–6 500 K) — closer to daylight, good for pinning.
  • Do not use coloured or full-spectrum "grow" lights. Mushrooms do not need them and they waste power.
  • Fit lights on a timer, not on a manual switch. Consistency matters more than total hours.
  • Position lights so they do not shine directly into open bags or into workers' eyes.

2. Harvesting

Harvest timing decides shelf life. A mushroom picked too late has opened spores, loses water fast, and bruises in transit. Picked too early it is small and light for its substrate cost.

SpeciesHarvest signalMethodTypical yield per 5 kg bag
OysterCap fully formed but edge still slightly curled; before spores releaseTwist and pull the whole cluster; trim base with a clean knife1.2–1.8 kg over 3 flushes
ShiitakeCap 70–90 % open; edge still inrolledCut at the base with a knife; do not pull0.8–1.2 kg over 3–4 flushes
Button / AgaricusCap closed or just opening depending on marketTwist and pull; trim the baseVaries — grown on compost beds
MilkyCap 3–6 cm, flat, before heavy spore dropCut at the base0.5–0.9 kg over 2–3 flushes
ReishiCap edge white and still growing; stop before sporulationCut the whole stipe0.2–0.4 kg per block (dried)

Harvest technique

  1. Harvest before the room is misted. Dry caps store longer and bruise less.
  2. Twist and pull clusters whole — do not cut individual mushrooms from a cluster. Pull the whole cluster cleanly from the substrate.
  3. Trim the base with a clean sharp knife or scissors. Remove any substrate attached.
  4. Place in a clean crate — do not overfill; the mushrooms will bruise themselves under their own weight.
  5. Move to the cool room immediately. Every hour at room temperature costs shelf life.
  6. Do not wash. Mushrooms are like sponges. Brush off substrate with a soft brush instead.

Spore drop is a health hazard

Oyster mushrooms release millions of spores at maturity. In a poorly ventilated room this is a respiratory hazard for workers — and it coats every surface in the room. Wear an N95 mask when harvesting mature oysters, harvest before full spore release, and ventilate the room before and after harvest.

3. Between-flush management

After a flush, the substrate is depleted and the surface is exposed. What you do in the next 5–10 days decides how good the next flush is.

StepTimingAction
Clean the surfaceImmediately after harvestRemove all aborted pins and substrate fragments — they rot and invite contamination
Reduce humidityDays 1–3 after harvestRH 80–85 %, less misting — lets the surface dry and discourages mould
Lower temperature slightlyDays 1–32–3 °C below fruiting temperature; helps the mycelium recover
CO₂ dropDays 1–3Increase air exchange to drop CO₂ below 800 ppm — this is a pinning trigger
Rest, then re-triggerDays 3–5Return to fruiting conditions: cooler, high humidity, high air exchange, light
Second flush pinsDays 5–10Wait for pinning, then return to standard fruiting conditions

Yield distribution across flushes

Typical oyster on a 5 kg bag, total 1.5 kg over 3 flushes:
Flush 1: 0.75 kg (50 %)
Flush 2: 0.50 kg (33 %)
Flush 3: 0.25 kg (17 %)
Then the bag is spent and should be removed, not left to attract pests and mould.

Plan your schedule around flush 1 being the biggest. If your buyer needs consistent volume, stagger bags so a new batch enters flush 1 each week.

4. Crop scheduling

Mushroom supply is lumpy by nature. Customers want the same amount every week. The answer is staggering — starting a new batch every week, so harvests overlap and average out.

Scheduling arithmetic

How many bags to start each week?

Target: 30 kg of mushrooms per week.
Yield per 5 kg bag over all flushes: 1.5 kg.
Number of bags per week = 30 / 1.5 = 20 bags per week.

Each bag occupies space for: 2 weeks colonisation + 3 weeks fruiting = 5 weeks total.
Bags in the house at any time = 20 × 5 = 100 bags.

Using 0.12 m² per bag: 100 × 0.12 = 12 m² of growing surface needed.
On 3-tier shelving, floor area = 12 / 3 = 4 m² of shelving footprint.
With aisles and working space, that is roughly a 10–12 m² growing room.

Biological efficiency — the number that matters

BE = (fresh mushroom weight / wet substrate weight) × 100 %
30 kg mushrooms from 100 kg wet substrate = 30 % BE.

Good commercial oyster growers reach 30–50 % BE. Below 20 % means something is wrong: contaminated substrate, poor spawn rate, wrong temperature, insufficient air exchange.

5. Post-harvest handling

StepTimingMethod
CoolingWithin 1 hour of harvest2–4 °C cool room; spread in shallow crates so cold air reaches all mushrooms
GradingAfter cooling, before packingRemove damaged, oversized or undersized; group by size for retail
PackingCooled, in the cool roomPerforated punnets or paper bags — mushrooms need to breathe; sealed plastic sweats and rots them
StorageUntil delivery2–4 °C, 85–90 % RH, dark. Shelf life 5–10 days depending on species
DeliveryWithin 24 h of packingInsulated boxes with ice packs; keep out of sun

Never seal mushrooms in plastic

Mushrooms continue to respire after harvest. Sealed in a plastic bag they use up the oxygen, build up CO₂, sweat, and go slimy within a day. Perforated film, paper bags or open crates in the cool room — always breathable packaging.

6. Yield and cost per kg

Cost per kg — worked example

Per 20-bag weekly batch (5 kg each = 100 kg wet substrate):
Substrate (straw + bran): 18
Spawn (10 % rate): 22
Bag and labour for prep: 15
Electricity (heat, light, air exchange): 20
Labour for harvest, pack, deliver: 25
Packaging and delivery: 15
Total cost = 115

Yield: 30 kg.
Cost per kg = 115 / 30 = 3.83

If you sell at 8 per kg, margin = (8 − 3.83) / 8 = 52 %.
If you sell at 6 per kg, margin = 36 %.

Track this every batch. Rising cost per kg is the earliest warning of a yield problem.
