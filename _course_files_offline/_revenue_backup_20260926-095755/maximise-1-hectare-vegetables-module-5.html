5. Soil Fertility and Plant Nutrition

Plants do not eat fertiliser. They eat nutrients that have been dissolved in soil water — and those nutrients only exist if your soil is alive, correctly balanced, and correctly fed. This module shows you how to test your soil, build fertility for free, and feed each crop exactly what it needs.

Module 5 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 5.1 — Test your soil before you spend a cent on fertiliser

Most African smallholders apply the wrong fertiliser, at the wrong rate, at the wrong time. The result is a crop that looks fed but yields poorly, and money that has disappeared into the ground.

Three tests you can do before planting:

  1. Soil pH test. Cheap test kits ($5–$15) or a lab ($10–$30). pH controls whether nutrients are available at all. At pH 5.0, phosphorus is locked up even if you applied it.
  2. Texture jar test. Fill a clear jar one-third with soil, top up with water, add a pinch of salt, shake hard, and leave 24 hours. Sand settles first, silt next, clay last. The proportions tell you your soil type.
  3. Organic matter check. Dig a spade of soil. Count earthworms. Dark, crumbly soil with 5+ worms per spade is healthy. Pale, dusty, hard soil is dead and needs compost urgently.

Fixing pH:

  • Too acidic (below 6.0): add agricultural lime. Sandy soil: 1–2 tonnes/ha. Loam: 2–3 tonnes/ha. Clay: 3–5 tonnes/ha. Apply 4–8 weeks before planting — lime is slow.
  • Too alkaline (above 7.5): add gypsum (calcium sulphate) or elemental sulphur, plus generous compost. Do not add lime.
  • Never lime and plant the same week. Lime needs time to react. Planting into fresh lime burns roots.
The test rule: One $15 pH test saves $300 of wasted fertiliser. Test every plot every 2 years, or every year if you are intensively cropping.
Practical exercise 5.1: Do the jar test and pH test on all your beds. Write the results on a map of your plot. You will see that different beds need different treatment.

Lesson 5.2 — Compost and manure — the foundation of everything

Compost is not "plant food". It is the living structure that holds water, feeds soil microbes, and slowly releases nutrients. A soil with 3% organic matter holds twice the water of a soil with 1%, and yields 40–60% more.

MaterialN %P %K %Notes
Poultry litter1.5–3.01.0–2.50.8–2.0Strongest common manure. Must be composted or aged — burns roots fresh.
Rabbit manure1.0–2.00.5–1.00.5–1.2Can be used fairly fresh. Excellent for vegetables.
Goat manure0.5–0.90.3–0.60.5–0.9Good all-round. Compost with bedding.
Pig manure0.4–0.80.3–0.60.3–0.7High disease risk — compost thoroughly.
Cattle manure0.3–0.60.2–0.40.3–0.8Lowest analysis but best bulk for soil structure.
Finished compost0.5–1.50.3–1.00.5–1.5Safest option. Apply 10–20 tonnes/ha.

The 4-part compost recipe (by volume):

  1. 3 parts browns — dry grass, straw, maize stalks, dry leaves, sawdust. These are carbon.
  2. 2 parts greens — fresh grass, weeds without seed, vegetable waste, manure. These are nitrogen.
  3. 1 part soil or old compost — brings in the microbes.
  4. Water — as much as a wrung-out sponge. Not dripping, not dry.

The C:N ratio should land around 25–30:1. Too much brown = slow, cold pile. Too much green = slimy, smelly pile.

Compost management:

  • Pile size: at least 1 m × 1 m × 1 m to hold heat.
  • Turn every 7–14 days. The pile should hit 55–65°C in the first week — that kills weed seed and pathogens.
  • Ready in 6–10 weeks. Test: put a handful in a jar with water — if it smells earthy and no seed sprouts in a week, it is finished.
  • Finished compost is dark, crumbly, and smells like forest floor. Not sour, not like ammonia.
Never use fresh manure on leafy greens. Fresh manure carries E. coli and Salmonella. It also burns roots and attracts flies. Compost or age it for at least 60 days, and never apply manure to kale or spinach within 90 days of harvest.
The compost rule: Every hectare of vegetables should have a compost heap producing 10–20 tonnes per year. It is the cheapest yield increase available to you — all the inputs are on your farm already.
Practical exercise 5.2: Build one compost pile this week using the 4-part recipe. Turn it every 7 days and record the temperature with a stick or thermometer. Apply the finished compost to 200 m² and compare to an untreated plot.

Lesson 5.3 — What N, P, K and the rest actually do

Fertiliser bags show three numbers: N-P-K. That is only the start. Plants need 16 nutrients, and a shortage of any one of them limits the whole crop.

NutrientWhat it doesDeficiency symptomExcess symptomCommon source
Nitrogen (N)Leaf and stem growth, proteinPale yellow lower leaves, stunted, thin stemsDark green leaves, soft growth, no fruit, delayed maturityCAN, urea, manure, compost
Phosphorus (P)Roots, flowering, seed, energyPurple/dark leaves, weak roots, poor floweringRare. Locks up zinc and iron.DAP, TSP, bone meal
Potassium (K)Fruit quality, disease resistance, water regulationBrown scorched leaf edges, weak stems, poor fruit colourRare. Blocks magnesium and calcium.SOP, MOP, wood ash
Calcium (Ca)Cell walls, root tips, fruit firmnessBlossom-end rot on tomato and pepper, tip burn on cabbageRare. Blocks magnesium and potassium.Agricultural lime, gypsum

Sulphur (S)Protein, flavour, onion pungencyPale young leaves, poor onion flavourAcidity if over-appliedGypsum, SOP
Zinc (Zn)Growth hormones, enzyme systemsSmall narrow leaves, shortened internodes ("little leaf")Toxic — stunts growthZinc sulphate foliar spray
Boron (B)Flowering, fruit set, cell divisionHollow stem in cabbage, cracked fruit, poor seed setToxic at very low overdose — very narrow marginBorax (tiny amounts), foliar spray

The three big rules of feeding:

  1. Nitrogen drives leaves, phosphorus drives roots and flowers, potassium drives fruit. Feed the wrong one at the wrong stage and you get leaves instead of tomatoes.
  2. Too much nitrogen is worse than too little. Over-fed plants grow huge and leafy, attract aphids, set almost no fruit, and mature late.
  3. The nutrient in shortest supply limits everything else. If boron is missing, adding more nitrogen will not help. That is why soil testing matters.
Practical exercise 5.3: Walk your plot and find one plant with a deficiency symptom. Identify which nutrient it is using the table above. Then check your soil pH and organic matter before you buy anything.

Lesson 5.4 — Fertiliser programme by crop

Apply fertiliser in splits, not all at once. One big dose at planting leaches away in the first heavy rain. Split applications feed the plant at each growth stage.

CropBasal (at planting)Top dress 1Top dress 2Notes
Tomato200 kg/ha DAP or TSP + 200 kg/ha lime (4 weeks before)150 kg/ha CAN at 3 weeks after transplant150 kg/ha CAN + 100 kg/ha SOP at first fruit setStop nitrogen once fruit begins to colour. Potassium is critical during fruit fill.
Pepper200 kg/ha DAP + 200 kg/ha lime (4 weeks before)150 kg/ha CAN at 3 weeks100 kg/ha SOP at fruit setCalcium is critical — blossom-end rot if missing.
Onion200 kg/ha DAP150 kg/ha CAN at 3 weeks150 kg/ha CAN at 6 weeksStop all nitrogen 4 weeks before harvest. Late nitrogen = thick necks and rotten bulbs.
Cabbage / kale150 kg/ha DAP150 kg/ha CAN at 3 weeks100 kg/ha CAN at head formation (cabbage only)Kale is a continuous crop — feed 50 kg/ha CAN after every third harvest.
Carrot150 kg/ha DAP, low nitrogenNone normallyNone normallyNever apply fresh manure. Excess nitrogen causes forking and hairy roots.
HerbsCompost only, 15–20 t/haLight compost tea monthlyNoneHigh nitrogen reduces oil content and flavour. Keep herbs lean.

Applying correctly:

  • Basal fertiliser goes in the planting furrow and is mixed with soil — never touching the seed or root ball directly.
  • Top dress goes 10–15 cm from the plant stem, in a band, then lightly watered in. Never on the stem.
  • Fertiliser needs moisture. Apply before rain or before irrigation, never onto dry soil in full sun.
  • Store fertiliser sealed, off the ground, in the dark. Damp fertiliser cakes and becomes unusable.
The split rule: Three small applications always beat one big one. Same total fertiliser, 20–40% more yield, less leaching, less waste.
Practical exercise 5.4: Write your fertiliser plan for the next crop on paper before you plant. Include dates, product, rate, and method. Then follow it exactly and compare yield to your last crop.

Lesson 5.5 — Foliar feeding and micronutrients

Foliar feeding sprays a dilute nutrient solution onto leaves, which absorb it directly. It is fast — results in 3–5 days — but it is a correction tool, not a replacement for soil fertility.

SituationFoliar productRate per 15 L knapsackTiming
General boost during heavy fruitingBalanced foliar NPK + micronutrients30–50 gEvery 10–14 days while fruiting
Calcium deficiency / blossom-end rotCalcium nitrate or calcium chloride30 gWeekly from flowering
Magnesium deficiency (yellow between veins)Epsom salt (magnesium sulphate)30 gEvery 10 days, 2–3 applications
Zinc deficiency (little leaf)Zinc sulphate15 gTwice, 10 days apart
Boron deficiency (hollow stem, poor fruit set)Borax or solubor5–10 g ONLY — very narrow marginAt flowering, once or twice

Foliar feeding rules:

  1. Spray early morning or late afternoon. Midday heat evaporates the droplet before the leaf absorbs it, and can burn the leaf.
  2. Spray the underside of leaves. That is where the stomata are — absorption is 3–5× higher than the upper surface.
  3. Add a few drops of liquid soap as a sticker-spreader if the product does not include one.
  4. Never exceed the label rate. Foliar nutrients are salts. Too strong and you scorch the leaves worse than the deficiency.
  5. Do not foliar feed in full sun above 30°C or within 3 days of a pesticide spray.
Boron warning: The gap between "enough" and "toxic" for boron is tiny. 5 g per 15 L is a correction dose. 30 g per 15 L will kill your crop. Measure it, do not guess it.
Practical exercise 5.5: Buy one balanced foliar micronutrient product. Spray half your tomato rows at first fruit set, leave the other half untreated. Compare fruit size and blossom-end rot counts at harvest.

Lesson 5.6 — Crop rotation and cover crops

Growing the same crop in the same bed year after year builds up soil-borne disease, exhausts specific nutrients, and multiplies pests. Rotation is free and it works.

FamilyCropsWhat it takes from soilWhat it gives back
SolanaceaeTomato, pepper, eggplant, potatoHeavy potassium, calciumNothing — heavy feeder, disease-prone
AlliumOnion, garlic, leekModerate nitrogen, sulphurPest-suppressing sulphur compounds
BrassicaceaeCabbage, kale, rape, broccoli, radish, turnipHeavy nitrogenDeep roots break hardpan; biofumigation
ApiaceaeCarrot, coriander, celeryLight nitrogen, potassiumDeep taproots improve structure
AmaranthaceaeBeetroot, spinach, amaranthModerate nitrogenQuick ground cover
Fabaceae (legumes)Beans, peas, cowpea, groundnut, lablabAlmost nothingFixes 40–150 kg/ha of nitrogen from the air

The 4-block rotation for 1 hectare (2-year cycle):

  1. Block A — Heavy feeders: tomato, pepper, cabbage.
  2. Block B — Light feeders: carrot, beetroot, onion.
  3. Block C — Legumes / cover crop: beans, cowpea, lablab — then incorporated as green manure.
  4. Block D — Rest and rebuild: compost, lime if needed, herbs or a light leafy crop.

Next season, shift every crop one block clockwise. Over 4 years, every block does every job.

Cover crops worth planting:

Cover cropNitrogen fixed (kg/ha)Days to incorporateBest for
Cowpea60–10060–75Fast, drought-tolerant, edible seed too
Lablab80–15090–120Deep taproot, huge biomass, drought-hardy
Mucuna (velvet bean)100–15090–120Smothering weeds, huge biomass
Sunn hemp100–15060–90Fast growth, nematode suppression
Groundnut40–8090–120Cash crop + nitrogen

Green manure incorporation:

  • Cut the cover crop at flowering — before seed set — when nitrogen content is highest.
  • Chop it up. Smaller pieces break down faster.
  • Incorporate into the top 10–15 cm, or leave as surface mulch.
  • Wait 3–4 weeks before planting the next crop so decomposition does not compete with seedlings for nitrogen.
The rotation rule: Never grow the same plant family in the same bed two seasons in a row. Every legume block you grow puts 40–150 kg of free nitrogen into your soil — that is $60–$200 of fertiliser you did not buy.
Practical exercise 5.6: Draw your hectare on paper and divide it into 4 blocks. Write which family goes in which block this season and next. Plant one block of cowpea or lablab as a green manure trial this season.
