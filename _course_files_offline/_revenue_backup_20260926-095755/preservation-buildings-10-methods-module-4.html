Module 4

Method 3 — Salting and Curing Rooms

Salt is the oldest chemical preservative. It works by pulling water out of food and creating an environment where bacteria cannot grow. This module is how to build a curing room and use salt correctly — safely, at scale, for meat, fish and some vegetables.

  • Explain how salt preserves food and the difference between salting, curing and brining
  • Design a curing room with correct temperature, humidity and airflow

Lesson 4.1

What salt does to food

Salt preserves by osmosis. When food is packed in dry salt, or immersed in strong brine, water moves out of the food into the salt. Bacteria need water to grow. Remove enough water and they cannot multiply.

The three salt methods

MethodWhat it doesUsed for
Dry saltingFood packed in dry salt; salt draws water out; water drains awayFish, some meats, some vegetables (cabbage for sauerkraut)
Wet salting (brining)Food immersed in a salt solution; salt penetrates through the liquidMeats, poultry, some fish
CuringSalt + nitrite + often sugar and spices; controlled time and temperature; often smokedHam, bacon, salami, cured fish

How much water must be removed

Bacteria stop growing when the water activity (aw) of food drops below 0.91. Fresh meat is 0.99. Salt-packed fish reaches 0.75. Heavily salted meat reaches 0.80–0.85.

Water activity is measured with a meter, but you can judge by the process: if a product is properly salted and dried, it will not spoil at room temperature. If it spoils, it was not salted enough or was not dried enough.

The salt rule: salt is measured as a percentage of the product's weight. Fish: 15–25%. Meat (dry-cured): 4–6% salt, plus nitrite. Too little and the product spoils; too much and it is inedible.

Lesson 4.2

Designing a curing room

A curing room holds the product at a controlled temperature and humidity during the days or weeks it takes to cure. It is not a cold room and it is not a drying shed — it is a hybrid with its own requirements.

Environmental targets

ProductTemperatureHumidityTime
Dry-cured ham2–4 °C for salting; 12–15 °C for ageing75–85%4–24 months
Bacon (wet-cured)2–4 °CSealed in brine5–10 days
Salted fish4–8 °C60–70%3–15 days
Cured sausage10–15 °C75–85%2–8 weeks

The room's essential features

  1. Temperature control. Most products need 2–15 °C. A small cool room with a refrigeration unit (see Cold Room course) sized for a higher temperature than a chill room.
  2. Humidity control. A humidifier or dehumidifier, depending on the product and local conditions. 75–85% relative humidity.
  3. Airflow. Slow, constant movement. A small fan circulating air is enough. Too much airflow dries the surface; too little lets mould grow.
  4. Cleanable surfaces. Walls tiled or rendered, floor sloped to drain, stainless steel or food-grade plastic surfaces on all work tables.
  5. Hanging system. Rails or hooks for the curing product. Load-rated for the weight.
  6. Insect-proof and rodent-proof. Mesh screens on all openings.
  7. Separate from other processing. Curing generates strong smells and airborne bacteria. Keep it separate from fresh meat, cooked food, and other preservation methods.

Room sizing

Curing rooms are sized by the weight of product held at one time. As a rule:

  • 1 m² of curing floor holds 30–50 kg of hanging meat at rail spacing
  • Or 5–8 kg per linear metre of rail

For 200 kg of curing meat, allow 5–8 m² of floor and 25–40 m of rail. Plus working space, this means a room of roughly 3 × 4 m.

Lesson 4.3

Salt types and rates

Salt types

  • Coarse rock salt: standard for dry-curing. Slow dissolution, easy to pack and drain.
  • Fine table salt: faster dissolution but can have anti-caking agents that affect colour. Not ideal for curing.
  • Sea salt: excellent flavour, contains trace minerals. More expensive.
  • Kosher salt: good for curing, no additives. Rare in Africa but increasingly available.
  • Salt with nitrite (curing salt / Prague powder / Instacure): salt pre-mixed with 6.25% sodium nitrite. Used for meat curing specifically to prevent botulism.

Nitrite — the safety rule

Nitrite is toxic above the legal limit. It prevents botulism, but too much causes methaemoglobinaemia (blue baby syndrome) and can be fatal. Never add nitrite directly. Use only pre-mixed curing salt, and follow the rate on the product exactly.

Lesson 4.4

The curing process, step by step

Dry-cured ham

  1. Select the cut. Fresh, well-chilled ham at 2–4 °C. Trim excess fat to 2–3 cm.
  2. Weigh and calculate. Salt at 4–6% of weight; curing salt at the manufacturer's rate (typically 2.5 g per kg meat).
  3. Rub. Mix the salt and curing salt; rub into all surfaces. Some goes into the bone area.
  4. Pack. Place in a clean food-grade bin at 2–4 °C. Cover with a cloth.
  5. Turn daily. Drain liquid; re-rub with salt on day 7.
  6. Salt time: approximately 2–3 days per kg of weight. A 5 kg ham takes 10–15 days.
  7. Rinse and dry. Rinse off surface salt. Hang in the curing room at 12–15 °C, 75% humidity.
  8. Age: 3–12 months, depending on style. During ageing, humidity drops to 65–70%.

Salted fish

  1. Gut, gill and clean. Split large fish, remove backbone for thick fish.
  2. Dry-salt: 20–25% salt by weight. Layer fish and salt in a bin.
  3. Weight down. Press the fish to expel moisture.
  4. Time: small fish 3–5 days, large fish 10–20 days.
  5. Dry: after salting, dry in a solar dryer or smoke to reduce moisture further.
  6. Pack: vacuum seal, or pack in oil, or keep in brine for short-term storage.

Recording

Keep a curing log: product type, weight, salt %, curing salt rate, dates, temperature and humidity for each batch, and outcome. Next batch improves because this batch is documented.

Lesson 4.5

Safety, storage and sales

Food safety

  • Start with fresh product. Curing does not rescue spoiled meat.
  • Keep cold through the process. 2–4 °C during salting and 12–15 °C during ageing.
  • Use food-grade bins, gloves, and clean surfaces. Curing uses hands and contact; contamination is easy.
  • Never mix raw and cured product in the same space.
  • Do not reduce salt or nitrite "for taste". The rates are safety rates, not flavour suggestions.

Storage after curing

  • Whole cured hams — hang at 12–15 °C, 65–75% humidity, 6–12 months. Cut surfaces protected with fat or cloth.
  • Sliced cured product — vacuum pack, refrigerate. 2–4 weeks.
  • Salted fish — vacuum pack, refrigerate, or keep in oil. 4–12 weeks.

Selling cured product

  • Label properly — product type, weight, ingredients, use-by date, your business name and address.
  • Declare nitrite on the label if used. Many markets require this.
  • Premium prices — cured ham, biltong, smoked fish sell at 3–8× fresh meat prices.
  • Health inspection — cured meat for sale is a food product and falls under food safety regulation. Confirm requirements with your district health office.
The curing rule: salt is measured, time is measured, temperature is measured. Curing is a science, not an art. Follow the numbers, keep the records, and the product is safe.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. How does salt preserve food?

Correct: osmosis. Bacteria stop growing below 0.91 water activity. Salted fish reaches 0.75.

2. What is the salt rate for dry-cured ham?

Correct: 4–6%. Plus curing salt at the manufacturer's rate. Salted fish needs 15–25%.

3. Why is nitrite added to cured meat?

Correct: prevent botulism. Also for colour and flavour. Never add nitrite directly — use pre-mixed curing salt.

4. What temperature should a curing room hold during the salting stage?

Correct: 2–4 °C. During salting and wet curing. Ageing is at 12–15 °C.

5. What is the penalty for reducing nitrite below the recommended rate?

Correct: botulism risk. The nitrite rate is a safety rate, not a flavour rate. Never reduce it.

Practical assignment — Module 4

Design a curing room and process for a specific product.

  1. Which product are you curing? (Ham, bacon, fish, sausage, or other.)
  2. Your weekly throughput in kg and the curing time for the product.
  3. The room's environmental targets (temperature, humidity) at each stage.
  4. Room design — dimensions, hanging system, cooling unit, humidifier, airflow. Include a dimensioned sketch.
  5. Your salt and curing salt rates, with the arithmetic for a batch of your size.
  6. The full process steps from raw to finished, with times and temperatures.
  7. Storage and packaging after curing.
  8. Two supplier quotations for salt, curing salt and the cooling unit.
  9. Your labelling plan — what will be on the label, and how it complies with local regulations.
  10. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest food-safety risk in your operation and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 5 and 10. The salt and nitrite rates are non-negotiable; the paragraph is where you commit to a safety decision.
