Module 2

Vines, Multiplication Beds & Planting

Sweet potato is not grown from seed — it is grown from vines. Getting vines right is 80% of the crop. This module teaches vine selection, multiplication and planting discipline.

Lesson 1

Why Vines, Not Seed

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Understand why sweet potato is vegetatively propagated
  • Know where to source vines
  • Understand the cost and quality difference between vine sources

Sweet potato is propagated from vine cuttings, not from seed. Each cutting is 20–40 cm long with 5–8 nodes. Roots form at the buried nodes; the top grows into the vine.

Vine sources, ranked by quality and cost:

  1. Certified clean-vine producers — national research programs, NGOs and specialised vine multipliers. Vines are tested free of the major viruses. $0.02–0.10 per cutting. The gold standard.
  2. Your own multiplication bed — if you start with clean vines once, you can multiply them for several seasons. Quality declines slowly; replace with certified vines every 2–3 years.
  3. Neighbour's field — convenient and free or cheap, but you cannot know the virus status. If the neighbour has good-looking vines and good yield, the risk is moderate.
  4. Local market — cheapest but highest virus risk. Only use if you cannot source better and you inspect the source field first.

The economics: certified clean vines cost $20–100 per 1,000 cuttings but yield 2–3x the harvest of infected vines. The extra cost is repaid 10x over in the first harvest. Never compromise on vine quality.

Lesson 2

Choosing Clean Vines — the Virus Problem

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Recognise the symptoms of virus-infected vines
  • Understand why vine quality declines over generations
  • Know the discipline of replacing vines every 2–3 years

Symptoms of virus infection

  • Yellowing or mottled leaves, especially the young ones
  • Distorted or curled leaf shape
  • Stunted growth compared to healthy plants
  • Small or misshapen roots at harvest
  • Reduced or absent flowering

The problem is that infected vines look almost normal in the field. A plant can carry a virus, produce a crop, and still transmit the virus to every cutting you take from it. Over 2–3 generations of saving your own vines, yield drops sharply.

The 2–3 year rule

If you start with certified clean vines, you can multiply them for 2–3 seasons before virus pressure and mutation build up to a yield-limiting level. At that point, replace your vine stock with fresh certified vines. The replacement cost is small compared to the yield gain.

Selecting vines from your own field

If you must select from your own or a neighbour's field, choose the most vigorous vines from the healthiest-looking plants. Avoid vines from plants with any visible symptom. This does not guarantee virus-free, but it slows the decline.

Lesson 3

Multiplication Beds — Producing Your Own Vines

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Set up a multiplication bed to produce vines for your crop
  • Manage the bed for maximum vine production
  • Calculate how many vines you need

A multiplication bed is a small area where you grow sweet potato vines specifically to generate cuttings for the main field. It gives you control over vine quality and quantity.

Setting up a multiplication bed

  1. Choose a bed 1 m wide, any length, on fertile, well-drained soil
  2. Mix in well-rotted manure at 5–10 kg/m²
  3. Plant certified clean cuttings at 30 cm × 30 cm spacing — closer than a main field
  4. Irrigate regularly; the bed should never dry out
  5. Fertilise with nitrogen (urea or CAN) every 4 weeks to push vine growth
  6. Do not harvest roots from the multiplication bed — the objective is vines

Vine harvest and yield

After 8–10 weeks, vines are ready for cutting. Cut 20–40 cm sections from the growing tips, leaving the base of the vine to regrow. A well-managed multiplication bed produces 200–400 cuttings per 10 m² every 8 weeks. Re-cut every 6–8 weeks through the growing season.

How many vines do you need?

Main field planting: 30,000–40,000 cuttings per hectare at 1 m × 30 cm spacing. So a 1,000 m² plot needs 3,000–4,000 cuttings. A 1-hectare crop needs a multiplication bed of 100–200 m² maintained continuously.

Lesson 4

Land Preparation and Ridging

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Prepare soil that allows roots to swell properly
  • Build ridges or mounds at the correct dimensions
  • Incorporate organic matter for good root shape

Sweet potato produces roots that swell to 100–500 g each. That swelling needs loose, deep, well-drained soil. On compacted or shallow soil, roots are small and misshapen, and their market value collapses.

Ridge dimensions

Ridges should be 30–40 cm high and 60–100 cm wide at the base, spaced 1 m apart. This gives enough soil depth for roots to swell and provides drainage in heavy rain. Build ridges 2–3 weeks before planting to let the soil settle.

Organic matter

Mix in 5–10 t/ha of well-rotted manure or compost before ridging. This improves soil structure, water holding, and root shape. It is the single most important pre-planting input.

Soil pH and drainage

Sweet potato prefers pH 5.5–6.5 and hates waterlogging. If your soil is waterlogged, use mounds or raised beds. If it is very acidic (pH below 5.0), apply lime at 1–2 t/ha a season ahead.

Lesson 5

Planting — Spacing, Depth, Timing

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Plant cuttings at the correct spacing and angle
  • Time planting for the right season
  • Water and care for the crop in the first 2 weeks

Cutting preparation

Use cuttings 20–40 cm long with 5–8 nodes. Strip the leaves from the lower 2/3 of the cutting (leaving the top 3–4 leaves). This reduces water loss during establishment.

Planting angle

Plant cuttings at an angle of about 30–45°, with 2/3 of the cutting buried and the top 1/3 above ground. This is the standard practice — it puts more nodes underground where roots form and leaves the growing tip exposed.

Spacing

Standard: 1 m between ridges × 30 cm between plants within the ridge. This gives about 33,000 plants per hectare. Some systems use closer spacing (75 cm × 25 cm) for higher plant populations and higher yields, at the cost of more vines per hectare.

Timing

Plant at the start of the rains, or under irrigation at any time. In rain-fed systems, plant when the soil is moist to 20 cm. Late planting reduces yield by 30–50%.

First 2 weeks

Water the crop twice in the first week if there is no rain. Mulch around the plants to reduce evaporation. The cuttings will wilt in the first 2 days — this is normal. If they recover by day 4, they have rooted. If they are still wilted at day 7, they are dead.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. Sweet potato is propagated from what?

Correct: vine cuttings. 20–40 cm sections with 5–8 nodes.

2. What is the biggest vine-quality risk?

Correct: virus infection. Cause 50–80% yield loss. Buy certified clean vines only.

3. How often should vine stock be replaced with certified vines?

Correct: every 2–3 years. Virus pressure builds up over generations of saving your own vines.

4. What ridge height is recommended for sweet potato?

Correct: 30–40 cm. Enough soil depth for roots to swell and enough drainage for heavy rain.

5. What is the correct planting angle for sweet potato cuttings?

Correct: 30–45° with 2/3 buried. More nodes underground = more roots.

Module 2 capstone

Plan your vine multiplication and planting. Include: (1) where you will source your first clean vines; (2) the size of your multiplication bed; (3) how many cuttings you will produce per cycle; (4) your main field area and cuttings needed; (5) your planting date and spacing.
