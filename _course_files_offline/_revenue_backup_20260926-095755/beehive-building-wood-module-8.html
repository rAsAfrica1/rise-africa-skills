Keeping a wooden hive at brood temperature when the sun, the rain and the colony all disagree.

1. The heat balance of a wooden hive

A colony holds its brood nest at 34–35 °C within about ±0.5 °C, day and night, whether it is 12 °C or 40 °C outside. That is a large, continuous energy bill paid in honey and bee lives.

Wood is a poor conductor — that is its virtue. A 20 mm softwood wall has roughly R ≈ 0.13 m²K/W, which is decent but not exceptional. The real problem is not the walls; it is the roof. Solar gain on a dark, uninsulated roof can push the top box 10–15 °C above ambient, and hot air rises into exactly the space the colony needs to keep cool.

The four jobs ventilation must do

  1. Remove metabolic heat in hot weather.
  2. Remove water vapour — a strong colony produces 1–3 L of water per day from nectar ripening and respiration.
  3. Supply oxygen and remove CO₂ (rarely the limiting factor, but relevant in a closed, over-insulated hive).
  4. Prevent condensation in cool weather — cold, wet bees die faster than cold, dry bees.

Sizing the vent pair — the stack-effect arithmetic

You do not need precision here, but you do need to know the openings are not arbitrary. The driving pressure from a chimney effect is:

ΔP = 0.043 × h × ΔT  (Pa, with h in metres and ΔT in kelvin)

Take hive height h = 0.6 m and ΔT = 12 K (hive interior 35 °C, outside 23 °C):
ΔP = 0.043 × 0.6 × 12 = 0.31 Pa

That is a very small pressure. Velocity through the opening is approximately:

v = √(2ΔP / ρ)  with ρ ≈ 1.15 kg/m³
v = √(0.62 / 1.15) = 0.73 m/s

For an inlet of 0.3 m × 0.02 m = 0.006 m²:
Q = A × v × 3600 = 0.006 × 0.73 × 3600 ≈ 15.8 m³/h

Compare with the colony’s need. A strong colony moves roughly 20–40 m³/h of air on a hot day through fanning. So a single 20 mm entrance slot is not sufficient on its own at peak — this is exactly why the screened bottom board and top vent matter. Open all three and you roughly triple the effective area.

3. Shade — the cheapest cooling you can buy

Shade beats ventilation on cost per degree. A hive in full sun can run 8–12 °C hotter inside than an identical hive in shade.

Never shade with a wet blanket

Wrapping a hive in wet cloth raises humidity inside the box. Bees cool by evaporating water, and they cannot evaporate into already-saturated air. Dry shade only.

4. Roof and insulation

  • Roof cavity: build a 25–40 mm air gap between the crown board and the roof skin. This alone reduces heat transfer into the top box more than doubling wall thickness.
  • Insulation insert: a 25 mm rigid foam or a 50 mm dry straw pad in the roof cavity. Must stay dry — wet insulation is worse than none.
  • Overhang: 50–75 mm on all sides to shed rain clear of the walls and entrance.
  • Vapour path: in cool climates the roof insulation should be ventilated above the crown board so moisture that rises can escape rather than condense on the underside of the roof.
  • Metal roofing: durable and reflective if light-coloured, but conducts heat — always pair with the air gap.

5. Cold season: condensation, not cold

Bees survive cold. They do not survive wet. In winter or cool highland nights, warm moist air rises, meets a cold crown board and condenses — then drips back onto the cluster.

  • Keep a small top vent open in cold weather; it costs little heat and removes a lot of water.
  • Reduce the entrance to 8–10 mm to cut draughts and prevent robbing.
  • Close the screened bottom board slider (leave the screen for varroa monitoring only during treatment periods).
  • Do not over-insulate the walls while sealing the top — that traps moisture.
  • In very cold regions, add a windbreak, not a sealed wrap.

6. Water supply

A colony can use over a litre of water a day in hot weather. Provide a shallow, shaded water source within 100 m with landing stones so bees do not drown. A reliable water source also keeps bees out of neighbours' swimming pools and animal troughs.

Bearding is a message

A large cluster of bees hanging outside the entrance on a hot evening means the hive cannot dump heat fast enough. Before you assume it is swarming, check ventilation and shade first. Bearding at the entrance with the brood nest intact = heat. Bearding with no brood and no queen = swarm.
