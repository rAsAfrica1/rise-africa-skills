Module 7 — Shelving, Beds and Grow Bag Support

The structure that holds your crop — and quietly decides your airflow, hygiene and yield.

Home › Mushroom House — Brick › Module 7

Learning outcomes

  • Calculate the dead and live loads your shelving must carry.
  • Choose materials that survive a humid, spore-laden room and can be cleaned.
  • Set shelf spacing, depth and aisle width from crop and ergonomics.
  • Design grow bag support that keeps bags stable and fully exposed to airflow.
  • Lay out shelving so the room air exchange from Module 6 actually reaches every bag.

1. What shelving has to do

Shelving is not furniture. In a mushroom house it must:

  1. Carry a heavy, wet, uneven load without deflecting.
  2. Survive 90 percent plus relative humidity, repeated fogging, and cleaning chemicals.
  3. Let air move around each bag, not just past the front row.
  4. Be cleanable — no raw timber, no crevices, no rust traps.
  5. Let you reach the back of every shelf without climbing.

Untreated timber is the classic mistake

Timber shelving looks cheap and works for one or two crops. Then it absorbs moisture, grows green mould on the underside, and becomes a permanent inoculum source that contaminates every subsequent flush. If you must use timber, use pressure-treated or sealed structural timber and accept a shorter service life. Galvanised steel or food-grade plastic is better.

2. Load calculation

Get this wrong and shelves collapse mid-crop. Work from the wet substrate weight, not the dry weight.

Worked load calculation

Given: grow bag of 20 kg wet substrate, footprint 0.5 m × 0.3 m = 0.15 m².
Load per bag = 20 kg × 9.81 = 196 N
Load per m² = 196 / 0.15 = 1 307 N/m² = 133 kg/m²

Add the shelf own weight (steel mesh shelf ≈ 8 kg/m², that is 78 N/m²).
Dead + live ≈ 1 385 N/m²

Apply a safety factor of 2.0 for a humid, dynamic, long-duration load:
Design load = 1 385 × 2.0 = 2 770 N/m² = 282 kg/m²

Per shelf bay: a 2.4 m × 0.6 m shelf = 1.44 m²
Total design load = 2 770 × 1.44 = 3 989 N = 407 kg per shelf

Now choose members. For a simply supported beam with a uniformly distributed load, the bending moment is:

M = wL² / 8 where w = load per metre, L = span

Load per metre on a 1.2 m span shelf carrying 282 kg/m² over 0.6 m depth:
w = 282 × 0.6 × 9.81 = 1 660 N/m
M = 1 660 × 1.2² / 8 = 299 N·m

For mild steel with allowable stress 165 N/mm², required section modulus:
Z = M / sigma = 299 000 / 165 = 1 812 mm³

A 40 × 40 × 3 mm square hollow section has Z ≈ 3 500 mm³ — comfortably adequate. A 25 × 25 × 2 mm angle at Z ≈ 900 mm³ is not. This is why thin angle-iron shelving sags.

Depth beats height

Many growers add tiers to increase capacity and end up with unharvestable top shelves and blocked airflow. A 3-tier shelf 600 mm deep with a full 1 200 mm aisle will out-yield a 5-tier shelf at 900 mm depth, because you can actually manage it.

5. Grow bag support

Bags sitting flat on a solid shelf sweat on the underside, block airflow, and develop anaerobic patches that invite contamination. Support them so air reaches the base.

MethodDescriptionAirflow under bagCost
Wire mesh deck50 × 50 mm galvanised weld mesh on steel frameExcellentLow to medium

Plastic grid trayMoulded HDPE nursery trayGoodMedium

Spacing bags on the deck: leave 50–75 mm between adjacent bags in a row, and 75–100 mm between rows. This gap is not wasted space — it is the air channel that carries CO₂ away and brings fresh air in. Packing bags shoulder-to-shoulder can cut yield per bag by 20–30 percent.

6. Bed systems for compost

If you grow on compost beds rather than bags (common for button and some oyster systems), the bed becomes the support structure.

  • Bed depth: 150–200 mm of compost. Deeper beds heat up and go anaerobic in the centre.
  • Bed width: 900 mm if accessible from one side, 1 800 mm if accessible from both.
  • Bed edges: 100 mm upstand to contain compost; sealed timber, concrete or HDPE.
  • Bed base: slatted or perforated, not solid, so leachate drains and air can move.
  • Drainage: slope 1:100 to a gully; never let leachate pool under a bed.
  • Sterilisation: beds must withstand steam or chemical sterilisation between crops — hence sealed concrete or stainless, not raw timber.

7. Layout and airflow

Connecting shelving to your air exchange design

Your Module 6 calculation gave you an ACH figure. That air is useless if the shelves block it. Three rules:

  1. Align shelves with the airflow direction. If air enters low on one wall and exits high on the opposite wall, run shelf rows parallel to that path — never perpendicular, which turns shelves into baffles.
  2. Keep 75–100 mm clear at the wall. Air must be able to move behind the last bag and up the wall.
  3. Use mesh or slatted decks. Air must be able to move vertically through the shelf stack, not only along the aisle.

Checking the aisle is not the bottleneck

Room volume 40 m³, target 4 ACH = 160 m³/h total.
Two aisles of 1.2 m × 2.5 m high = 6 m² free cross-section.
Required velocity = 160 / 6 / 3600 = 0.0074 m/s — trivially achievable.

Now try a badly designed room: one aisle 0.6 m × 2.5 m = 1.5 m².
Velocity = 160 / 1.5 / 3600 = 0.030 m/s — still achievable, but the air will short-circuit down the aisle and never enter the shelf stack. Aisle width is less about velocity and more about distribution.

8. Hygiene in the structure

  • Every surface must be cleanable with a brush and a sprayer. No open box sections — cap them.
  • Use bolted connections, not welded, where possible: bolts can be undone and the shelf removed for full-room washdown.
  • Round or chamfer edges; square edges collect spore dust.
  • Shelf decks should lift out individually so you can clean the tier above.
  • Replace any rusted or split component immediately — rust bleeds and harbours contaminants.
  • After each crop: remove all bags, brush down, wash, and sterilise the whole structure before the next fill.

9. Lighting and harvest access

  • Fit lights along the aisle, not above the top shelf. Aim for 200–500 lux at the picking surface.
  • Use IP65 or better sealed LED fittings — standard fittings fail in 90 percent RH.
  • Position lights so they do not shine directly into open bags (some species pin toward light).
  • Every bag must be reachable with both hands without a ladder. If you need a ladder to harvest, redesign the shelf.
