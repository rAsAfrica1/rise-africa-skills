Module 12 — Pests, Diseases and Colony Health Records

Recognising the problems early, treating what is treatable, and recording what you did.

Home › Beehive Building — Wood › Module 12

Learning outcomes

  • Recognise the main pests and diseases of honey bees by sight and by symptom.
  • Distinguish between what you can treat, what you must report, and what you must destroy.
  • Apply integrated pest management rather than reaching for chemicals.
  • Keep colony health records that show trends and support decisions.
  • Know when to call a bee inspector.

1. The main problems

Learn these eight. Between them they account for the vast majority of colony losses in small apiaries.

AFB is a legal and ethical emergency

American foulbrood spores survive for decades in wood, wax and honey. If you suspect AFB, do not sell, gift or move any hive material. Contact your bee inspector or national apiculture authority immediately. In most countries the hive must be burned and the site treated before you can keep bees there again. Doing anything else risks spreading it across your region.

2. Varroa — the default problem

Varroa is present in most beekeeping regions. It is not a question of whether you have it, but how many. Untreated, a colony can be killed within 18–24 months.

Monitoring

Worked varroa count

Sugar roll on 300 bees yielded 7 mites.
Rate = 7 / 300 × 100 = 2.33 mites per 100 bees — below threshold.
Two weeks later: 11 mites / 300 bees = 3.67 per 100 — above threshold. Treat now.

The trend matters more than a single reading. Two consecutive rising counts mean the mite population is doubling roughly every 3–4 weeks in the build-up season.

Integrated pest management

  1. Monitor. A count every 4 weeks through the active season. Records, not guesses.
  2. Use a screened base — some mites fall through and cannot climb back up.
  3. Drone brood removal — mites prefer drone cells. Cut out sealed drone comb and freeze it before the mites emerge.
  4. Break the brood cycle — caging the queen for 21 days cuts off new brood and starves varroa of reproduction sites. Practical with a queen cage or split.
  5. Choose a treatment appropriate to your climate and market. Organic acids (oxalic, formic), essential oils (thymol), or synthetic acaricides in rotation.
  6. Rotate treatments — mites develop resistance. Never use the same active ingredient two seasons in a row.
  7. Treat within the label. Respect dose, temperature range and withdrawal period. Honey for sale must be free of residues.

Treat early, not when you see damage

By the time you see deformed wings or spotty brood, the colony is already losing winter bees. Treat at the threshold from your counts, not from what you can see with your eyes.

3. Recognising brood disease

Field test — the ropiness check for AFB

  1. Insert a matchstick or twig into a suspect capped or sunken cell.
  2. Stir gently and withdraw.
  3. If the contents come out as a brown, stringy, glue-like rope that stretches 20 mm or more, suspect AFB.
  4. Smell the cell. AFB has a distinct, foul, rotting smell.
  5. Do not confuse with EFB, where the larva is twisted, yellow-brown and mostly uncapped, and the smell is sour rather than foul.
  6. If AFB is suspected: close the hive, mark it, and contact your inspector. Do not open other hives until you have washed and changed.

Suspect but not sure?

Treat every uncertain case as if it were AFB until an inspector confirms otherwise. The cost of being wrong the other way — spreading AFB to your neighbours — is far worse than the cost of a false alarm.

Master colony record

One page per hive, updated at every inspection. Include:

  • Hive number, site, build date.
  • Queen origin, introduction date, age, marked colour.
  • All treatments applied, with dates, product, dose and withdrawal period.
  • All inspections with the summary from the log line.
  • Yields per harvest.
  • Any disease or pest events, with dates and outcomes.
  • Any movements of the hive, boxes or frames.

The habit that saves colonies

Write the varroa count and the brood condition down every time you open the hive, even when nothing is wrong. Trends show up in the record long before they show up in the bees. A colony that loses a frame of brood at every inspection is failing, and you will only see it in the numbers.

6. When to call the inspector

  • Any suspicion of AFB or EFB — this is a legal obligation in most countries.
  • Sudden, unexplained colony death with dark, sunken brood.
  • Unusual mortality at the entrance with no obvious cause.
  • Any disease you cannot identify with confidence.
  • Any suspicion of an exotic pest (small hive beetle in a region where it is not established, Tropilaelaps mite, etc.).

7. Closing — course review

You have now designed, built and now learned to keep a wooden beehive through its full life cycle: base and stand, brood box and supers, frames, roof, ventilation, feeding, harvesting and health.

The next steps in your operation are usually one of:

  • Increase colony numbers — splits, swarm capture, queens. Plan the apiary space before you increase the count.
  • Move to a brick hive system — the companion course covers the same material with masonry design, thermal mass and permanent siting.
  • Value-add — comb honey, creamed honey, beeswax products, propolis. Each has its own market and its own regulations.
  • Queen rearing — a specialty skill that rewards good record keeping and consistent management.

The habit that matters most

Keep the record. Every inspection, every treatment, every yield. Your record is what turns a hobby into a business, and it is the only thing that lets you see what actually works in your own apiary.

Assignment 12 — Final assignment for the course

  1. List the eight main pests and diseases and the key sign of each.
  2. Describe the ropiness test for AFB and state what you would do if the test is positive.
  3. Choose a varroa monitoring method and state your threshold and treatment plan.
  4. List three IPM measures you will use and one treatment you will use if monitoring crosses the threshold.
  5. Design a one-page inspection log sheet with at least 9 fields. Use it for one month.
  6. Design a master colony record for one hive, including queen details, treatment history and yields.
  7. List the situations where you would contact your bee inspector.
  8. Write a single paragraph: the one habit that, if you keep it for a year, will most improve your colony survival. Explain why.
