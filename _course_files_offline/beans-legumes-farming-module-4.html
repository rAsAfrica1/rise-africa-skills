Lesson 1

Water Needs and Waterlogging Risk

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand legume water needs
  • Avoid waterlogging, the most common cause of legume failure
  • Time supplementary irrigation if available

Legume water needs are similar to cereals, but the sensitivity is different:

  • Common bean: 300–500 mm over the season. Sensitive to both drought and waterlogging.
  • Cowpea: 300–400 mm. Most drought-tolerant.
  • Groundnut: 500–700 mm. Sensitive to drought at pegging and pod fill.
  • Soybean: 450–700 mm. Sensitive to drought at flowering and pod fill.

Waterlogging — the silent killer

Legumes hate waterlogged soil. Their nitrogen-fixing nodules need oxygen. Waterlogged soils suffocate the nodules, cause root rot, and can kill the crop within a week. If your field holds water after heavy rain, beans will fail.

Prevention:

  • Plant on ridges or raised beds where waterlogging is a risk
  • Ensure drainage channels around the plot
  • Avoid heavy soils with poor drainage for bean crops; use them for cereals instead
  • Do not irrigate heavily — light, frequent watering is better

Drought and irrigation

Critical stages: flowering and pod fill. If you have irrigation, prioritise water for those two windows. A single irrigation at flowering can save 30–50% of the yield in a dry spell.

Lesson 2

Intercropping Legumes with Cereals

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Design a cereal-legume intercrop that pays
  • Match species to the intercrop system
  • Time planting so both crops get established

Why intercrop

Cereal-legume intercrops are one of the oldest and most productive African farming systems. They:

  • Fix nitrogen that benefits the cereal in the same season
  • Cover the ground and suppress weeds
  • Diversify income and reduce risk
  • Feed the household from two crops instead of one

The classic intercrop combinations

  • Maize + cowpea: cowpea between maize rows. Cowpea fixes nitrogen and provides grain. Maize yields drop by 10–20%, but total value rises.
  • Sorghum + cowpea: the Sahel's classic system. Both drought-tolerant.
  • Maize + common bean: climbing beans on maize stalks. Common in East Africa.
  • Groundnut + maize: both cash crops, complementary root systems.
  • Pigeon pea + maize: long-season pigeon pea intercropped with short-season maize. Pigeon pea continues after maize harvest.

Design rules

  • Reduce cereal density by 25–40% to make room for the legume
  • Use a legume that matures at a similar or later time — avoid a short legume that matures before the cereal and loses to shading
  • Plant in alternating rows or in a row between cereal rows
  • Do not intercrop a climbing legume with a short cereal — the legume will smother it
  • Adjust fertiliser: less nitrogen needed (legume fixes some), same phosphorus

Lesson 3

Reading the Crop's Growth Stages

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify the five growth stages of a legume crop
  • Know what action each stage requires
  • Catch problems before they are irreversible

Stage 1 — Emergence (0–7 days)

Seedlings break the surface. Check for uniform stand. Gaps mean either poor seed, dry soil, or pest damage. Re-plant gaps within 10 days.

Stage 2 — Vegetative (1–4 weeks)

Leaf and stem growth. Check for bean fly, aphids, and leaf diseases. Weed at 2–3 weeks. First phosphorus uptake happens here.

Stage 3 — Flowering (4–6 weeks for bush beans; 6–8 weeks for climbers)

Flowers appear. Pod borer pressure begins. Water stress now causes flower drop. Do not disturb roots. A light nitrogen top-up is sometimes applied (30 kg/ha) but must not suppress nodulation.

Stage 4 — Pod fill (6–9 weeks)

Pods fill with seed. Water stress now reduces seed size. Continue scouting for pod borer. No heavy cultivation.

Stage 5 — Maturity (8–12 weeks)

Pods turn from green to yellow to dry. Harvest when 80–90% of pods are dry, before they shatter.

Lesson 4

Weeding and Canopy Closure

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Time the critical weedings
  • Understand when the crop can defend itself
  • Weed without damaging shallow roots

The critical weed-free period

The first 4–6 weeks after planting. This is when the crop is most vulnerable to weed competition and when weeds do the most damage. After the canopy closes, the crop suppresses most weeds on its own.

Two weedings are enough

  1. First weeding: 2–3 weeks after emergence. Hoe between rows, hand-pull within the row.
  2. Second weeding: 5–6 weeks after emergence. Final pass before canopy closure.

Legume root systems

Legume roots are shallower and more delicate than cereal roots, especially at nodulation. Do not hoe deep — a 3–5 cm scratch is enough. Deep hoeing damages nodules and reduces nitrogen fixation.

Mulching

A 5–8 cm mulch layer between rows reduces weed emergence, keeps soil moisture steady, and keeps soil temperature cooler. Especially useful in areas with irregular rain.

Lesson 5

The Daily and Weekly Routine

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Adopt the daily walk
  • Set the weekly tasks that keep the crop on track
  • Keep records that make next season better

Daily (10–15 min per 1,000 m²)

  • Walk the plot at the same time each morning
  • Check the crop for wilting, yellowing, pest damage
  • Remove obviously diseased plants
  • Check soil moisture — water if wilting in the morning

Weekly

  • Scout for pod borer (from flowering) or bean fly (in seedling stage)
  • Weed small escapes
  • Fertilise if scheduled
  • Record observations and dates

Monthly

  • Review records — what has worked, what has not
  • Plan next weeding or spray
  • Check stored seed for weevils

Legume crops reward attention. An hour a day of walking and observing is more valuable than an extra bag of fertiliser.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. What does waterlogging do to legumes?

Correct: suffocates nodules and causes root rot. Plant on ridges where waterlogging is a risk.

2. What is the most drought-tolerant legume?

Correct: cowpea. Grows where common bean and soybean fail.

3. What is the critical water stage for groundnut?

Correct: pegging and pod fill. Water stress now causes empty pods.

4. How much should cereal density be reduced in an intercrop?

Correct: 25–40%. Makes room for the legume without collapsing cereal yield.

5. When should legumes be weeded?

Correct: 2–3 weeks and 5–6 weeks. After 6 weeks the canopy suppresses most weeds.

Module 4 capstone

Write your legume growing plan. Include: (1) your water source and reliability; (2) whether waterlogging is a risk and how you will prevent it; (3) your intercrop design (if applicable); (4) your weeding dates; (5) your daily walk time.
