Lesson 1

Why Sorghum and Millet Are a Different Business

~10 min
In this lesson
  • State why these cereals behave differently from maize in African dryland systems
  • List the three commercial markets that pay for sorghum and millet grain
  • Explain why "climate change crop" is not a marketing slogan but an economic fact

Maize fails first when the rain is short. Sorghum and millet finish. That single fact decides which crop sits at the centre of a dryland farm business, and it has been true in Africa for five thousand years.

But being climate-resilient is not enough. A farmer who grows sorghum that has no buyer is worse off than a farmer who grows maize with a buyer. The reason sorghum and millet are commercially interesting right now is that three real, contracting markets have appeared in the last two decades and are still growing: grain for food (especially in Nigeria, Sudan, Ethiopia and the Sahel), malting-grade grain for the brewing industry, and feed-grade grain for poultry and livestock.

Each of those three buyers wants something different. Food buyers want taste and grain colour. Malting buyers want low-tannin white sorghum that meets tight brewing specifications. Feed buyers want volume and protein. When a farmer cannot name which buyer they are growing for, they cannot choose the right variety, the right fertility, or the right harvest method — and the crop fails commercially even if it succeeds agronomically.

The storage advantage is where the business quietly sits. Maize must be sold within months or treated with chemicals. Sorghum and millet, properly dried and hermetically stored, hold their quality for years. That means a farmer can refuse a low harvest-time price and sell later — the single most powerful pricing move available to a smallholder.

Lesson 2

The Three Crops and What Each One Is For

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet by use, yield and market
  • Choose which of the three fits your rainfall zone
  • Identify the processing products each one feeds

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)

The largest of the three. Grain heads are heavy, yields are the highest of the group under good management, and the malting market is built almost entirely on sorghum. Two broad types matter commercially: white low-tannin sorghum for food, malting and feed, and brown tannin sorghum that birds dislike, used in areas where quelea damage is severe.

Pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum)

The most drought-tolerant grain in the world. Yields are lower than sorghum but the crop succeeds where sorghum would still fail. Pearl millet is the food crop of the Sahel and northern Nigeria, and its grain feeds a growing instant-food market in West Africa. Some varieties have high iron and zinc content, which is opening new institutional-buyer interest.

Finger millet (Eleusine coracana)

The premium small grain. Finger millet is the grain behind a growing health-food market — high calcium, high fibre, gluten-free. Prices per kilogram are often 2–3 times pearl millet prices in urban markets where the flour is sold as a specialty. Yields are modest, but the margin per hectare can be the best of the three if the farmer has a health-food buyer.

Most smallholders in a sorghum belt grow all three, in different plots, and use them for different purposes. The commercial decision is which one gets the best land, the best fertility and the best timing — because that is the one whose margin will carry the household.

Lesson 3

Yield Reality — African Averages vs Achievable

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Read the gap between African average yields and achievable yields
  • Identify the four factors that close most of the gap
  • Set a realistic target yield for your first sorghum season

The African average for sorghum sits near 0.8–1 t/ha. The achievable yield with the same improved varieties, basic fertility and timely weeding is 2.5–4 t/ha. The gap is not genetic — the seed already exists. It is management.

Four factors close most of the gap:

  1. Variety. Improved, short-statured, Striga-resistant varieties such as the released sorghum lines from ICRISAT and national programs. Landrace seed is free but costs 60–75% of potential yield.
  2. Stand. Sorghum and millet seed is small. It must be sown shallow (2–3 cm) and thinned to a single plant per station at the recommended spacing — typically 75 cm × 25 cm for sorghum. Most smallholders leave 2–3 plants per station and lose half the yield to competition.
  3. Fertility. Manure placed at the station rather than broadcast, plus a modest top-up of nitrogen at tillering. A 50 kg bag of ammonium nitrate per hectare lifts yield by around 0.8 t/ha on responsive soils.
  4. Weeding and Striga control. Two timely weedings in the first six weeks. Striga, if present, needs the multi-line strategy in Module 3.

The instruction is to stop comparing yourself to a research station and start comparing yourself to your own last season. Keep a plot record. Aim to move from 1 t/ha to 2 t/ha in one year — a 100% yield improvement, achievable from agronomy alone.

Lesson 4

The Three Markets That Pay

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish the food, malting and feed markets by price and specification
  • Identify the buyer type for each and what they require
  • Decide which market you are growing for before you sow

Food market

The default market. Households, urban millers and the growing instant porridge industry buy sorghum and millet for direct food use. Prices track season, and are typically 15–40% higher off-season than at harvest. Buyers care about: grain colour (white preferred for most flours), freedom from aflatoxin, and stone-free cleanliness. Payment is normally cash, sometimes short credit.

Malting market

The brewing industry — from large companies to the growing craft sector — buys sorghum for malting. This is the highest-paying market for grain that meets specification. Requirements are tight: white, low-tannin sorghum, high germination percentage, low broken-grain, tight moisture. Prices are typically 20–60% above food market when specification is met. Payment is often through contracted out-grower schemes and can be 30–60 days. Rejection is real if the grain fails spec.

Feed market

Poultry and livestock feed producers buy sorghum as a maize substitute. Prices track the maize price closely, with sorghum typically trading at 80–100% of the maize price depending on the season. Feed market is the largest buyer by volume in many countries, but the least demanding on specification. Payment is often on delivery, sometimes cash, sometimes 30 days.

A farmer cannot sell to all three markets with one batch. The variety, the harvest timing and the post-harvest handling differ for each. The business decision — made before sowing — is which market the crop is aimed at. Malting pays best but requires the right variety and clean post-harvest handling. Food is the fallback that always exists. Feed is the volume buyer that absorbs whatever fails the other two specs.

Lesson 5

Climate Resilience and Why This Crop Wins

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Explain why sorghum and millet survive drought that kills maize
  • Distinguish drought escape from drought tolerance
  • Use that difference in rotation and planting decisions

Sorghum and millet use three different strategies to survive drought, and understanding them changes how a farmer plants them.

Drought escape

Short-season varieties mature in 90–110 days, finishing before the rains fail. This is the strategy pearl millet uses in the Sahel — grow fast, finish early, avoid the drought. The planting decision is timing: sow with the first reliable rain, not the first rain.

Drought tolerance

Longer-season sorghum has deep roots, can roll its leaves to reduce water loss, and can suspend growth during a mid-season dry spell then resume when rain returns. This is true tolerance — the crop lives through the drought, not around it.

Recovery

Both sorghum and millet can partially recover grain yield after a mid-season stress that would kill a maize crop at the same stage. The yield loss is real, but the crop is not zero.

These strategies have commercial consequences. In areas with a reliable short rainy season, sorghum and millet can be planted late as a rescue crop when maize has already failed — a real business decision, not a symbolic one. In areas with a single, long, erratic season, the resilience premium shows up as yield stability rather than yield peak, and yield stability is what a lender, a co-op or an off-taker is actually buying.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. What is the African average sorghum yield per hectare?

Correct: 0.8–1 t/ha. The improved-potential figure is 2.5–4 t/ha; the African average is far below it.

2. Which market pays the highest price for sorghum grain?

Correct: malting market. Typically 20–60% above food market, but strict on variety and post-harvest handling.

3. Why do sorghum and millet store longer than maize?

Correct: drier grain and smaller size. Combined with hermetic storage, this gives 2–5 years of safe storage.

4. Which is a drought-escape strategy?

Correct: short-maturing variety. Escape is timing; tolerance is physiology.

5. What is the intended yield target for your first sorghum season per this course?

Correct: 2 t/ha. A doubling of the regional average from agronomy alone, achievable in one season.

Module 1 capstone

Write a one-page business brief for sorghum and/or millet on your farm. Include: (1) which of the three crops you will grow and why; (2) which market you are aiming at — food, malting or feed; (3) the target yield per hectare you will hold yourself to; (4) the rainfall zone you farm in and whether you should choose a short-season or long-season variety; (5) the one buyer or buyer type you will approach before sowing.

Send the brief to the person who will hold you to it. Read it again at harvest.
