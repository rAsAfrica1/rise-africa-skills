6. Health, Disease and Lameness

Horses hide pain. Every limp, dull eye, or missed meal is a message you must read.

Module 6 of 12 · Horse Breeding for Profit

Why horse health is demanding

Horses evolved as prey animals. They instinctively hide weakness - a sick horse in the wild is a target. By the time a horse shows obvious signs of illness, it is often seriously ill. Early detection is the entire skill.

Daily health check - the 10-point routine

CheckWhat to look for
AttitudeBright, alert, interested in surroundings
AppetiteCleans up feed, eager to eat
Water intakeNormal amount (25-45 L/day)
ManureNormal colour, texture, count (6-10 piles/day)
UrineNormal colour (pale yellow to dark), no straining
EyesBright, no discharge, no cloudiness
NoseClear discharge (a little is normal), no blood
LegsNo heat, swelling, or filling in lower legs
FeetNo heat, no digital pulse, no tenderness
MovementSound at walk and trot, no limping

Colic - the horse killer

Colic is abdominal pain. It has many causes but the symptoms are similar. Colic can kill within hours. Any horse with colic signs needs immediate vet contact.

SignWhat it means
Pawing at the groundMild colic - watch closely
Looking at flanksAbdominal pain
Rolling repeatedlySevere colic - call vet
No droppingsImpaction or blockage
Rapid breathing, sweatingSevere - emergency
Stretching out to urinateUrinary or colic

What to do (while waiting for the vet)

  1. Call the vet immediately.
  2. Keep the horse walking - do not let it roll (risk of twisted gut).
  3. Remove all feed but keep water available.
  4. Do not give painkillers unless the vet advises - they mask symptoms.
  5. Watch and record signs - heart rate, breathing, droppings, urine.
  6. Do not try to give medication by mouth - risk of choke.

Lameness - the biggest economic loss

Lameness is the number one reason horses lose value. A sound horse can work or compete; a lame one cannot. Most lameness is preventable with good hoof care and correct training.

ProblemSignsCause
Abscess in hoofSudden severe lameness, hot hoofStone bruise, bacteria entering sole
LaminitisBoth front feet, rocking back stanceRich feed, obesity, metabolic
NavicularChronic front hoof pain, worse on hard groundConformation, wear, hereditary
Tendon injurySwelling behind cannon bone, heatOverwork, fast work on hard ground
Bruised soleTender on hard ground, no heatStones, hard work, thin soles

Age/StatusFrequency

Parasite management

  • Rotate paddocks - reduce worm burden 60-80%.
  • Harvest manure regularly - remove before larvae spread.
  • Do not overstock - 1 horse per 0.4-0.8 ha pasture.
  • Rotate dewormers - prevent resistance.
  • Fecal egg counts - monitor worm burden instead of blind deworming.
  • Quarantine new horses - 3 weeks minimum.

Common emergencies

EmergencyImmediate action
Severe colicCall vet, walk horse, no feed
Choke (feed stuck in throat)Call vet, do not let horse eat or drink
Severe bleedingPressure bandage, call vet
Eye injuryCover, call vet - eye damage worsens fast
Deep woundClean, bandage, call vet
Sudden severe lamenessCheck hoof for abscess, call vet
Difficult foalingCall vet immediately
Founder (acute laminitis)Remove feed, call vet, deep bedding

First-aid kit for horses

  • Bandages, gauze, cotton wool
  • Antiseptic solution (iodine, chlorhexidine)
  • Antibiotic ointment
  • Poultice for hooves
  • Hoof pick, rasp, shoe puller
  • Thermometer (digital)
  • Scissors, tweezers, syringe without needle
  • Stethoscope (for heart rate)
  • Epsom salts (for abscesses)
  • Electrolytes
  • Clean buckets, water
  • Twitch (for restraint)

Biosecurity

  1. Quarantine new horses 3-4 weeks.
  2. Quarantine horses returning from shows or sales.
  3. Separate paddocks, tools, and water for quarantined animals.
  4. Wash hands between handling groups of horses.
  5. Disinfect trailers and equipment between uses.
  6. Limit visitors - especially from other yards.
  7. Vaccinate all horses on schedule.
  8. Isolate any horse with fever or discharge immediately.

When to call the vet

  • Any colic signs beyond mild.
  • Lameness that does not resolve in 24 hours.
  • High fever (over 38.5 C).
  • Nasal discharge with fever or cough.
  • Any wound near a joint or tendon.
  • Eye injuries.
  • Difficult foaling.
  • Sudden severe weight loss.
  • Any nervous signs (staggering, circling).
The lameness rule: A lame horse is worth 10-30% of a sound horse. Treat every lameness seriously, treat every hoof problem immediately, and keep a good farrier on your team.
Practical exercise 6: Check all your horses daily for 7 days using the 10-point routine. Pick out feet daily. Note any changes. Establish a working relationship with a horse vet before you need one.
