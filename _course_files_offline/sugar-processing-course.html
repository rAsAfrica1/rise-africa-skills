Aflatoxin contamination in maize and groundnuts is a serious health hazard and a major trade barrier. Aflatoxins are produced by Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus moulds, which thrive in warm, humid conditions common across much of sub-Saharan Africa. Chronic exposure causes liver cancer, immune suppression, and stunting in children. Acute high-dose exposure can be fatal.

  • Never sell or process visibly mouldy, discoloured, or musty-smelling grain. Mould does not always produce aflatoxin, but you cannot tell by sight or smell alone.
  • Dry grain to below 13% moisture content before storage. Use a moisture meter â do not rely on feel.
  • Store in hermetic (airtight) containers or well-ventilated, raised, dry stores. Never store on bare ground or against damp walls.
  • Use certified aflatoxin test kits before selling to formal processors or exporters. Regulatory limits are typically 10â20 ppb for food and 20â300 ppb for animal feed depending on destination market.
  • Train all staff handling grain on hygiene, moisture control, and pest management. Document every batch.
  • If you suspect contamination, do not blend contaminated grain with clean grain to dilute levels â this is illegal in most markets and creates liability.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Validate your market before you produce. Secure at least one written expression of interest or offtake contract before planting or processing. Verbal assurances are not bankable.
  2. Calculate your break-even price per unit (per tonne, per litre, per crate) including all variable costs, transport, packaging, and a 10% contingency. Compare it to the prevailing market price before committing capital.
  3. Invest in post-harvest handling first. Drying, grading, and hermetic storage typically deliver the highest return on capital of any intervention in African staple value chains.
  4. Test for aflatoxin and other contaminants before every sale to formal buyers. Keep batch records for traceability.
  5. Register your business and obtain required certifications (food safety, weights and measures, tax). Informal operators are increasingly locked out of formal supply chains.
  6. Build a 3-month working capital buffer. Payment terms in African agri-markets are often 30â90 days, and seasonal price swings can be severe.
  7. Track key metrics monthly: cost per unit, gross margin, post-harvest loss rate, and customer payment days. What is not measured cannot be improved.
  8. Diversify your customer base. Relying on a single buyer exposes you to payment default and price manipulation. Aim for at least three active buyers.
