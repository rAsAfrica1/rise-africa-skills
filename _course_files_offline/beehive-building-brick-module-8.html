Module 8 — Ventilation, Thermal Mass and Cooling

Using brick's greatest strength — and stopping it becoming its greatest liability.

1. What thermal mass actually does

A brick wall stores heat. Thermal mass does not make a hive cool; it makes it slow. That is useful in a desert climate with a 15 K swing between day and night, and it is a liability in a humid coastal climate where the wall never gets a chance to discharge.

Diurnal lag — how far behind the wall runs

For a wall of thickness L, thermal diffusivity α (brick ≈ 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s), the time lag for the inner surface to peak after the outer surface peaks is approximately:

t_lag ≈ 0.14 × L² / α   (seconds)

For a 230 mm brick wall:
t_lag = 0.14 × (0.23)² / (0.5 × 10⁻⁶)
= 0.14 × 0.0529 / 0.0000005
= 14 812 s ≈ 4.1 hours

So a wall that peaks at 14:00 outside peaks at about 18:00 on the inside — after the sun is off it. In a dry climate, that lag is exactly what you want: the wall absorbs the afternoon heat and releases it at night when you can purge it.

The night purge

The brick hive's cooling strategy is not "stay cool all day". It is:

  1. Daytime: the wall absorbs heat slowly; the interior stays below ambient until late afternoon.
  2. Evening: open the high vent and the bottom inlet wide. Cool night air flushes the stored heat out.
  3. Dawn: close down to a minimum setting. The wall is now cool and will stay cool well into the next afternoon.

Night purge only works if night air is cooler than the wall

In a humid tropical night where the air stays at 27 °C and 90 % RH, purging does nothing except push wet air into the hive. Check your night temperature before you rely on this strategy. If night air is not at least 4–5 K cooler than the wall, do not purge — insulate instead.

2. Vent design for a brick hive

Brick hives usually have thick walls and a heavy roof, so ventilation openings must be designed in, not added later. Provide:

  • Low inlet — at or just above the floor, on the shaded side, screened. Minimum two, 40 × 20 mm each, adjustable with a sliding plate.
  • High outlet — at the top of the cavity, below the crown board, on the opposite (sunny) side. Two, 40 × 20 mm, screened and pluggable.
  • Gap above the crown board — 25–40 mm continuous air space between the crown board and the roof slab, open to the high outlets.
  • Entrance — kept separate from the ventilation inlet where possible, so bee traffic and airflow do not fight each other.

Sizing the vent pair

Stack pressure, h = 0.9 m cavity height, ΔT = 10 K:
ΔP = 0.043 × 0.9 × 10 = 0.387 Pa

Velocity: v = √(2 × 0.387 / 1.15) = 0.82 m/s

Two inlets of 0.04 × 0.02 m = 0.0016 m² total inlet area (treat outlet as at least as large):
Q = 0.0016 × 0.82 × 3600 = 4.7 m³/h

That is far below the 20–40 m³/h a strong colony needs. The lesson: small holes are decorative. To reach 25 m³/h at 0.82 m/s you need:

A = Q / (v × 3600) = 25 / (0.82 × 3600) = 0.0085 m² = 85 cm²

That is roughly a 300 × 30 mm slot, or four 50 × 40 mm holes. Design the openings at that scale from the start — retrofitting large openings into a finished brick hive is unpleasant work.

3. Roof, overhang and solar gain

The roof is where brick hives fail. A heavy masonry roof slab in direct sun becomes a radiator pointed straight down at the brood nest.

MeasureSpecificationEffect
Overhang100–150 mm all round (wider than a wooden hive)Shades the top of the wall; sheds rain clear of the vents
Reflective topWhite lime wash or light-coloured tileReduces surface temperature 10–20 °C
Roof cavity25–40 mm ventilated gap between slab and crown boardBreaks the conduction path; connects to high vents
Insulation25 mm rigid board above crown board (dry climate) or below slab (humid)Cuts downward radiation into the brood cavity
Double roof / second skinSeparate light cover on 50 mm spacers above the slabBest performance; slab stays near ambient

4. Evaporative cooling — useful and dangerous

Evaporating water absorbs about 2 450 kJ per litre. A shaded, wetted sand floor or a damp burlap panel on the shaded vent can drop incoming air temperature by several kelvin.

How much cooling is available?

Wet 2 m² of shade cloth, evaporating 3 L/day:
Energy absorbed = 3 × 2450 = 7 350 kJ/day = 7 350 / 86 400 ≈ 0.085 kW continuous

Compare with the heat a strong colony generates: roughly 40–80 W. So evaporative cooling can offset a meaningful fraction of colony heat — but only in dry air. At 80 % relative humidity the evaporation rate collapses and you are simply making the hive damp.

ClimateEvaporative coolingReason
Hot dry (RH < 40 %)Yes — very effectiveHigh evaporation potential
Hot humid (RH > 70 %)No — harmfulAir cannot take up more vapour; adds condensation and mould risk
Cool / coldNeverChills the cluster and wets the hive

6. Condensation and mould in a brick hive

Brick is porous and hygroscopic. It absorbs moisture and releases it slowly. If you add water faster than it can dry, the wall stays damp, mould grows, and the colony suffers.

  • Slope the floor to a drain and fit a drain plug — never let water pool.
  • Seal the interior of the brood cavity with a breathable lime render, not a plastic sheet. Plastic traps moisture behind it and rots the brick.
  • Inspect the high vents monthly for cobwebs and propolis blockages.
  • After any wet season, run a full dry-out period with all vents open before the next flow.

7. Brick vs wood — where each wins

PropertyBrick hiveWooden hive
Daytime heat dampingExcellentPoor
Night purge potentialExcellent (dry climates)Limited by low mass
Cold-season warmthGood, if dryGood, if insulated
Humidity controlPoor — holds moistureBetter — dries faster
AdaptabilityFixed openings onlyCan add boxes, screens, reducers freely
Cost of changeHighLow

Brick wins in hot, dry, high-swing climates and loses everywhere else. If you farm in a humid lowland, consider brick walls with a wooden super and roof — the best of both.

  2. Record your local day and night temperatures for one week. Is the night air at least 4–5 K cooler than the wall? State whether night purge is viable for you.
  3. Size your low inlet and high outlet. Show ΔP, v, and required area in cm² for 25 m³/h.
  4. Specify your roof: overhang, surface colour, cavity depth, insulation type and thickness.
  5. Decide whether evaporative cooling is appropriate for your climate and justify it with a humidity figure.
