Practical Reference

Cattle farming in Africa rewards operators who treat it as a business, not a lifestyle. The two commercial models are structurally different: beef is a capital-cycling business where your profit is locked into weight gain and sale timing, while dairy is a cash-flow business where your profit is locked into litres per cow per day and herd longevity. Before you spend money on animals, understand the cost structure, the biological timelines, and the market you are actually selling into. Below are the reference figures, a decision diagram, and the operating discipline that separates profitable herds from expensive hobbies.

Cattle farming involves live animals, veterinary medicines and food products. Treat the following as non-negotiable:

  • Withdrawal periods are law, not advice. Milk and meat from animals treated with antibiotics, dewormers or anti-inflammatory drugs must be withheld from sale for the full labelled withdrawal period. Selling milk containing antibiotic residues can collapse your buyer relationship, trigger regulatory action, and contribute to antimicrobial resistance.
  • Never use human medicines on cattle or exceed veterinary dosages. Off-label use without a vet's written instruction is both illegal and dangerous.
  • Zoonotic disease protection: brucellosis, tuberculosis and anthrax can pass from cattle to humans. Wear gloves for calving and abortus handling, do not drink raw milk from untested animals, and report sudden deaths to the veterinary authority immediately.
  • Vaccines and cold chain: store vaccines at 2-8Â°C. A broken cold chain means a failed vaccination and a false sense of protection.
  • Chemical safety: tick dips, pour-ons and pesticides are toxic. Use PPE, never decant into food or drink containers, and observe re-entry intervals for treated pens.
  • Record everything: animal ID, drug, dose, date, withdrawal end date. If you cannot show records, you cannot sell to any serious processor.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm your buyer before you buy animals. A signed or written expression of interest from an abattoir, feedlot, processor, co-op or milk collection centre is worth more than any business plan.
  2. Price the full cycle, not the animal. Include purchase price, feed for the whole cycle, vet costs, transport, labour, water and mortality allowance.
  3. Test your soil and water before stocking. Poor pasture and dirty water are the two most common hidden causes of low weight gain and low milk yield.
  4. Set a herd health calendar. Vaccinations, deworming, tick control and pregnancy checks scheduled by month, not by crisis.
  5. Keep individual animal records. Ear tag or brand every animal; record birth, treatments, weights and calving dates.
  6. Budget for the dry season before it arrives. Buy or conserve hay, silage or supplements while prices are low.
  7. Separate your business cash from household cash. Pay yourself a fixed salary; reinvest the rest. Cattle farms fail from cash-flow confusion, not from bad animals.
  8. Review numbers monthly. Cost per kg gain (beef) or cost per litre produced (dairy) is your single most important management figure.
