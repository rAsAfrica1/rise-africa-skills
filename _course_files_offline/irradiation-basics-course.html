Irradiation is a commercial decision before it is a scientific one. The buyer's question is never "was this irradiated?" â it is "will this shipment clear customs, survive the shelf, and still make margin?" This reference block gives you the numbers, the approval routes, and the documentation trail that turn a food-irradiation capability into a billable service or a cost-saving in-house step.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Radiation, food and chemical handling

Irradiation facilities are licensed radiation installations. The rules below are non-negotiable, and breaching them risks prosecution, loss of licence, and serious injury.

  • Only trained, authorised personnel may operate, load or maintain an irradiator. Never allow untrained staff, visitors or clients into the irradiation cell or source room.
  • Never enter a cell or source room without a functioning personal dosimeter, a survey meter reading, and the source in the confirmed safe position under a written lockout procedure.
  • Dosimetry is mandatory on every run. A shipment without a dose record is not a saleable shipment â it is an unverified claim.
  • Do not exceed national or Codex dose limits. Over-dosing can degrade food quality and breaches your licence conditions.
  • Irradiation does not sterilise a dirty product. It reduces microbial load; it does not remove toxins, chemicals, glass, metal or pest contamination. Do not market it as a substitute for hygiene.
  • Do not irradiate non-permitted categories â including any product not on your national clearance list â even if a client asks and pays.
  • Radiation sources are not the only hazard. Ozone, coolant, and high-voltage equipment in e-beam and X-ray facilities carry their own electrical and chemical risks. Follow the manufacturer's lockout/tagout and ventilation procedures.
  • Keep the source security plan current. Loss or theft of a radioactive source is a national security incident, not an insurance claim.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the clearance first. Before quoting any client, check that the exact product category is cleared in your country and, for exports, under Codex CXS 106. Get the clearance reference in writing.
  • Match technology to product format. Thin, flat, single-layer product can run on e-beam; palletised or dense product needs gamma or X-ray. Decide this before you buy equipment.
  • Fix your dose range and write it down. Minimum dose for efficacy, maximum dose for quality. Put both numbers on the client quotation.
  • Build a dosimetry routine. Calibrated dosimeters, a written procedure, and a retained dose record for every batch. This is your audit trail and your defence.
  • Cost per kilogram, not cost per pallet. Include source decay, throughput, labour, dosimetry, transport and rejected-batch risk. Recalculate monthly.
  • Train and certify staff. Radiation safety training, refresher intervals, and named responsible officers with documented authority.
  • Label correctly. Irradiated food must carry the international Radura symbol and the required statement where national law demands it. Non-compliance loses the shipment.
  • Test the claim before you sell it. Run a pilot batch and measure the actual shelf life or microbial reduction. Sell measured results, not brochure numbers.
