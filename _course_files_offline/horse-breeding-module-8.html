8. Training Young Horses

A well-trained horse is worth 3x an untrained one. Here is how to get there.

Module 8 of 12 · Horse Breeding for Profit

Why training matters

A horse's value depends on what it can do. An unhandled 3-year-old is worth little; a well-started 3-year-old that walks, trots, and canters under saddle is worth 3-10x more. Training is where breeding turns into money.

Handling a foal

The first 6 months set the tone for a lifetime. Handle young foals daily but gently:

  • Introduce the halter at 2-4 weeks.
  • Teach to lead quietly alongside the dam.
  • Pick up feet - always release when the foal is calm, not struggling.
  • Brush and touch all over - head, ears, belly, legs.
  • Introduce the farrier early - a foal that knows the farrier is easy to trim.
  • Never lose your temper. Foals remember frights.
  • Never let them push, bite, or kick. Correct gently but immediately.

Groundwork - the foundation

Groundwork is all training done from the ground. It teaches the horse respect, focus, and response to cues. A horse that will not lead, tie, or lunge is not ready to ride.

The 7 groundwork principles

  1. Consistency. Same cue every time. Never change signals.
  2. Patience. A horse that is rushed learns fear. Slow is fast.
  3. Release. The reward is release of pressure. When the horse does right, stop asking.
  4. Timing. Reward within 3 seconds of the correct response, or the horse does not connect it.
  5. Repetition. Short, frequent sessions (20-30 minutes) beat long, rare sessions.
  6. Routine. Same time, same place, same order. Horses relax with routine.
  7. Never end on a bad note. Finish on a success, even a small one.

Backing (breaking to ride)

Backing is the process of getting a young horse used to a rider. It should be done slowly and without force. A horse backed in 2 days will be reschooled later; a horse backed over 6 weeks will be solid for life.

The backing sequence

  1. Groundwork complete: leading, lunging, long-reining, tying, trailer.
  2. Saddle introduction: place the saddle on and off in the stable for a week. Lunging with the saddle on.
  3. Bridle introduction: bit and bridle on for short periods, then lunging with bridle.
  4. Accepting weight: lean over the back while standing still. Progress to hanging both legs on one side.
  5. First sit: a light rider sits up in the saddle while horse is standing. Someone holds the horse still.
  6. First walk under saddle: 5-10 minutes, quiet, straight lines, an assistant on the ground.
  7. Introduction to trot: only after walk is calm and forward.
  8. Introduction to canter: only after trot is balanced and easy.
  9. Continue schooling: every session for the first year is a school session.

Hacking out

Taking a young horse out of the arena is critical - it builds confidence and sanity. Start after 3-6 months of school work:

  • Start with 10 minutes on a quiet road, in company.
  • Build up to 45-60 minutes of quiet hacking.
  • Vary the routes and directions.
  • Introduce traffic slowly, always in company with an experienced horse.
  • Never hack a young horse alone in the first year.
  • Introduce hills, water crossings, and varied ground gradually.

Continuing education

Training is never finished. Even a 15-year-old horse benefits from schooling. For breeding stock:

  • Young horses need constant handling to stay safe.
  • Mares in foal benefit from light riding in early pregnancy.
  • Stallions need regular work to stay manageable.
  • Retirement means reduced work, not no handling.
The training rule: A horse trained slowly and correctly will be worth 5-10x a horse trained fast and wrong. Time spent on groundwork pays back tenfold under saddle.
Practical exercise 8: Handle a foal or young horse daily for a week. Teach or reinforce: leading, picking up feet, standing tied. Log every session and note progress.
