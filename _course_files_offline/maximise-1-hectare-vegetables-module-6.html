6. Pest and Disease Control

Pests and diseases do not destroy crops at random. They attack plants that are stressed, overcrowded, over-fed, or grown in the same bed for years. This module teaches you the 70% of control that costs nothing — and the 30% that uses chemicals correctly and safely.

Module 6 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 6.1 — Scouting: the 10-minute daily walk

Every commercial vegetable farm scouts daily. It is the single highest-return habit in farming: 10 minutes a day catches a problem when it costs $5 to fix, instead of $500.

How to scout properly:

  1. Walk a zig-zag path across the plot — not just along the edge.
  2. Check 5 plants at each of 10 random stops.
  3. Look at three places on each plant: the growing tip, the underside of a middle leaf, and the base of the stem.
  4. Turn leaves over. 80% of pests live on the underside.
  5. Write down what you see, where, and how many. Numbers matter — a "few" aphids is fine, "200 per leaf" is an emergency.

First, decide what kind of problem you have:

Problem typeSignatureClues
Chewing insectHoles, missing leaf edges, cut stemsFrass (droppings), night damage, caterpillar on leaf underside
Sucking insectYellow stippling, curling, sticky honeydew, sooty mouldAphids, whitefly, thrips, mites visible on leaf underside
Boring insectWilting shoot tips, entry holes in fruit or stemSawdust-like frass at the hole, hollow stem
Fungal diseaseSpots with defined edges, powdery or fuzzy growth, spreading in patchesWorse in humid weather, spreads up the plant from old leaves
Bacterial diseaseWater-soaked spots, sudden wilting, slimy cut stemNo fuzzy growth, spreads fast, often after rain or overhead irrigation
Viral diseaseMottled yellow/green mosaic, distorted leaves, stunted plantNo cure. Spread by aphids, whitefly, thrips. Remove the plant.
Nutrient deficiencyUniform pattern across many plants, older or younger leavesSame symptom on every plant of that crop, no insects present

Action thresholds — do not spray below these:

  • Aphids: spray when 20%+ of leaves have colonies and you see few ladybirds.
  • Whitefly: spray when you disturb 10 plants and 5+ whiteflies fly up from each.
  • Caterpillars (tomato): spray when 1 in 10 plants has active larvae in the fruit or growing tip.
  • Thrips: spray when 10+ per flower.
  • Spider mites: spray when webbing is visible on 10%+ of leaves.
  • Fungal disease: spray preventively in humid weather, or at the very first spot — never wait for it to spread.
The scouting rule: Never spray because you "saw a bug". Spray because you counted a population above the threshold. Spraying below threshold kills the beneficial insects that were doing the job for free.
Practical exercise 6.1: Scout every morning for 7 days. Record date, crop, pest, count, and location. By day 7 you will see a pattern — certain beds and certain edges always have the problem first.

Lesson 6.2 — Cultural controls — the free 70%

Most pest problems are created by how the crop is grown, not by the pest. Fix the growing conditions and most of the problem disappears.

The stress principle:

Pests and disease attack weak plants first. A plant that is water-stressed, over-fed with nitrogen, or growing in compacted waterlogged soil has weak cell walls and low natural defence. The best pesticide is a healthy plant in healthy soil.

  • Over-watered: roots suffocate, root rot, wilt.
  • Under-watered: flower drop, blossom-end rot, spider mite explosion.
  • Over-nitrogen: soft leafy growth, aphid and whitefly magnet.
  • Compacted soil: poor roots, all diseases worse.
The 70% rule: Rotation, spacing, mulching, drip irrigation, sanitation, and resistant varieties will prevent 70% of the pest and disease problems you would otherwise spray for. Do these six things first, every season, before you buy a single chemical.
Practical exercise 6.2: Walk your plot and list every cultural control you are NOT currently doing. Pick the two easiest and implement them this week. Sanitation and mulching are usually the fastest wins.

Lesson 6.3 — Beneficial insects and biological control

Your farm already has a free pest-control workforce. The problem is that most farmers kill it with the first broad-spectrum spray.

BeneficialWhat it eatsHow to recognise it

Lacewing larvaAphids, mites, thrips, caterpillarsBrown, sickle-jawed, fast-moving
Hoverfly larvaAphidsGreen maggot among aphid colonies
Parasitic waspAphids, whitefly, caterpillars, leafminerTiny wasp; look for brown mummified aphids

How to keep them on your farm:

  1. Plant flowering borders. Coriander, dill, basil, marigold, sunflower, and fennel give adult beneficials nectar and pollen. A 1 m border along one side is enough.
  2. Leave some undisturbed habitat. A small patch of tall grass or a hedge is where beneficials shelter and overwinter.
  3. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides. Products that kill aphids also kill ladybirds, lacewings, and parasitic wasps. Then aphids return with no enemies and explode.
  4. Use selective and botanical products first. They have far less impact on beneficials.
  5. Tolerate a low pest population. Beneficials need food. Zero aphids means zero ladybirds, so the next aphid wave is unopposed.

Biological and botanical options:

ProductTargetsNotes
Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt)Caterpillars onlyVery selective — does not harm beneficials or bees. Caterpillar must eat it. Degrades in sunlight in 2–3 days.
Neem (azadirachtin)Aphids, whitefly, thrips, mites, caterpillarsAnti-feedant, growth disruptor, low mammalian toxicity. Slower acting. Spray late evening.
Insecticidal soapAphids, whitefly, mites, soft-bodied insectsMust contact the pest directly. No residual. Do not use in full sun — burns leaves.
Pyrethrum (natural pyrethrin)Broad range of insectsBroad-spectrum and harms beneficials. Use as a last resort, at dusk, when bees are gone.
Diatomaceous earthAnts, slugs, soil insectsDry powder scratches insect cuticle. Useless when wet.
TrichodermaSoil fungi — fusarium, damping off, root rotApply to compost or soil at planting. A preventive, not a cure.
Wood ashAphids (dusting), snails (barrier)Use sparingly — raises soil pH if over-applied.
The beneficial rule: Before you spray, ask: "How many ladybirds and parasitic wasps will this kill?" If the answer is "many", and the pest count is below threshold, do not spray. You are about to make the problem worse.
Practical exercise 6.3: Spend 15 minutes in your crop with a hand lens, counting beneficials and pests separately. Photograph what you find. Once you can tell a hoverfly larva from a caterpillar, you will never spray the same way again.

Lesson 6.4 — Spraying correctly and safely

If you must spray, spray properly. A badly applied spray wastes money, fails to control the pest, and poisons you and your family.

The correct sequence:

  1. Identify the pest or disease correctly. Wrong product = wasted money. Fungicide does nothing for aphids.
  2. Read the label. Rate, target, pre-harvest interval, safety precautions, and re-entry time.
  3. Calibrate the sprayer. Measure how much water your knapsack puts out over 100 m². Label rates are per hectare — you must convert to your knapsack.
  4. Wear full PPE. Gloves, long sleeves, long trousers, boots, face mask, eye protection. Every single time.
  5. Spray in early morning or late evening. No wind (above 10 km/h drift will miss the crop and hit your neighbour). Never in midday heat.
  6. Spray the underside of leaves and the growing tips. Most pests are there.
  7. Do not spray wet foliage. Wait until the dew dries, or spray in the evening after it is dry.
  8. Respect the pre-harvest interval (PHI). This is the number of days between the last spray and harvest. It is a legal and health limit, not a suggestion.
  9. Rotate active ingredients. Never use the same chemical group twice in a row. Pests develop resistance fast.
  10. Wash yourself immediately after. Shower, wash clothes separately from family laundry.

The five pesticide rules that are not negotiable:
1. Never spray without a mask and gloves.
2. Never spray at flowering — you kill pollinators.
3. Never spray in wind or midday heat.
4. Never eat or smoke while spraying.
5. Never store pesticides in a water bottle or near food. Farmers die every year from drinking from a "water" bottle that held pesticide.
Practical exercise 6.4: Calibrate your knapsack sprayer this week. Fill it with 15 L of clean water and spray a measured 100 m² bed. Measure how much you used. That number is the basis for every correct dose you will ever apply.

Fungal vs bacterial vs viral — how to tell in the field:

  • Fungal: visible growth (fuzz, powder, mould) on the leaf surface, spreads in clearly defined patches. Treatable with fungicides.
  • Bacterial: water-soaked, slimy, sharp-edged spots, often angular between leaf veins. Spreads explosively after rain. Only copper-based products work, and only preventively.
  • Viral: mosaic patterns, distortion, no rot or spots. Cannot be cured. Remove the plant and control the insect vector.
The wilting rule: If a green tomato plant wilts overnight with no yellowing and the cut stem shows a brown ring or white ooze, it is bacterial wilt. There is no spray. Pull it, bag it, remove it from the field today, and plan a 4-year rotation.
Practical exercise 6.5: Walk your plot and find one diseased plant. Using the table, diagnose which of the three categories it falls into. Decide: treat, remove, or ignore. Write down your reasoning.

Lesson 6.6 — Your integrated pest management plan

IPM is not a product. It is an order of operations: prevent first, monitor second, treat last, and always use the least harmful effective option.

The IPM decision ladder — work down only when the step above fails:

  1. Prevent. Rotation, resistant varieties, clean seed, sanitation, drip irrigation, correct spacing, mulching, balanced nutrition.
  2. Monitor. Daily scouting, sticky traps, written records, action thresholds.
  3. Mechanical / physical. Handpicking, row covers, traps, barriers, pruning out infected parts.
  4. Biological / botanical. Bt, neem, soap, Trichoderma, encourage beneficials.
  5. Targeted chemical. Correct product, correct rate, correct timing, correct PPE, PHI respected, active ingredients rotated.

Weekly seasonal calendar:

WeekTask
Pre-plantSoil test, lime if needed, compost incorporated, resistant varieties chosen, seed sourced, nursery trays sterilised
Week 1–2 (nursery)Daily nursery check, airflow, avoid overwatering, remove any collapsed cells immediately
Week 3–4Hardening off, bed preparation, mulch ready, sticky traps installed at field edges
Week 5 (transplant)Transplant late afternoon, shade 3 days, daily scouting begins, record log started
Week 6–10 (vegetative)Scout daily, handpick caterpillars, weekly aphid/whitefly counts, foliar micronutrient if needed, stake plants
Week 10–14 (flowering)Do not spray insecticides at flowering — protect pollinators. Watch for thrips and blossom-end rot. Weekly calcium if needed.
Week 14–20 (fruiting/harvest)Scout fruit for borer and anthracnose. Spray only above threshold and only products with a short PHI. Harvest early morning.
Post-harvestRemove and burn all crop debris. Do not compost diseased material. Soil test every 2 years. Plan next rotation.

The record you must keep:

  • Date, crop, block
  • Pest or disease found, count, and where
  • Action taken (nothing / handpicked / sprayed with what and how much)
  • Result 3 days later

After one season, this log becomes the most valuable document on your farm. It tells you exactly which block gets which pest, at which week, every year — so you can prevent it before it starts.

The IPM rule: Every spray you avoid by preventing or handpicking is money saved, a beneficial insect saved, and a residue you did not put on your customer's food. Spraying is the last step, not the first.
Practical exercise 6.6: Write your own IPM plan for your main crop on one page. List: the 3 worst pests/diseases you expect, the preventive actions, the threshold for each, and the product you would use if you had to. Keep it in your pocket for the season.
