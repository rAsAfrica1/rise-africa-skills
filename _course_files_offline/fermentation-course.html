Practical Reference

Fermentation is one of the highest-margin food businesses you can run from a small African kitchen or backyard shed, because your main inputs â vegetables, milk, cereals, salt â are cheap and locally available, while the finished product (yoghurt, kimchi-style kraut, mageu, ogi, sourdough, hot sauces) sells at a multiple of the raw material price. The single biggest reason small fermenters fail is not skill; it is inconsistency. Buyers return when the product tastes the same every week. This reference section gives you the temperature, salt, pH and yield numbers you need to hit that consistency, plus the safety limits that protect your brand and your customers.

By 72 hours, a healthy ferment has dropped below pH 4.0 â well under the pH 4.6 threshold that stops Clostridium botulinum and most spoilage organisms. If your batch has not crossed below pH 4.6 within 72 hours at ambient African temperatures (which are usually warm, so fermentation is fast), you have a salt, temperature, or hygiene problem. Test it, do not guess it. A digital pH meter costs little compared to the cost of a spoiled 50-litre batch or a customer complaint.

â  SAFETY WARNING â READ BEFORE YOUR FIRST BATCH

Fermentation is a food-safety-critical process. Follow these non-negotiables:

  • Never sell a batch that has not reached pH 4.6 or below within 72 hours. Above that line, Clostridium botulinum and other pathogens can survive and grow. A cheap digital pH meter is mandatory equipment, not optional.
  • Never taste or sell a ferment with visible mould (fuzzy, coloured), slime, or a rotten/faecal smell. Kahm yeast (a thin white film) is usually harmless, but fuzzy mould is not. When in doubt, throw the batch out â one bad batch can end your business reputation permanently.
  • Use only clean, food-grade containers and never metal that can corrode. Use glass, food-grade plastic, or ceramic. Keep everything submerged under the brine with a weight â exposed vegetables grow mould.
  • Do not reduce salt below 2% for vegetable ferments. Salt is your primary safety barrier in the first 48 hours. Cutting salt to "improve taste" is the most common cause of failed and unsafe batches.
  • Pasteurise or refrigerate dairy and high-risk products. Raw-milk yoghurt and soft cheeses carry Listeria and E. coli risk. If you sell to the public, follow your national food safety authority's requirements for registration and testing.
  • Keep a batch log. Record date, recipe, salt %, temperature, pH readings and observations. If a customer ever reports illness, your log is your defence and your route to fixing the problem.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Weigh, don't guess. Use a digital scale for salt and a measuring jug for brine. 2.0â2.5% salt by weight is your vegetable baseline.
  • Test pH at 24, 48 and 72 hours. Log every reading. Do not move product to sale until it is below pH 4.6, ideally below 4.0.
  • Keep everything submerged. Use a clean weight or a water-filled bag to hold vegetables under the brine â air contact means mould.
  • Control temperature. Ferment in the coolest part of your building. In hot African conditions, aim for 18â25Â°C for vegetables and 42â45Â°C for yoghurt.
  • Sanitise everything. Wash hands, jars, lids, weights and utensils with hot soapy water, then rinse. Clean equipment is the cheapest insurance you can buy.
  • Label and date every container. Product name, batch number, date started, pH at packaging, and best-before date.
  • Start small, then scale. Run 3â5 test batches of 5 litres or less before committing to a 50-litre production run.
  • Know your market before you scale. Confirm price, packaging size and shelf-life expectations with at least three potential buyers before you invest in bulk containers.
