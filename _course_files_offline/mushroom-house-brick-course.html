Practical Reference

This reference block consolidates the hard numbers, drawings and checklists you will need on site. It assumes you have worked through the 12 modules and now want to build, cost and operate a fixed brick grow-house for oyster (Pleurotus spp.) and button (Agaricus bisporus) mushrooms at small commercial scale. Treat every figure as a starting specification to be adjusted for your species, altitude and micro-climate — then verified locally before you spend money on bricks.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING — Health, food and chemical hazards

  • Never eat or sell mushrooms you cannot positively identify. Some Amanita and Galerina species are deadly and grow near cultivated sites. Train every worker to recognise the difference between your crop and wild look-alikes.
  • Spore exposure: prolonged inhalation of mushroom spores can cause "mushroom worker's lung" (hypersensitivity pneumonitis). Wear an N95 or FFP2 mask during harvesting, cleaning and substrate turning. Ventilate before entering a closed room.
  • Pasteurisation and sterilisation: open-flame burners and steam drums produce carbon monoxide and scalds. Pasteurise outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed, never in a closed room. Keep a fire extinguisher within 5 m.
  • Chemicals: formalin, bleach, hydrogen peroxide and fungicides are corrosive and toxic. Store in locked, labelled containers away from substrate and food. Use gloves, goggles and a mask. Never mix bleach with acid or ammonia — it releases chlorine gas.
  • Electrical: misting pumps and exhaust fans in a wet room require an earth-leakage (RCD) breaker and IP-rated fittings. Water and electricity kill.
  • Food safety: wash hands, use clean crates, and cold-hold harvested mushrooms at 2–4 °C. Do not sell produce from a contaminated batch — destroy it away from the grow-house.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm species requirements first. Get written temperature, humidity and CO₂ targets from your spawn supplier before you lay the first brick. Do not build for a species you have not confirmed.
  2. Check site drainage and orientation. Slope the ground away from the house, keep the long wall off the prevailing wind, and never site below a livestock pen or compost heap.
  3. Verify wall thermal mass. A 230 mm burnt-brick wall gives useful thermal buffering. In hot lowveld conditions add a ventilated roof space and reflective roof sheet.
  4. Install the vapour barrier before the ceiling board. Tape every joint. A failed vapour barrier causes condensation, mould and contamination within one cycle.
  5. Calibrate your instruments. A digital thermo-hygrometer and a CO₂ meter are not optional — you cannot manage what you cannot measure. Log readings twice daily.
  6. Separate clean and dirty routes. One door in for clean substrate and spawn, one door out for spent substrate and waste. Never let the two cross.
  7. Test pasteurisation with a thermometer, not a clock. Hold substrate at 60–70 °C for 60–90 minutes and confirm core temperature in at least three bags.
  8. Cost every cycle before you sell. Track substrate, spawn, fuel, water, labour and losses. Know your break-even price per kilogram before you negotiate with a buyer.
