Lesson 1

Why Wheat and Barley Matter to Africa

In this lesson
  • Understand the scale of African wheat imports
  • Recognise the import substitution opportunity
  • Understand where wheat and barley can be grown in Africa

Africa imports over $15 billion of wheat every year — more than any other food commodity. Egypt, Nigeria, Algeria, Morocco, Sudan, Ethiopia and South Africa are the largest importers. In most of these countries, domestic production covers a fraction of demand.

That gap is the single biggest business opportunity in African agriculture. A farmer who produces wheat or barley for the domestic milling or malting market is competing directly with imports — and governments support that competition with tariffs, subsidies, and guaranteed purchase programmes.

Where wheat and barley grow in Africa

  • North Africa: Egypt, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia — large-scale irrigated and rain-fed
  • East African highlands: Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania — high-altitude rain-fed
  • Southern Africa: South Africa, Zimbabwe, Zambia — irrigated winter wheat and dryland barley
  • West Africa: Nigeria, Mali — irrigated dry-season wheat in the Sahel

Wheat and barley are cool-season crops. In tropical Africa, they grow at altitude (over 1,500 m) or in winter/dry-season irrigation systems.

Lesson 2

Wheat vs Barley — What Each One Is For

In this lesson
  • Distinguish wheat and barley by use
  • Choose the right crop for your market
  • Understand the differences in agronomy

Wheat (Triticum aestivum and T. durum)

  • Bread wheat (T. aestivum): the dominant type. Used for bread, biscuits, pasta (some), noodles. High gluten content.
  • Durum wheat (T. durum): hard wheat for pasta and couscous. Higher price, more demanding.
  • Cycle: 120–150 days depending on variety and altitude
  • Yield: 3–6 t/ha under good management

Barley (Hordeum vulgare)

  • Malting barley: for beer and whisky production. Strict quality specifications — protein content, germination, grain size.
  • Feed barley: for livestock. Less strict on quality. Prices track feed grain.
  • Food barley: for porridge and traditional foods. Smaller market.
  • Cycle: 100–140 days
  • Yield: 2.5–5 t/ha

Key differences

  • Barley is more drought and salt-tolerant than wheat
  • Barley matures faster — important in short seasons
  • Barley has a lower yield ceiling but higher price when malting grade
  • Wheat has a bigger total market but competes with imports
Lesson 3

The Four Markets — Food, Malting, Feed, Seed

In this lesson
  • Name and price the four markets
  • Match grade to buyer
  • Plan for the highest margin

Market 1 — Wheat milling

Millers buy wheat for flour. Prices track imports plus a local premium — typically $0.30–0.55/kg. Requires clean, dry grain with protein and moisture specifications. Payment on delivery or 30 days.

Market 2 — Malting barley

Breweries and maltsters buy malting barley. Prices 20–50% above feed barley when spec is met. Strict requirements: protein 10.5–12.5%, grain size above 2.5 mm, germination above 95%, moisture below 12.5%. Contract-based.

Market 3 — Feed grain

Feed mills buy feed wheat and feed barley. Prices track maize and global feed markets — $0.25–0.45/kg. Larger volume, lower specification.

Market 4 — Seed

Certified seed producers and distributors buy certified parent seed at 2–3x food price. Requires certification and strict quality control.

Lesson 4

Yield Reality and the African Gap

In this lesson
  • Know the African average vs achievable yield
  • Identify the five factors that close the gap
  • Set a realistic first-season target

African average wheat yield is 1.5–2.5 t/ha. Achievable with improved varieties, irrigation and fertiliser: 4–6 t/ha. Under good irrigation and management, 7+ t/ha is recorded in Egypt and South Africa.

Five factors that close the gap

  1. Variety. Improved semi-dwarf varieties with rust resistance. Local landraces yield half.
  2. Irrigation. Wheat needs 400–650 mm of water. Rain-fed wheat in Africa is unreliable; irrigation delivers the highest yields.
  3. Fertility. Wheat is a heavy feeder. 150–200 kg/ha of nitrogen split across three applications, plus phosphorus and potassium.
  4. Rust control. Rust can cut yield 30–70%. Rust-resistant varieties plus a spray programme are essential.
  5. Timing. Planting date, first irrigation, and harvest timing all affect yield significantly.
Lesson 5

Choosing Wheat or Barley for Your Farm

In this lesson
  • Match crop to your environment
  • Match crop to your market
  • Decide entry scale

Choose wheat if

  • You have irrigation (wheat is water-hungry)
  • You have a milling market or contract
  • You farm above 1,500 m altitude, or in a cool-season winter system
  • You can access rust-resistant seed

Choose barley if

  • Your rainfall is marginal (barley is more drought-tolerant)
  • You have a brewery or maltster nearby
  • Your season is short (barley matures faster)
  • Your soil is slightly saline (barley tolerates salt)

Entry points

  • Smallholder ($300–1,500): 0.5–1 ha, irrigated or high-altitude rain-fed
  • Commercial ($2,000–10,000): 2–5 ha, irrigation, contract with a miller or malting plant
  • Enterprise ($15,000+): 5+ ha, own seed production, contracts at scale
