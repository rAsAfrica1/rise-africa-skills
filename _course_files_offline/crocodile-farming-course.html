â  SAFETY WARNING â Handling, Slaughter and Chemicals
  • Crocodiles are dangerous wild animals. Never handle animals over 1 m without trained staff, a restraint pole, and a second person present. A single bite can sever limbs.
  • Slaughter and skinning must be done in a licensed abattoir or approved on-farm facility. Crocodile meat can carry Salmonella, Trichinella and other zoonotic organisms. Cook meat to a minimum internal temperature of 70 Â°C.
  • Wear cut-resistant gloves, apron, eye protection, and closed boots during skinning. Cover all open wounds before entering the processing area.
  • Tanning and preservation chemicals â including sodium chloride, alum, chromium salts, and biocides â are hazardous. Store in labelled, locked containers away from feed and water. Use nitrile gloves and eye protection. Never mix chemicals in unlabelled vessels.
  • Wash hands and equipment with clean water and disinfectant after every handling session. Keep children and visitors out of pens and processing areas.
  • Report any bite, scratch, or chemical splash to a clinic immediately. Keep a first-aid kit and emergency contact list at the farm gate.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the licence path first. Contact your national wildlife authority and confirm the permit, CITES registration, and abattoir requirements before buying a single animal.
  2. Price three suppliers. Get written quotes for hatchlings, feed, water heating, and processing. Compare against the ranges in Table 1.
  3. Lock a buyer before you stock. Secure a letter of intent from a tannery, meat processor, or regional trader. Do not build 50 animals of inventory on hope.
  4. Plan a 42-month cash runway. Budget for feed, labour, heating, and vet costs with no skin revenue. Identify an interim income line (hatchling sales or meat).
  5. Build the paper trail from day one. Tag animals, log feed, record mortality, and keep health and movement records. Export buyers and CITES officers will ask.
  6. Install biosecurity and safety systems. Perimeter fencing, locked chemical store, footbaths, first-aid kit, and a written handling protocol signed by every worker.
  7. Grade your own skins. Learn the AâD grading scale used by tanneries so you can negotiate on evidence, not on the buyer's word.
  8. Review unit economics every six months. Update feed, fuel, and labour costs against actuals. Adjust stocking and sales timing accordingly.
