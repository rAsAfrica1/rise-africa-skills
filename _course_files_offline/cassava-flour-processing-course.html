Practical Reference

Cassava (Manihot esculenta) is one of Africa's most strategic crops: it tolerates poor soils, survives drought, and yields more calories per hectare than almost any cereal under low-input conditions. But raw cassava roots contain cyanogenic glucosides (linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide when the tissue is damaged. Flour processing is therefore not just a value-addition exercise â it is a detoxification process. Get the processing right and you have a shelf-stable, high-margin product. Get it wrong and you have a food-safety liability. This reference section gives you the numbers, the flow, and the checks you need to run a profitable, compliant cassava flour business anywhere in Africa.

â ï¸ SAFETY WARNING â CYANIDE AND FOOD SAFETY

Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glucosides that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when the root is crushed or grated. Bitter varieties can contain more than 400 mg HCN/kg fresh weight. Inadequate processing causes acute cyanide poisoning (headache, dizziness, vomiting, respiratory distress) and, with chronic low-level exposure, konzo â an irreversible paralytic disease documented in parts of Mozambique, DRC, Tanzania and Cameroon.

You MUST: (1) peel and grate thoroughly; (2) press and ferment for at least 12 hours for sweet varieties and 24â72 hours for bitter varieties; (3) dry to below 12% moisture; (4) test finished flour for residual HCN â the Codex limit is 10 mg/kg. Do not sell flour that has not been tested. Do not process bitter varieties without fermentation. Do not allow children to eat raw or under-processed cassava. Wear gloves and eye protection when handling press effluent, which is high in cyanide. Never discharge press water into drinking-water sources.

If anyone shows symptoms of cyanide poisoning after eating cassava products, seek emergency medical care immediately. Keep the flour batch for testing and do not distribute it.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Source roots correctly: Buy from known farmers, specify variety (sweet vs. bitter), and reject roots with rot, soft spots or visible mould. Record the source for traceability.
  • Process within 24â48 hours: Fresh roots deteriorate fast. Peel, wash and grate the same day where possible to prevent post-harvest losses and toxin build-up.
  • Ferment and press adequately: Press grated mash to 40â50% moisture, then ferment 12â72 hours depending on variety. This is your detoxification step â do not skip it.
  • Dry to below 12% moisture: Use raised solar dryers or mechanical dryers. Test with a moisture meter or the salt-and-bottle method. Higher moisture means mould, aflatoxin and short shelf life.
  • Mill and sieve to specification: Target 250â500 Âµm particle size for retail flour. Sieve out coarse particles and re-mill. Consistent particle size commands premium prices.
  • Test every batch for HCN: Use a picrate paper test kit or send samples to a certified lab. Keep a batch record. Do not sell untested flour.
  • Package in food-grade, sealed material: Use laminated or high-density polyethylene bags with proper sealing. Label with production date, batch number and best-before date.
  • Track your unit economics: Know your cost per kilogram (roots + labour + utilities + packaging) and your selling price. Recalculate monthly. Target a gross margin above 35%.
