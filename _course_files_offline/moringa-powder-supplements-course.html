Treat this as your working desk reference: print it, laminate the checklist, and use the tables when building loan applications, grant proposals or investor decks.

  • Aflatoxin: Groundnuts, maize and other staples can carry aflatoxin above safe limits. Test every lot before sale or processing. Do not blend contaminated grain into clean grain â this spreads the risk and is illegal in most markets.
  • Moisture: Store grains at or below safe moisture (typically 12â13% for maize, 8â9% for groundnuts). Damp grain creates mould, mycotoxins and total stock loss.
  • Pesticides: Never store crop chemicals near food, feed or seed. Use labelled containers only, wear gloves and a mask, and respect pre-harvest intervals. Re-entry intervals protect your workers.
  • Fumigation: Only trained, licensed operators should fumigate stores. Fumigants are lethal in enclosed spaces.
  • Food contact surfaces: Use food-grade lubricants and cleanable surfaces. Rust and flaking paint contaminate product and fail buyer audits.
  • Traceability: Keep batch records. If a recall happens, batch-level records are the difference between a contained incident and a business-ending one.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Validate demand before you build. Get written interest from at least three buyers, with volumes and price bands, before ordering equipment.
  2. Cost your working capital cycle. Add 60â90 days of grain purchase, wages and packaging to your capex figure. Underfunded working capital kills more agribusinesses than bad machinery.
  3. Secure raw material supply. Contract with aggregators or farmer groups. Spot buying in harvest season is cheap but unreliable in the lean season.
  4. Test quality at intake. Moisture meter and aflatoxin test kits at the gate. Reject or price-discount substandard lots immediately.
  5. Register and certify. Confirm your product meets national standards (for example KEBS, SON, NAFDAC, Ghana Standards Authority) and obtain the required food safety certification.
  6. Price with a sensitivity model. Run your numbers at Â±20% on input cost and selling price. If the business only works in the best case, it does not work.
  7. Build route density. Combine deliveries to the same market or town. Distribution cost per tonne falls sharply with fuller vehicles.
  8. Keep records from day one. Production, sales, losses and cash. Lenders and investors fund businesses that can show numbers, not stories.
