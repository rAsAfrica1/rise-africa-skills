Module 5

Harvest Discipline, Post-Harvest & Value Addition

Lesson 1

Harvest Indicators by Crop

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Judge the correct harvest stage for tomato, leafy greens, onion, cabbage, carrot
  • Decide between harvest-early-for-price and harvest-late-for-yield
  • Plan harvest against the buyer's schedule

Tomato

Harvest at mature green for transport to distant markets (they ripen in transit and hold 10–14 days). Harvest at pink to light red for local market (sells within 2–3 days). Harvest fully red only for immediate sale or processing.

Leafy greens (kale, spinach, chard)

Harvest outer leaves when they are 15–25 cm long, before they become tough. Cut-and-come-again style; leave the growing tip. Best harvested in the cool of the morning.

Onion

Harvest when 50–75% of the tops have fallen over and the necks are soft. Pull, cure in the field for 2–3 weeks (or in a ventilated shed) until the outer skin is papery. Curing is essential for storage.

Cabbage

Harvest when the head is firm and full size, before it splits. Cut with a sharp knife at the base of the stem. For a second small crop, leave the stem and cut a cross in the top — some varieties produce small secondary heads.

Carrot

Harvest when roots are 2–3 cm diameter at the top. Earlier is sweeter and more tender. Later is bigger but woodier. Water before harvest to make lifting easier. Pull, twist off the tops (leaving 2 cm), wash and pack.

Lesson 2

Harvesting Without Damage

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Handle produce so it arrives at market undamaged
  • Use the right tools and containers
  • Harvest at the coolest time of day

Every bruise, cut or skin break is where rot starts. Five minutes of careless handling costs a week of shelf life and up to 30% of the crop's market value.

Rules

  • Harvest in the cool of the day — early morning is best. Produce harvested hot respires faster and wilts sooner.
  • Use sharp tools. A clean cut is less damaging than a pull or a tear.
  • Leave the right amount of stalk. Tomato: 5 mm stalk attached, or no stalk — but not a torn stem. Onion: leave 2–3 cm of neck. Carrot: leave 2 cm of leaf base.
  • Use shallow, lined crates. Never dump produce into a bucket or a sack. A tomato dropped 10 cm has invisible bruising that turns to rot in 2 days.
  • Do not overfill. Layers no more than 20 cm deep for tomato, 15 cm for leafy greens.
  • Keep produce in the shade between harvest and market. A 30-minute exposure to direct sun at 35 °C is worth a day of shelf life.
Lesson 3

Grading, Packing and Transport

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Grade into premium, standard, processing
  • Pack to protect produce, not to hide it
  • Choose transport that protects the crop

Grading

GradeCriteriaPrice
PremiumUniform size, no blemish, correct colour+20–40%

RejectSplit, rotten, insect damageNot saleable

Packing

Use shallow crates or boxes with ventilation. Line with paper or a clean cloth for tomato. For leafy greens, pack upright if possible — horizontal packing crushes the leaves. Never use a plastic sack for fresh produce — it sweats and rots.

Transport

  • Load carefully — nothing on top of tomato
  • Transport in the cool of the day or at night
  • Cover with a shade cloth if the vehicle is open
  • A 30-minute drive with the load in the sun costs a week of shelf life
Lesson 4

Cooling and Storage

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand why cooling doubles shelf life
  • Use evaporative cooling where refrigeration is unavailable
  • Decide when cold storage is worth the investment

Every 10 °C drop in temperature roughly doubles the shelf life of fresh produce. A tomato at 30 °C lasts 3 days. At 15 °C it lasts 7–10 days. At 10 °C it lasts 2 weeks. Cooling is not a luxury — it is the difference between a glut that sells at $0.20/kg and a planned supply that sells at $0.60/kg.

Zero-cost cooling

  • Shade. Any shade over the harvest area reduces temperature by 5–10 °C. A simple roof of grass thatch or shade cloth costs almost nothing.
  • Evaporative cooling. A wet cloth or a porous clay pot (pot-in-pot) cools its contents by 5–10 °C below air temperature in dry climates. Effective, cheap, traditional.
  • Night harvest. Produce picked at 5 a.m. is 10–15 °C cooler than produce picked at noon.

Powered cooling

  • Cold room — best, most expensive. $3,000–15,000+. Pays back if you handle 500+ kg/day of premium crops (tomato, leafy greens, herbs) year-round.
  • Chest freezer for processing — for tomato paste, sauce, or freezing. Not for fresh-market tomato.
  • Refrigerated transport — worth hiring for premium loads to distant markets, if the price differential covers the cost.
Lesson 5

Value Addition — Sauce, Dried, Juice

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Turn gluts into higher-value products
  • Compare tomato paste, dried tomato, and juice on cost and margin
  • Understand the food-safety requirements for each

Tomato paste / sauce

The most common African value-addition for glut tomato. Process: wash, blanch, crush, cook down (4–6 hours), sieve, add salt/oil/spices, jar and seal, pasteurise.

Yield: 5 kg fresh tomato → 1 kg paste. Cost: labour and fuel. Shelf life: 6–12 months if properly sealed. Price: 3–8x fresh tomato per kg. Requires food-safety compliance: clean water, sterile jars, correct pasteurisation.

Dried tomato

Sun-dried or solar-dried tomato halves. Process: wash, halve, salt lightly, dry in the sun (3–5 days) or a solar dryer (1–2 days) to 10–12% moisture.

Yield: 8 kg fresh → 1 kg dried. Shelf life: 6–12 months if dry and stored in airtight containers. Price: 5–10x fresh tomato per kg. Higher value than paste, but requires more careful drying and storage.

Tomato juice

Fresh juice, sold locally, has a short shelf life (1–3 days refrigerated). Higher margin than fresh fruit but limited volume. Pasteurised bottled juice has a longer shelf life but requires a bottling line and health certification.

Leafy green value addition

Dried kale or spinach powder is a growing health-food product. Wash, blanch, dry completely (10% moisture), grind, pack in airtight containers. Sells at 8–15x fresh greens price to health shops and urban supermarkets.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. For transport to a distant market, at what stage should tomato be harvested?

Correct: mature green. Ripens in transit and holds 10–14 days.

2. Why is harvesting in the early morning best?

Correct: cooler produce lasts longer. Every 10 °C drop roughly doubles shelf life.

3. What should you do with a slightly blemished tomato?

Correct: processing grade. Good enough for paste, sauce, drying — just not for the fresh retail market.

4. How many kg of fresh tomato make 1 kg of tomato paste?

Correct: 5. The 5:1 ratio makes tomato paste a real margin product from glut fruit.

5. What is the value multiplier of dried kale or spinach powder?

Correct: 8–15x. Dried greens powder commands a premium in the health-food market.

Module 5 capstone

Plan your post-harvest chain for the next tomato and leafy-green cycle. Include: (1) your harvest stage and time of day; (2) your crates, tools and shade structure; (3) your grading criteria; (4) your buyer for each grade; (5) if a glut is expected, what value-added product you will make and where you will sell it.
