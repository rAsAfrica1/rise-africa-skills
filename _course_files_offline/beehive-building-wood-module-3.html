Module 3

The Langstroth Hive — Boxes, Supers and Frames

The Langstroth is the most widely used hive in the world. Every dimension is standard, every part is interchangeable, and the whole hive is designed around the bee space you met in Module 1. This module is the full build.

Module 3 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Cut and assemble a Langstroth brood box to bee-space tolerance
  • Build a super box that fits and stacks correctly
  • Cut and assemble a frame that lifts out cleanly
  • Install foundation and manage spacing

Lesson 3.1

The brood box — the hive's foundation

The brood box is where the queen lays. It is the largest box in the hive, tall enough for brood comb (240 mm) and wide enough for 8 or 10 frames. Standard dimensions for a 10-frame Langstroth:

DimensionValue
External length505 mm
External width (depth front-to-back)412 mm
Height240 mm
Timber thickness20–22 mm
Frame rest (rabbet) width11 mm
Frame rest depth18 mm
Bottom of frame rest to box top~10 mm — this is bee space

The rabbet cut — the most important detail

The top of the long sides has a rabbet (a step cut into the inside top edge) where the frames hang. The frame top bar sits on this rabbet and the frame's top surface ends up flush with the top edge of the box.

Between the top of the box and the inner cover above it, you have exactly 8 mm of bee space. Too much and the bees build burr comb in the gap. Too little and the inner cover propolises shut.

Corner joints

  • Box joint (finger joint): strongest, water-resistant, works well but is slow to cut. Best for hives in wet climates.
  • Rabbeted butt joint: fast, strong enough if glued and screwed, most common commercial joint.
  • Dado and rabbet: middle ground.

Glue every joint with waterproof wood glue (exterior grade, not interior). Screw or nail with galvanised screws or nails. Do not rely on nails alone.

Assembly steps

  1. Cut all four sides to length with 45° ends — no, actually cut square and use rabbet or box joint.
  2. Cut the rabbet along the top inside edge of both long sides.
  3. Dry-fit the box. Check that diagonals are equal.
  4. Glue and screw. Use at least 3 fixings per corner joint.
  5. Check for square again. Diagonals must be equal to within 2 mm.
  6. Fill any gaps with waterproof filler or glue-and-sawdust mix.
  7. Sand only the frame rest area and the exterior. Do not sand the inside more than necessary.
Square matters more than perfect finish. A hive 3 mm out of square will not stack. A hive with a rough interior finish is fine — bees propolise it. Square first, pretty second.

Lesson 3.2

The super — the honey box

A super is a box that sits above the brood box. It is shorter (168 mm) so that the honey comb is easier to uncap and lighter to lift. Dimensions match the brood box exactly — length, width, timber thickness, rabbet — only the height changes.

Dimensions (10-frame Langstroth super)

DimensionValue
External length505 mm
External width412 mm
Height168 mm
Timber thickness20–22 mm
Rabbet dimensionsIdentical to brood box

Because the super and brood box share the same footprint, any super fits on any brood box. This is the whole point of standardisation.

Stacking discipline

  • Supers must be stacked with the rabbet facing up. Every box.
  • Do not mix 8-frame and 10-frame boxes in the same hive stack.
  • Mark each box with a number and the year built. Helps track which boxes are being used and for how long.

Lesson 3.3

Frames — where the bee space is precise

A Langstroth frame is a rectangle of four parts: top bar, bottom bar, and two side bars. It hangs from the rabbet and holds either a wax foundation sheet or a small starter strip.

Dimensions (standard frame)

PartLengthWidthThickness
Top bar483 mm25 mm19 mm
Bottom bar448 mm19 mm10 mm
Side bar (brood)232 mm25 mm10 mm
Side bar (super)156 mm25 mm10 mm

The top bar

The top bar has extended ends that rest on the rabbet. Between the extended end and the main body of the bar, a small notch locates the frame side-to-side. In a 10-frame box, 10 frames are spaced 35 mm apart centre-to-centre.

There is a 8 mm gap between the outermost frame and the box wall. This is bee space. It prevents propolisation and lets the frame lift out cleanly.

Side bar

Two side bars per frame. The brood side bar is longer (232 mm) than the super side bar (156 mm) because the brood box is taller. The bottom end of the side bar has a notch to accept the bottom bar.

Assembly

  1. Glue and nail (two nails per joint, or staple) the top bar to both side bars.
  2. Slide the bottom bar into place. Glue and nail.
  3. Check for square. A frame that is not square will not hang straight.
  4. Optional: drill and fit horizontal wire to support the foundation in the brood frame.

Foundation

Foundation is a thin sheet of beeswax (or wax-coated plastic) embossed with the base of worker cells. It gives the bees a starting pattern so they build straight, uniform comb. Without foundation, bees build free comb, which is often crooked and cross-combed.

  • Wax foundation: pure beeswax sheet. Best quality, most expensive, moths eat it if stored poorly.
  • Plastic foundation: cheaper, longer lasting, not always accepted by African bees in dry conditions.
  • Starter strips: a 20 mm strip of wax at the top of each frame. Bees draw comb downward. Works well for African bees which build fast.

For African conditions, starter strips are often the practical choice. They cost less, African bees draw comb rapidly, and the comb is genuinely theirs, not stuck to a full sheet.

Lesson 3.4

The floor, inner cover and roof

Floor

A simple solid floor: one board 560 × 412 × 20 mm, on which the brood box sits. The floor should extend 20–30 mm beyond the brood box at the front to create an alighting board.

Cut a notch in the floor for the entrance: 20 mm deep, 300–400 mm wide, running the width of the entrance wall. Some beekeepers make the entrance in a separate removable entrance block so that it can be reduced in cold weather or during robbing season.

Inner cover

A board 505 × 412 × 10 mm that sits on top of the top super. It stops the bees propolising the outer roof to the box. It should have a small bee-space gap around the edges.

Some inner covers have a small notch or hole in the centre for ventilation. This helps in hot climates. In very hot conditions, an inner cover with a screened ventilation hole is standard.

Roof

The outer roof protects the whole hive from rain. It should overhang the box by 50–80 mm on all sides.

  • Roof top: 560 × 460 × 12 mm board.
  • Roof rim: four sides 540 mm long, 80 mm tall, 20 mm thick, forming a box under the roof top.
  • Metal covering: galvanised sheet on top of the roof, wrapped down the edges. Non-optional in most of Africa; without it, the roof rots within 2 years.

Some beekeepers add an insulated ceiling in the roof (a 25 mm foam board between the rim and the top). This is worth it in hot or cold climates. In a temperate climate, a plain wooden roof is enough.

Lesson 3.5

Finishing, painting and hardening

Painting

  • Exterior only. Never paint the inside of the box or the frame rest. Bees propolise these and paint is not food-safe.
  • Use exterior-grade acrylic or oil-based paint. Not lead-based. Not treated timber stain inside.
  • Two coats. First coat thinned 20% to soak in; second coat full strength.
  • Paint the roof white or light colour. Reflects heat. Reduces internal temperature in hot climates.
  • Paint the box exterior a bright colour. Helps bees find home. Also helps you identify your hive from a distance.

Hardening the roof

Galvanised sheet metal on the roof is the standard. Attach with roofing nails or staples, wrapped down the sides and under the rim. Fold the corners carefully so water cannot enter.

The stand

The hive should sit on a stand at least 300 mm above the ground. The stand keeps termites out, keeps the hive dry, and reduces ant and rodent access. A simple stand of four treated timber legs with a wooden platform is enough. Metal stands (angle iron or concrete) are more durable but more expensive.

The four rules of a good Langstroth: built to bee space (35 mm frame spacing, 8 mm clearances); square enough to stack; painted only on the exterior; on a stand 300 mm above ground. Miss any and the hive will underperform.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. What is the standard brood box height for a Langstroth hive?

Correct: 240 mm. Tall enough for brood comb. The super is 168 mm.

2. What is the rabbet on a Langstroth box?

Correct: frame rest. The top bar sits on the rabbet, and the space above it is 8 mm bee space.

3. What is the frame-to-frame spacing in a 10-frame Langstroth?

Correct: 35 mm. Centre-to-centre. This plus 8 mm at the walls is the bee space standard.

4. Why paint the roof white or light colour?

Correct: reflects heat. A dark roof in Africa's sun bakes the hive. Light colour keeps the interior cooler.

5. Why must the hive sit on a stand at least 300 mm above the ground?

Correct: pest and damp control. Ground contact invites termites, ants and rot. A stand is the cheapest protection available.
