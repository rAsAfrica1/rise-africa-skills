Module 6

Method 5 — Fermentation Chambers

Fermentation is not spoilage. It is controlled microbial action that preserves food, improves nutrition, and creates new flavours. A fermentation chamber is a controlled environment for that process.

  • Explain what fermentation does and why it preserves food
  • Distinguish the four main fermentation types
  • Design a fermentation chamber with correct temperature, humidity and airlock
  • Choose the right vessel for the product

Lesson 6.1

What fermentation actually is

Fermentation is the controlled growth of microorganisms — bacteria, yeasts, or moulds — on or in food. These microorganisms convert sugars and starches into acids, alcohols, or gases. The end products:

  • Lower the pH (acidic environment, hostile to spoilage bacteria)
  • Produce alcohol (in some ferments, itself a preservative at high enough concentration)
  • Produce CO₂ (creates anaerobic conditions, also hostile to many spoilage organisms)
  • Modify flavour and texture (the reason we ferment in the first place)

Fermentation is not the same as spoilage. Spoilage is uncontrolled microbial growth that damages the food. Fermentation is controlled microbial growth that transforms the food — with predictable and desirable results.

The fermentation rule

Every fermentation works on the same principle: create conditions where the desired microorganisms thrive and the undesired ones cannot. Salt, acid, temperature, and oxygen exclusion are the tools.

The fermentation rule: controlled conditions. If you do not control salt, temperature, and air exclusion, you do not get fermentation — you get spoilage.

The three key variables

  1. Temperature. Each fermentation has an optimum. Yoghurt at 42 °C. Sauerkraut at 18–22 °C. Beer at 15–20 °C.
  2. Salt concentration. 2–3% for vegetable fermentation. Higher (10–20%) for fish and meat ferments.
  3. Oxygen exposure. Some ferments need air (vinegar, kombucha). Others need exclusion (sauerkraut, kimchi).

Room design

A versatile fermentation room can hold several types at once, but each type needs its own zone:

  • Warm zone (35–45 °C). For yoghurt. A cabinet or a small insulated box with a low-power heater and thermostat.
  • Cool zone (15–22 °C). For sauerkraut, kimchi, beer, wine. A shaded, well-ventilated room or a small cool cabinet.
  • Ageing zone (10–15 °C). For cheese and long-aged ferments. A small cool room with humidity control.

A small producer starting out usually combines zones in one room using cabinets, insulated boxes, and simple controls. Only as volume grows does a dedicated chamber make sense.

The yoghurt chamber

Simplest fermentation chamber. A small insulated box (foam-lined) with a 40 W bulb and a thermostat set to 42 °C. Holds 20–40 L of yoghurt at a time. Cost: $100–300.

The sauerkraut chamber

Just a cool, shaded room with a stable temperature of 18–22 °C. The fermentation vessel does the real work — an airtight crock with an airlock. Chamber cost: minimal.

The vinegar chamber

A warm, well-ventilated room or cabinet at 25–30 °C. The vessel must allow air contact. Screening keeps fruit flies out. Cost: minimal.

The airlock

An airlock is a small device that lets gas escape the fermentation vessel but stops air entering. It is essential for anaerobic fermentation (sauerkraut, beer, wine). Without an airlock:

  • Air enters, oxygen-loving moulds and yeasts grow
  • The surface becomes mouldy
  • The batch is ruined

Airlock types:

  • Bubbler airlock — S-shaped or three-piece plastic. Cheap ($2–5), effective.
  • Blow-off tube — a tube from the vessel into a jar of water. Simple for large vessels.
  • Crock with water seal — traditional. The crock lid has a channel filled with water that lets gas escape but not air in.

Weights and submersion

For vegetable ferments, the vegetable must stay under the brine. Any vegetable exposed to air grows mould.

  • Glass weight — a clean glass disc or a filled jar on top.
  • Zip-lock bag of brine — food-grade bag filled with 2% brine, placed on top.
  • Traditional stone — smooth, clean, non-porous. Boil before first use.

Lesson 6.5

Contamination and failure

Signs of a healthy fermentation

  • Bubbles rising (CO₂ production)
  • Sour, tangy smell (lactic acid)
  • Vegetable stays under the brine
  • Colour stays bright (for sauerkraut, pale to golden)
  • pH dropping steadily toward 3.5–4.0

Signs of a failed fermentation

  • Foul, putrid, or rotten smell. Discard. Not safe.
  • Fuzzy mould (blue, green, or black). Discard, or remove the surface and check underneath carefully. Discard if unsure.
  • Slimy or ropy texture. Wrong bacteria. Discard.
  • Pink or red discolouration. Often indicates unwanted bacteria. Discard.
  • pH still above 4.6 after expected time. Discard.

White film (kham yeast)

A thin white film on the surface is usually kham yeast — harmless. Skim it off. If the smell is normal, continue. But if the smell is bad or the film is fuzzy, discard.

Prevention

  • Clean everything before use (vessels, weights, hands).
  • Use correct salt concentration.
  • Keep vegetables submerged.
  • Maintain correct temperature.
  • Use an airlock for anaerobic ferments.
  • Do not open the vessel repeatedly during fermentation.
The fermentation rule: if it smells bad, throw it out. Trust your nose. A healthy fermentation smells sour and appetising. A failed one smells foul and rotten. There is no in-between. Do not eat doubtful fermented food.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. What is fermentation?

Correct: controlled microbial growth. Different from spoilage, which is uncontrolled and undesirable.

2. What is the correct temperature for yoghurt fermentation?

Correct: 40–43 °C. Warm chamber for 4–8 hours. Too cool and it stalls; too warm kills the culture.

3. Why must vegetable ferments be submerged under the brine?

Correct: exposure to air causes mould. The brine creates the anaerobic environment the lactic acid bacteria need.

4. What is an airlock and why is it used?

Correct: gas out, no air in. Essential for sauerkraut, kimchi, beer, wine.

5. What should you do if a fermented batch smells foul or putrid?

Correct: discard. A bad smell is the body's warning system. Never eat doubtful fermented food.

Practical assignment — Module 6

Design a fermentation facility and process for one product.

  1. Which fermentation are you doing? (Sauerkraut, yoghurt, cheese, vinegar, fermented fish, other.)
  2. Weekly production volume and the fermentation time for the product.
  3. Chamber design — temperature, humidity, ventilation, dimensions.
  4. Vessel choice and airlock design (if anaerobic).
  5. Salt, temperature, and pH targets, with the arithmetic.
  6. Process steps from raw to finished, with times and temperatures.
  7. Contamination control plan — cleaning, PPE, sourcing.
  8. Failure recognition and batch disposal plan.
  9. Two supplier quotations for vessels, airlocks and a pH meter.
  10. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest contamination risk in your operation and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 5 and 10. pH and salt control are the safety foundation; the paragraph is where you commit to a discipline.
