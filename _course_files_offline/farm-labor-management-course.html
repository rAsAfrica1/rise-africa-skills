Practical Reference

Farm labour is usually the single largest controllable cost on a smallholder or emerging commercial farm in Africa â often 40â60% of total operating expenditure. Treat it the way you treat seed or fertiliser: as an input you buy, measure and optimise for return. The reference material below gives you the benchmarks, ratios and paperwork you need to run labour as a business function rather than a daily cash drain.

Three principles drive profitability in labour management. First, separate family labour from hired labour in your books â unpaid family labour hides true cost and makes pricing decisions unreliable. Second, pay for output, not presence, wherever the task can be measured (harvesting, weeding by row, bagging). Third, invest in supervision before you invest in headcount; one trained supervisor managing eight workers typically outperforms twelve unsupervised workers at the same total wage bill.

Agrochemical exposure is a serious occupational hazard for farm workers across Africa. Before any worker applies pesticides, herbicides or fumigants: (1) provide and enforce the use of certified personal protective equipment â gloves, face mask or respirator, goggles, long sleeves, boots; (2) never allow application within the restricted-entry interval stated on the label; (3) store all chemicals in a locked, ventilated, labelled store away from food, feed and water sources; (4) train every applicator on the WHO hazard class of each product and the correct dilution rate; (5) keep a written record of every application â date, product, rate, applicator name; (6) ensure workers can wash immediately after handling and know the location of the nearest clinic and the product's antidote information. Pesticide poisoning symptoms â headache, blurred vision, vomiting, excessive sweating â require immediate removal from the area and urgent medical attention. Never send a worker back into a treated field to "finish the job" if they report feeling unwell.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Write a one-page job description for every role â permanent, seasonal and casual â stating the task, the measurable output, the daily rate and the reporting line.
  2. Keep a signed attendance and output register (paper or mobile app) for every worker, every day. This is your evidence for wage disputes and your data for productivity analysis.
  3. Set a labour budget as a percentage of expected revenue before the season starts â typically 25â40% for horticulture, 15â25% for grain â and review it monthly.
  4. Train supervisors separately from workers on planning, record-keeping, conflict resolution and basic first aid. One good supervisor protects your entire wage bill.
  5. Pay on time, every time. Late wages are the number-one cause of labour walkouts at harvest â the exact moment you cannot afford them.
  6. Comply with statutory obligations â minimum wage, NSSF/social security, workers' compensation and casual labour conversion rules â and keep proof of payment.
  7. Provide clean drinking water, shade and a basic first-aid kit at every work site. This is a legal obligation in most African jurisdictions and directly reduces absenteeism.
  8. Review labour cost per unit of output (per tonne, per crate, per litre) at the end of each cycle and set a target reduction for the next season.
