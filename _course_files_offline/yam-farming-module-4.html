Lesson 1

Why Staking Is Not Optional

In this lesson
  • Understand why staking increases yield 30–60%
  • See the leaf area and light interception difference
  • Understand vine behaviour

Yam is a climbing vine. Unstaked vines sprawl on the ground, competing with weeds for light, exposing leaves to soil-borne disease, and reducing photosynthesis. A staked yam plant produces 30–60% more tuber weight than an unstaked plant.

Why staking helps

  • More light interception: vines climb to full sun, leaves receive more photosynthesis
  • Less disease: leaves stay off the soil; airflow reduces fungal infection
  • Less pest damage: rodents and insects have less access to the plant base
  • Easier management: easier to weed, spray, and harvest

Types of yam

  • White yam (D. rotundata): vigorous climber, needs 2–3 m tall stakes
  • Water yam (D. alata): very vigorous, needs 3–4 m stakes
  • Yellow yam: moderate climber, 2 m stakes suffice
Lesson 2

Stake Materials and Trellis Systems

In this lesson
  • Choose stake material
  • Build a trellis system that lasts a season
  • Understand the cost-benefit

Stake materials

  • Wooden stakes: traditional. 2–3 m long, 5–8 cm thick. Cost $0.30–1.50 each, or free if harvested from farm bush. Lifespan 1–3 seasons.
  • Bamboo: durable, cheap where available. 3–4 m lengths.
  • Metal stakes: expensive but last many seasons. Not common at smallholder scale.
  • Living stakes: some trees (e.g. Gliricidia, Leucaena) can be planted and used as stakes. Long-term solution but competes for water and nutrients.

Cost per hectare

At 13,000 plants/ha with one stake per plant at $0.50 each = $6,500. That is expensive. Most farmers use one stake for 2–3 plants, or reuse stakes from previous seasons.

Trellis systems

  • Individual stakes: one stake per plant or per 2 plants. Simplest.
  • A-frame trellis: two stakes leaning together with a crossbar. Supports 3–4 plants.
  • Horizontal trellis: horizontal wires between posts, vines trained up strings. Efficient but requires wire.
The rule: the stake is the second-biggest cost after seed yam. Budget it — do not skip it.
Lesson 3

Feeding Yam — Organic and Mineral

In this lesson
  • Apply the right fertiliser at the right stage
  • Understand yam's potassium need
  • Use organic matter strategically

Base fertiliser (at planting)

Incorporate 10–20 t/ha of well-rotted manure or compost into the mound or ridge before planting. This is the single most important fertiliser input.

Mineral fertiliser

StageProductRate
At plantingNPK 15-15-15 or DAP200–300 kg/ha in the mound
6–8 weeks (vine growth)Urea or CAN80–100 kg/ha
12–16 weeks (tuber formation)SOP or MOP (potassium)100–150 kg/ha
20+ weeks (tuber bulking)Potassium-rich top-up50–80 kg/ha if leaves pale

Why potassium matters

Yam tubers are 20–25% starch. Potassium drives starch accumulation. A potassium-deficient yam crop produces small tubers with low dry matter. Potassium is the most important mineral for yam after the base organic matter.

Organic alternatives

  • Wood ash: 2–4 t/ha, high in potassium
  • Poultry manure: 3–5 t/ha, good nutrient balance
  • Compost: 10–20 t/ha, base of the fertility programme
Lesson 4

Water Needs and the Dry Season

In this lesson
  • Understand yam water needs
  • Plan for the dry season at the end of the cycle
  • Decide if irrigation is worthwhile

Water needs

Yam needs 1,000–1,500 mm of water over the cycle. The two critical stages are:

  • Tuber initiation (3–4 months after planting): water stress now reduces the number of tubers formed
  • Tuber bulking (5–8 months): water stress now reduces tuber size and quality

Dry season at the end

Yam is normally harvested at the start of the dry season. The plant senses the dry season and starts to senesce. This is normal — the vine yellows, dies back, and the tuber matures. Do not irrigate to keep the vine green; you will delay maturity.

Irrigation

Supplementary irrigation during the growing season is rarely economic for yam at smallholder scale. It pays back only on high-value seed yam production or in areas with marginal rainfall where the crop would otherwise fail. Drip irrigation at 40–60% water saving is the most efficient option.

Lesson 5

Weed Control and the Critical Window

In this lesson
  • Time the critical weedings
  • Weed without damaging shallow roots
  • Use mulch to reduce labour

Critical window: weeks 1–12

Yam is a slow starter. The vine does not close canopy until 2–3 months after planting. Weeds do the most damage in this window. Three weedings are usually needed:

  1. 3–4 weeks after planting: shallow hoe between mounds, hand-pull within
  2. 7–8 weeks: second weeding before vine spreads
  3. 11–12 weeks: final weeding before canopy closure

After 12 weeks, the canopy suppresses most weeds. Hand-pull any large escapes but do not disturb the mound or ridge.

Weeding care

Yam roots and tuber initiation happen in the top 20 cm. Deep hoeing damages the crop. A shallow scratch or hand-pull is enough.

Mulch

A 5–10 cm mulch of dry grass over the mound reduces weeds by 50%, conserves moisture, and keeps soil cooler. Excellent in dry or weedy fields.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. How much does staking increase yam yield?

Correct: 30–60%. Yam is a climbing vine — staking is essential.

2. What is the most important mineral for yam after organic matter?

Correct: potassium. Drives starch accumulation in the tuber.

3. When are the critical water stages for yam?

Correct: initiation and bulking. Stress now reduces tuber number and size.

4. When do the three weedings happen?

Correct: 3–4, 7–8, 11–12 weeks. Three weedings before canopy closure.

5. Why not irrigate to keep the vine green at the end of the season?

Correct: delays maturity. Yam naturally senesces at the start of the dry season.
