3. Seedling and Nursery Management

Every vegetable starts as a seed. Getting that seed to become a strong, healthy seedling is the difference between a great harvest and a wasted season. This module teaches the exact techniques used by commercial nurseries to achieve 90–95% seedling survival.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Seed quality and sourcing

Cheap seed is the most expensive mistake in vegetable farming. Poor seed germinates at 40–60%. Good seed germinates at 85–98%. The difference in yield is 100%+.

Seed sourceGermination rateCost indexBest for
Certified hybrid (Syngenta, Bayer, East-West Seed)90–98%100Commercial crops — tomato, pepper, cabbage
Certified open-pollinated (Seed Co, Klein Karoo)80–90%40–60Onion, carrot, kale, traditional varieties
Local market seed (unpackaged)50–75%15–30Very low-risk crops, pilot plantings
Saved seed from own crop40–80% (variable)FreeOnly for non-hybrid varieties

How to check seed quality:

  1. Read the packet. Look for: variety name, lot number, germination rate, packaging date.
  2. Packaging date. For tomato and pepper, seed older than 2 years loses vigour. Onion seed loses vigour after 1 year.
  3. Physical inspection. Good seed is uniform in size, colour, and shape. No mould, no insect holes, no dust.
  4. Germination test. Put 20 seeds between two damp paper towels. Keep moist in a warm spot. Count how many sprout in the expected time. Under 70%? Buy new seed.
Never use seed from an unlabelled bulk bin. You cannot verify age, variety purity, or germination. The $5 saved on seed costs $500 in lost crop.
Practical exercise 3.1: Buy 3 different tomato varieties. Do a germination test on each. Record germination rates at 7 and 14 days. Choose the winner for your main crop.

Lesson 3.2 — Seedbed preparation and tray filling

There are two ways to start seedlings: direct sowing in a raised seedbed, or sowing in trays. Trays are better for tomatoes, peppers, cabbage, and kale — they produce uniform transplants with less root damage.

Growing media recipe (for 1 cubic metre):

IngredientProportionPurpose
Loamy topsoil (sieved 6 mm)40% (0.4 m³)Structure and minerals
Well-rotted compost40% (0.4 m³)Nutrition and water holding
Coarse river sand15% (0.15 m³)Drainage
Wood ash or agricultural lime5% (0.05 m³)pH balance, calcium

Tray filling steps:

  1. Moisten the media so it clumps slightly in your hand.
  2. Fill each cell loosely, then firm gently with your thumb.
  3. Level off with a straight edge.
  4. Water the filled tray before sowing — this settles the media.
  5. Sow 1–2 seeds per cell at the correct depth (see module 3 lesson 3).
  6. Cover with a thin layer of sieved media (2–3 mm).
  7. Water with a fine mist. Not a stream — that washes seed out.
  8. Place in shade. Cover with plastic or newspaper to hold humidity.
  9. Remove cover as soon as first sprouts appear (usually 4–10 days).
The tray rule: A 128-cell tray at 95% germination gives you 120 transplants for $1.50–$3.00 in seed and media cost. Sold at $0.10 each, that is $12 revenue. The nursery pays for itself 4× over.
Practical exercise 3.2: Mix 100 litres of growing media. Fill 10 trays of 128 cells. Sow tomato seed. Track germination over 10 days.

Lesson 3.3 — Sowing depth, spacing, timing

Sowing depth is the single most common mistake. Too shallow, the seed dries out. Too deep, it uses all its energy reaching the surface and dies before it can photosynthesize.

CropDepthGermination tempDays to sprout
Tomato5 mm20–28°C6–10
Pepper5 mm22–28°C7–14
Cabbage6 mm15–22°C6–10
Kale/rape6 mm15–22°C6–10
Onion6 mm15–22°C10–14
Lettuce3 mm (barely covered)15–22°C5–10
Carrot (direct sow)6 mm15–22°C10–14
Beetroot (direct sow)15 mm15–25°C7–14
Spinach10 mm10–20°C6–12

Timing by season:

  • Hot season: Sow tomato, pepper, okra, amaranth, beans.
  • Cool dry season: Sow cabbage, kale, carrot, beetroot, lettuce, spinach, onion.
  • Rainy season: Sow almost everything, but watch for fungal disease.
Practical exercise 3.3: Plant 3 trays of tomato this week. Follow the depth rule exactly. Count emergence daily. Target 90%+.

Lesson 3.4 — Nursery conditions and hardening off

Seedlings need three things in the nursery: light (filtered, not direct), warmth, and moisture (steady, not wet).

StageLightWaterTemperature
Germination (days 0–7)Dark or dimDamp surface22–28°C
Week 1 after sprout50% shadeOnce daily, morning20–26°C
Weeks 2–450% shadeOnce daily18–28°C
Week 4–6 (pre-hardening)30% shadeEvery other day18–30°C

The 7-day hardening schedule:

DayExposure
Day 12 hours outdoor, morning sun only
Day 24 hours outdoor
Day 36 hours outdoor
Day 48 hours outdoor
Day 5Full day, direct sun
Day 6Overnight outdoor
Day 7Ready to transplant
Never transplant unhardened seedlings. They look fine in the tray and collapse in the field within 48 hours. Seven days of hardening is the difference between 95% survival and 40% survival.
Practical exercise 3.4: Take 20 seedlings and harden them with the 7-day schedule. Take 20 others straight to the field. Compare survival at 5 days. The difference will convince you forever.

The 5-minute daily rule: Check seedlings daily. Any problem spotted in the first 24 hours is fixable. The same problem at 72 hours usually kills the tray. Speed of response is everything in a nursery.
Practical exercise 3.5: Start a nursery log. Every day, note: temperature, water amount, any visible problems. After 4 weeks, identify which conditions produced the best seedlings.

Lesson 3.6 — Transplanting correctly

Transplanting is the moment of highest stress. Done right, seedlings barely notice. Done wrong, they shock and stall for 2 weeks — losing yield potential forever.

Transplanting steps:

  1. Water the seedlings 2 hours before transplanting. Damp media holds the root ball together.
  2. Transplant in late afternoon or on a cloudy day. Never midday sun.
  3. Water the destination bed first. Wet soil is easier for roots to enter.
  4. Use a small trowel or stick to make a hole slightly larger than the root ball.
  5. Lift the seedling by the leaf, not the stem. Never hold a seedling by its delicate stem.
  6. Place the root ball level with the surface — not buried deeper, not floating above.
  7. Firm the soil gently around the roots, eliminating air pockets.
  8. Water immediately with a soft stream or watering can.
  9. Shade for the first 3 days using shade cloth, palm fronds, or newspapers.
  10. Water every other day for the first week, then return to normal schedule.

Transplant stress signs:

  • Wilting for more than 24 hours = severe stress
  • Yellowing lower leaves = normal 2–3 day adjustment, not a problem
  • Seedling does not grow for 7 days = check root damage
Practical exercise 3.6: Transplant 100 tomato seedlings this week. Follow every step exactly. Track survival at 3, 7, and 14 days. Compare to a control group transplanted at noon without prior watering.
