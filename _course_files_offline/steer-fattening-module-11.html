Module 11

Feed Security: Making, Buying and Storing Your Own Feed

Feed is the biggest cost in a fattening pen and the one most likely to destroy a batch. Module 4 taught you to balance a ration. This module teaches you to actually have that ration, in the yard, at a price you agreed months earlier: buying in bulk and comparing quotes honestly, making silage and treating crop residue, storing feed without losing it to damp, rodents and mould, and budgeting the tonnes and the cash a whole batch will need.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to work out what share of your own cost of gain is feed, and use that number to decide where to spend effort.
  • You will be able to compare feed quotes on delivered cost per kilogram of dry matter instead of price per bag.
  • You will be able to make silage in a pit, a bag or a drum, and judge whether it fermented properly.
  • You will be able to treat low-quality crop residue with urea safely, and say honestly what that treatment can and cannot do.
  • You will be able to store feed so that damp, rodents and mould do not quietly eat your margin.
  • You will be able to build a complete feed budget for one batch: total tonnes needed, storage space required, and the month each payment falls due.

Lesson 11.114 min

Why Feed Security Decides the Margin

In this lesson
  • Work out feed as a share of your own cost of gain
  • Build your own twelve-month feed price curve
  • Name the three levers of feed security and pick yours

By this point in the course you have a ration on paper and a costing sheet that tells you your cost per kilogram of gain. Open that sheet and do one piece of arithmetic: divide your total feed cost by your total cost of gain. On most fattening enterprises that answer is well over half, and often much more. Purchase price decides whether the batch had a chance; feed decides what you do with that chance.

That single ratio is why this module exists. If feed is the majority of your controllable cost, then a ten percent swing in feed price moves your margin far more than a ten percent saving on anything else you buy. And feed price does swing, predictably, every year.

The seasonal price curve

In almost every African production region, grain, bran and roughage are cheapest in the weeks immediately after harvest, when everyone is selling at once and stores are full, and dearest in the last weeks before the next harvest, when stocks are thin. The gap between those two points is not small. It is routinely large enough to be the difference between a profitable batch and a loss.

Do not take that on trust. Build your own curve. Phone the same three suppliers on the same day each month for a year and write down the price of your two main ingredients. Twelve rows in a notebook will tell you more about your business than any figure printed in a course.

What feed insecurity actually does to a batch

It is worth being precise about the damage, because the usual response to a feed price rise is the worst available option.

  • You cut the ration. Gain slows, the days on feed stretch out, and every extra day adds maintenance feed that produces no weight. The batch gets more expensive per kilogram of gain, not less.
  • You switch ingredients suddenly. A rapid change in the concentrate portion risks exactly the acidosis and off-feed problems Module 5 spent six lessons teaching you to avoid. A feed crisis becomes a health crisis.
  • You sell early and unfinished. You take an unfinished-animal price, which in most grading schemes is a meaningfully worse price, and you take it on a day you did not choose.
  • You borrow. Module 10 covered what short-cycle credit does to a margin. Borrowing to buy feed you should have bought at harvest is the most expensive feed you will ever eat.

The three levers of feed security

Every practical answer in this module is one of three things:

  1. Buy ahead. Purchase at or near harvest and store. Costs you storage, storage losses, and cash tied up early.
  2. Grow or make your own. Silage, hay, fodder. Costs you land, labour and a season of planning, and gives you a roughage you control.
  3. Fix the price without taking delivery. A written forward agreement with a mill or a farmer. Costs you nothing to store, but depends entirely on the other party honouring it.

Most working fatteners use all three. A common shape is: roughage grown or made on the farm, the bulk energy feed bought at harvest and stored, and the protein concentrate bought monthly because it is a smaller tonnage and stores less well.

Match your feed plan to your batch calendar

Module 10 asked you to plan intakes backwards from sale windows. Now lay the feed curve underneath that calendar. If your cheap feed month is July and your batches start in January and August, the August batch can be fed almost entirely from feed bought in its first weeks, while the January batch is eating feed bought at the peak unless you stored for it. That is an argument either for storage capacity or for shifting a batch. It is a decision you can only make if both calendars are on the same sheet of paper.

Lesson 11.216 min

Buying in Bulk: Quotes, Contracts and Delivered Cost

In this lesson
  • Convert any quote to delivered cost per kilogram of dry matter
  • Inspect, weigh and reject a delivery properly
  • Write a forward agreement that survives a failed crop

Feed is sold in bags, in tonnes, and in whatever unit the seller finds flattering. Your job is to convert every offer to one honest number before you compare anything: delivered cost per kilogram of dry matter. Anything else will mislead you, and it will mislead you in the seller's favour.

The four adjustments

Take the quoted price and work through these in order.

  1. Get to a per-kilogram price. A bag is not a unit. Weigh a sample bag yourself. Bags described as 50 kg frequently are not, and a bag five percent light makes the price five percent higher than quoted.
  2. Add delivery. Transport per tonne falls sharply with load size. A cheap feed forty kilometres away with a small load can land dearer than a dearer feed next door. Ask for the delivered price, in writing, for your actual order size.
  3. Convert to dry matter. You learned this in Module 4. You are not buying feed, you are buying dry matter. Wet brewers grains at a very low price per tonne can be expensive dry matter once you account for the water you paid to cart.
  4. Divide by the nutrient you are buying it for. For a protein feed, finish on cost per kilogram of crude protein. For an energy feed, compare on cost per kilogram of dry matter and use the energy figure from the label or from your extension service.

What to check before money moves

  • Sample before you commit. Ask for a sample from the actual lot, not a display bag. Judge it by the look, feel, smell and shake test from Module 4.
  • Agree a rejection right. Put in writing that you may reject the load on arrival if it is mouldy, wet, heating, or visibly different from the sample. Without that clause a bad load is your problem.
  • Check the load on arrival, before it is tipped. Once it is in your store, mixed with your stock, you have no case. Open bags at random from different parts of the load, not the ones handed to you.
  • Weigh what arrives. If you cannot weigh the whole load, weigh ten bags at random and multiply. Short delivery is common and is almost never corrected unless you raise it the same day.
  • Insist on a written invoice with the lot identified. If a feed later causes a problem, the paperwork is the only thing that connects the animals to the load.

Forward agreements with small sellers

A forward agreement with a neighbouring farmer for stover, hay or maize can be the cheapest feed security available to you, and it is common for it to be made on a handshake and then to fail. Write it down, one page, both signing: the quantity, the quality description, the price, the delivery or collection date, who pays transport, and what happens if the crop fails. An agreement that does not say what happens when the crop fails is not an agreement.

Prepayment is a risk, not a discount

Suppliers will offer a better price for payment in advance. Sometimes that is real; sometimes it is a supplier funding their own cash problem with your money. Treat any prepayment as an unsecured loan you have made. Only prepay to a supplier you have dealt with before, never prepay your whole feed requirement, and never prepay an amount you could not survive losing.

Lesson 11.317 min

Making Silage for a Fattening Pen

In this lesson
  • Harvest forage at the right dry matter for silage
  • Chop, pack and seal a silo that ferments properly
  • Judge good silage from spoiled, and manage the feed-out face

Silage is preserved wet forage. Green material is chopped, packed tightly enough to exclude air, and sealed. Bacteria already on the crop ferment the sugars into acid, the acid drops the pH, and at a low enough pH nothing else can grow. Done properly it keeps for many months. Done badly it grows mould and is a genuine health risk to the cattle you feed it to.

For a fattening pen silage is attractive for one reason above all: it lets you capture a whole field of forage at its best moment and feed it in the dry season, when the alternative roughage is expensive standing hay or nothing.

Harvest at the right dry matter

This is the decision that most often ruins a pit. Maize or sorghum cut too green is too wet: it seeps, ferments badly and produces sour, unpalatable silage. Cut too late it is too dry to compact, air stays in the pit and it moulds.

For maize, the widely used target is the dough stage, when the grain is firm but a thumbnail still marks it and the milk line has moved roughly a third to halfway down the kernel. Practically, that is commonly around 30 to 35 percent dry matter. If you have no way to measure dry matter, the squeeze test is the field version: squeeze a handful of chopped material hard for thirty seconds and release. If juice runs freely it is too wet. If it falls apart instantly and feels crisp it is too dry. If the ball holds its shape briefly and your hand is damp but not wet, you are close.

Chop length and compaction

  • Chop short. Roughly 10 to 20 mm is the usual target. Short material packs densely; long material bridges and traps air.
  • Fill fast. Aim to fill and seal a pit within a day where you can. Every hour a partly filled pit stands open, the top layer is respiring and heating.
  • Pack hard, in thin layers. Spread in layers no more than about 15 cm deep and drive over each layer repeatedly, or tamp it by foot and by drum if you have no tractor. Compaction is the single biggest determinant of quality and it is almost always the step people cut short.
  • Seal completely. Plastic sheet over the top, overlapping generously at joins, weighted continuously with soil, sand bags or old tyres laid touching each other. A sheet held down at the edges only will lift and breathe.

The fermentation window

Leave it sealed. Fermentation is essentially complete and the pit stable after roughly three weeks, and opening it early to look interrupts exactly the process you need. Silage improves nothing by being inspected.

Judging the result when you open it

Good silage smells pleasantly sharp and slightly sweet, is olive to yellowish green or light brown, and holds together without being slimy. Reject material that is:

  • Mouldy. White, grey, blue, green or pink fuzz. Do not feed it, do not cut the mould off and feed the rest, and do not feed it to pregnant animals under any circumstances.
  • Slimy and foul-smelling, with a rancid or manure-like smell. That is a butyric fermentation, usually from material that was too wet or soil-contaminated.
  • Hot, dark brown or charred-smelling. That is heat damage from poor compaction. The protein in it is largely unavailable even where it looks intact.

Managing the feed-out face

Once a silo is open, air is reaching the face every day. Take the whole face down evenly rather than digging holes in it, take enough each day to move the face back visibly, and reseal the top sheet tightly over the unopened part. A face that moves too slowly will heat and spoil faster than you are feeding it. This is a real argument for sizing your silo to your pen rather than building the largest pit you can.

Silage does not replace the concentrate

Be clear about what you have made. Maize silage is principally an energy and bulk feed. It is not a protein feed and it will not on its own carry a finishing steer at the gain figures in this course. It replaces or supplements the roughage portion of the ration you built in Module 4. Rebalance that ration around it; do not simply add silage and hope.

Lesson 11.416 min

Hay, Crop Residue and Treating Low-Quality Roughage

In this lesson
  • Rank your available roughages honestly
  • Store hay and stover without spoilage or fire risk
  • Treat crop residue with urea safely, and know the limits of what it achieves

Most fattening operations in Africa are built on crop residue: maize and sorghum stover, groundnut and bean haulms, rice and wheat straw, sugarcane tops. These are cheap or free, available in quantity, and nutritionally poor. Understanding exactly how poor, and what can and cannot be done about it, keeps you from building a ration on hope.

Rank your roughages honestly

RoughageRough standingPractical role in a fattening ration
Good legume hay or haulms (groundnut, bean, lucerne)Best available roughageGenuinely contributes protein; use it where you have it
Well-made grass hay, cut youngGoodSound roughage base
Maize or sorghum stoverPoorBulk and rumen function; contributes little else
Rice or wheat strawPoorestBulk only; needs treatment and generous supplementation

The two things that decide hay quality are the stage at which it was cut and whether it got rained on after cutting. Grass cut young and dried quickly is a different feed from the same grass cut after seeding and rained on twice. Cut early, turn it to dry it fast, bale or stack it dry, and keep it off the ground and under cover.

Storing hay and stover

  • Stack on pallets, poles or a raised bed of stones. Ground contact wicks moisture into the bottom of a stack and spoils it.
  • Cover the top, and leave the sides able to breathe. A fully sealed stack of not-quite-dry material will heat.
  • Never stack material that is still warm or damp. Heating stacks are a real fire risk and a certain mould risk.
  • Keep it away from the pen. Trampled, fouled roughage is wasted roughage, and feed stores next to cattle attract rodents.

Urea treatment of crop residue

Treating stover or straw with urea raises its crude protein content and improves how much of it cattle can digest and how much they will eat. It is a genuinely useful technique, widely promoted by extension services, and it is also the point in this course where you can most easily poison your own cattle. Read the safety section before you read the method.

The widely used extension recipe is approximately 4 to 5 kg of urea dissolved in roughly 40 to 60 litres of water, sprinkled evenly over 100 kg of dry stover, packed and sealed airtight, and left for about 14 to 21 days — longer in cool weather, shorter in hot. Confirm the rate and the timing with your own extension or veterinary officer before you do it, because recommendations vary with local material and climate.

  1. Chop the stover. Untreated long material will not absorb the solution evenly.
  2. Dissolve the urea completely in the water in a separate container. Undissolved granules create dangerous hot spots.
  3. Spread the stover in a layer, sprinkle the solution evenly, and repeat layer by layer.
  4. Pack tightly and seal airtight under plastic, weighted down. Air escaping means ammonia escaping, and ammonia is the whole point.
  5. Open after the treatment period and air it for a day before feeding. It will smell strongly of ammonia. That smell should fade substantially on airing.

Urea safety, which is not optional

  • Urea is toxic to cattle in excess and kills quickly. Signs include restlessness, muscle tremors, staggering, bloat, frothing and rapid collapse. It is an emergency; call a vet immediately.
  • Never allow cattle access to raw urea, urea bags, or the mixing container. Cattle will eat loose urea. Lock it up as you would a chemical.
  • Never dissolve urea in drinking water or allow the run-off into a trough.
  • Introduce treated material gradually, over roughly two weeks, exactly as you stepped up concentrate in Module 5.
  • Never feed urea or urea-treated material to animals that are not adapted, and never to young calves whose rumens are not developed.
  • Observe the total ration limit. Non-protein nitrogen should stay a small fraction of total ration dry matter — the commonly cited ceiling is around one percent of total dry matter intake — and your Module 4 label reading applies: a purchased concentrate may already contain NPN, and the two add up.

What treatment cannot do

Urea treatment improves poor roughage. It does not convert straw into a finishing feed, it does not add energy, and it does not add minerals. A steer on treated straw still needs the energy concentrate, the protein source and the mineral supplement your Module 4 ration specifies. Treatment buys you a better base, not a complete diet.

Lesson 11.515 min

Storage: Losses, Rodents, Damp and Mycotoxins

In this lesson
  • Store feed dry, off the ground and in dated lots
  • Control rodents without endangering stock or people
  • Recognise mycotoxin risk and get your own national limit in writing

Feed you bought and then lost is the most expensive feed in the business, because you paid for it and got no gain from it. Storage loss is largely invisible: it happens in bags at the back of the store, a few percent at a time, and it never appears on any invoice.

Moisture is the master variable

Almost every storage problem is a moisture problem wearing a different hat. Damp grain heats. Heating grain moulds. Mould produces toxins. Damp attracts insects, and insect activity generates more moisture and more heat. The chain starts in one place.

Grain for storage needs to be genuinely dry. The commonly used working threshold for safely storing maize is around 13 percent moisture or lower, and lower again for long storage in a humid climate. If you cannot measure it, the practical tests are the bite test — a properly dry kernel cracks sharply rather than denting — and the salt-in-a-bottle test with dry salt, where salt sticking to the glass indicates the grain is still giving off moisture. Neither is a substitute for a moisture meter, and a meter is one of the cheapest instruments that will ever pay for itself in this business.

Building a store that works

  • Off the ground. Pallets, poles or a raised floor. Never bags directly on concrete or earth.
  • Off the walls. Leave a gap so air moves and so you can inspect and see rodent activity.
  • Dry roof, no leaks. Check it before the rains, not during.
  • Ventilated but shaded. Air movement without sun on the bags.
  • Stack in named lots with dates. A chalked board with lot, date, supplier and quantity turns your store from a pile into records.
  • First in, first out, without exception. New deliveries go behind, not in front. This one habit removes most of the old-stock spoilage in a typical store.

Rodents and birds

Rodents cost you twice: what they eat, and what they contaminate with urine and droppings, which is usually far more than what they eat. Clear vegetation and rubbish from around the store, seal gaps and vent openings with mesh, keep a cat or run traps consistently, and sweep spilled feed up daily rather than weekly. If you use rodenticide, it goes in covered bait stations only, it is recorded, and it never goes anywhere cattle, children, dogs or poultry can reach.

Mycotoxins

Mycotoxins are poisons produced by moulds growing on feed, principally on grain and grain by-products. Aflatoxin is the one most often discussed in this region and is produced mainly by Aspergillus species growing on crops stressed in the field or stored damp. Others, including fumonisins and the toxins associated with Fusarium, also matter.

What they do in cattle varies with the toxin and the dose, and the effects at realistic contamination levels are more often chronic than dramatic: reduced intake, reduced gain, poor feed conversion, immune suppression, and animals that simply do not perform to the ration you built. In severe contamination, liver damage and death occur. There is a second issue that matters commercially: aflatoxin residues carry into milk, and in some markets into liver, so contaminated feed is a food-safety issue and not only a production one.

Legal maximum levels for aflatoxin in feed differ by country and by feed type. Do not work from a number you read anywhere, including here. Get your own country's limit from your national standards body or veterinary services, in writing, and keep it with your feed records.

Practical mycotoxin control

  1. Buy from suppliers who dry and store properly, and sample every lot.
  2. Reject anything musty-smelling, caked, discoloured, or visibly mouldy on arrival. A cheap contaminated load is not cheap.
  3. Store dry, ventilated, off the ground, and rotate.
  4. Inspect the store weekly with a torch. Open bags from the middle of the stack, not just the top.
  5. If a batch of cattle goes off feed for no reason you can find, suspect the feed and change the lot before you reach for anything else.
  6. Where a lot matters enough, pay for a laboratory test. Your veterinary services or a university department will usually know where.

Measure your own loss rate

Weigh in and weigh out. Record the tonnage that entered the store and the tonnage that left it as feed, and the difference is your loss rate. Smallholder grain storage losses are commonly quoted in ranges that run into double-digit percentages, but the only figure that should inform your decisions is the one from your own store. If it is high, the cheapest tonne of feed you can buy this year is the tonne you stop losing.

The Batch Feed Budget: Tonnes, Timing and Money

In this lesson
  • Calculate total dry matter for a whole batch
  • Convert to as-fed tonnes and check it fits your store
  • Find the month of peak cash need

This lesson turns everything above into one sheet of paper: how many tonnes a batch will eat, how much space that occupies, what it costs, and in which month each payment falls due. Without this sheet, feed buying is reactive, and reactive buying is what the whole module exists to prevent.

Step 1: total dry matter for the batch

Use the method from Module 4 and Module 8.

  1. Take the average live weight across the whole feeding period, not the starting weight. If animals come in at 250 kg and leave at 400 kg, the average is roughly 325 kg.
  2. Estimate daily dry matter intake as a percentage of that average live weight. The range used through this course is roughly 2.0 to 2.5 percent. Take the upper end for the budget: running out is worse than having a margin.
  3. Multiply by the number of animals and by the days on feed.

Step 3: add a wastage and contingency margin

Order more than the calculation says, for three reasons: feeding wastage at the trough, storage loss, and the chance that days on feed run longer than planned. A practical working margin is around ten percent, adjusted by your own measured storage loss from Lesson 5. Write down which margin you used and why, so that next batch you can judge whether it was right.

Step 4: check it fits in your store

Tonnes need cubic metres. Before you order, confirm the store can hold the whole quantity, dry and off the ground, and that you can still walk between stacks to inspect. A quantity that does not fit will end up under a tarpaulin outside, which converts your bulk-buying saving directly into spoilage.

Step 5: put each payment in a month

This is the step that connects the feed budget to the cash-cycle work in Module 1 and the working-capital work in Module 10. Bulk buying moves money forward: you pay in month one for feed eaten in month four. That is the whole point, and it is also the thing that catches people out.

Step 6: write the three decisions down

Close the sheet with three lines, because these are the ones you will forget and then repeat:

  1. What I bought forward, and at what price. Next year this tells you whether buying at harvest actually paid.
  2. What I ran short of, and by how much. Shortfalls are systematic, not random. The same ingredient usually runs short every time.
  3. What I lost in store, in tonnes. From the weigh-in and weigh-out in Lesson 5.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. You are offered maize bran at a price per bag that is clearly lower than your usual supplier's price per tonne. What is the first thing to do?

Bags, tonnes, ex-works and delivered prices are not comparable. Converting both to delivered cost per kilogram of dry matter is the only honest comparison, and it frequently reverses which offer is cheaper.

2. You open a maize silage pit and find grey-green fuzzy growth through part of the top layer. What is the correct action?

Mould means possible mycotoxins, which are not removed by trimming or dilution. Mouldy silage is not fed, and it is never fed to pregnant animals.

3. Why does urea treatment of maize stover require a gradual introduction over about two weeks?

Urea is non-protein nitrogen. Rumen microbes must adapt to handle it, and an unadapted animal given a full ration of treated material can be poisoned quickly and fatally.

4. Your store shows a sharp rise in mould and insect activity in the third month. Which single variable is most likely behind it?

Moisture drives the chain: damp feed heats, heating feed moulds, insects add more moisture and heat. Almost every storage problem traces back to it.

5. Ten steers average 325 kg over the feeding period and eat 2.5 percent of live weight as dry matter for 110 days. Roughly how much dry matter must the batch budget cover, before any wastage margin?

325 kg times 2.5 percent is about 8.1 kg of dry matter per head per day. Multiplied by 10 animals and 110 days that is roughly 8.9 tonnes, before adding any wastage or contingency margin.

Module 11 capstone

Build a complete feed security plan for your next batch on two sheets of paper. Sheet one: your twelve-month price curve for your two main ingredients from three named suppliers, with the month you intend to buy marked and the reason written beside it. Sheet two: the full batch feed budget — total dry matter from your own average weight and days on feed, split by ingredient, converted to as-fed tonnes, with your wastage margin stated, a confirmation that the quantity physically fits in your store off the ground, and a month-by-month cash table with the month of peak cash need circled. Add three lines at the bottom: your country's legal aflatoxin limit for cattle feed obtained in writing from your national standards body or veterinary services, your own measured storage loss rate if you have one, and the phone numbers of the three suppliers you priced.

Price check, always. Before you buy feed, call three suppliers and compare on delivered cost per kilogram of dry matter. Feed prices, availability and quality vary widely between countries and seasons. Urea and rodenticide are chemicals: store them locked away from stock and people, and confirm treatment rates and legal feed contaminant limits with your own veterinary or extension officer before you act.
