Practical Reference

This reference section is written for the operator, the parent, the teacher, and the entrepreneur who intends to turn drone skills into income. Everything below is designed to be printed, pinned to a workshop wall, or opened on a shared phone before a real flight. In African markets, drone work is already a paying trade: medical delivery corridors in Rwanda and Ghana, crop spraying and field mapping in Kenya, Zambia and South Africa, and flood and disaster mapping across Mozambique, Malawi and Zimbabwe. Treat the numbers below as commercial planning inputs, not classroom trivia. Every figure you can measure before takeoff is a figure you do not have to guess at with a client's money on the line.

Table 1: Thrust-to-Weight Ratios and What They Mean Commercially

â  SAFETY WARNING â Read Before Any Flight or Spraying Operation

Drone operations involve lithium batteries, high-speed rotating blades, and â in agricultural spraying â regulated chemicals. These are not classroom risks. They cause real injuries and real poisoning.

  • Batteries: LiPo and Li-ion packs can catch fire if punctured, overcharged, or charged while swollen. Never charge unattended. Never charge near fuel, dry grass, or inside a vehicle in African midday heat. Store at 3.8 V per cell. Dispose of swollen packs at a certified e-waste point â never in household refuse.
  • Propellers: Carbon-fibre blades at full RPM cause deep lacerations. Remove props during bench testing. Never arm motors with people within 10 m. Wear eye protection.
  • Spraying chemicals: Pesticides and herbicides are hazardous to the operator, to livestock, and to water sources. Wear gloves, goggles, long sleeves and a respirator. Observe the manufacturer's pre-harvest interval and buffer zones near rivers, wells and homes. Never spray in wind above 3 m/s. Follow national pesticide registration rules.
  • Airspace: Flying without authorisation is a criminal offence in most African jurisdictions. Confirm with your national civil aviation authority before every commercial job.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh and measure first. Record AUW and total thrust. Calculate TWR. Do not fly below 1.3 for commercial work.
  2. Check the battery log. Note cycle count, cell balance, and any swelling. Retire packs past 200 cycles or showing voltage drift.
  3. Confirm airspace and permission. Contact your civil aviation authority and, where required, the landowner and local authority before takeoff.
  4. Set your mission numbers before leaving the office. Fix altitude, ground sample distance, front overlap (75â80%) and side overlap (65â70%) in advance.
  5. Walk the site. Identify power lines, people, animals, wells and water bodies. Mark hazards on the flight plan.
  6. Check weather on the day. Wind under 8 m/s for mapping, under 3 m/s for spraying. Avoid flying in rain, dust storms or temperatures above 40Â°C.
  7. Brief your observer. One person watches the aircraft and airspace at all times; the pilot watches the screen.
  8. Log everything. Flight time, battery used, area covered, anomalies. This log becomes your proof of professionalism to future clients.
