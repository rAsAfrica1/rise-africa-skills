Module 9 — Contamination Control and Sterile Technique

The practices that decide whether you lose 5 % of bags or 60 %.

Home › Mushroom House — Brick › Module 9

Learning outcomes

  • Identify the main contaminants and their sources.
  • Apply pasteurisation and sterilisation correctly to substrate.
  • Build a clean work area — still air box, laminar flow, or clean room.
  • Use sterile technique during inoculation, harvesting and between crops.
  • Set a cleaning schedule and respond correctly when contamination appears.
  • Record contamination rates and use the records to fix the process.

1. The contaminants

In a mushroom house there are five contaminants you will meet repeatedly. Learn to recognise them by sight and smell.

ContaminantAppearanceSmellWhere it comes from
Green mould (Trichoderma)White turning to forest-green powder; grows fast, spreads fastMusty, sharpUnsterile substrate, airborne spores, damp wood, dirty tools
Cobweb (Cladobotryum)Fine, white, cottony threads spreading over the surface in 24–48 hFaint mushroomHigh humidity, poor airflow, contaminated spawn
Black pin mould (Aspergillus)Dense black powdery patchesSharp, mustyWarm, wet substrate; dirty equipment
Bacterial blotch (Pseudomonas)Brown, wet, sunken spots on capsSour, fishyFree water on caps, high humidity, dirty water
Wet bubble (Mycogone)Deformed mushrooms with white, then brown, weeping blistersRottenContaminated casing, recycled trays, poor hygiene

Green mould has no cure

Once Trichoderma turns green it is releasing spores by the million. Every movement of air spreads them. Do not open the bag to inspect it. Do not compost it near the house. Bag the whole thing, seal it, and remove it from the property. Then clean the shelf and the surrounding area with a sporicidal disinfectant.

2. Sources of contamination — in order of importance

  1. Substrate that was not properly pasteurised. This is the most common cause. Every batch you fail to bring to temperature long enough is a batch waiting to go green.
  2. Airborne spores in the inoculation area. Spores settle on everything — hands, tools, spawn, exposed substrate.
  3. Dirty hands and clothing. Your hands are the most frequent vector between contaminated and clean material.
  4. Contaminated spawn. If the spawn was already contaminated, the whole batch is lost. Buy from a reputable supplier and inspect on arrival.
  5. Water. Misting with unchlorinated or dirty water introduces bacteria and mould spores.
  6. Wood, cardboard or soiled shelving. Porous surfaces harbour spores indefinitely.
  7. Insects and mites. They carry spores from bag to bag and from outside into the house.

3. Pasteurisation vs sterilisation

These are not the same thing, and using the wrong one ruins the substrate.

MethodTemperatureTimeKillsLeavesUse for
Hot water pasteurisation65–75 °C60–90 minMost competitors, not all thermophilesBeneficial bacteria that help the mushroomStraw, straw + bran mixes
Steam pasteurisation60–65 °C6–12 hSlow, thorough kill of competitorsUseful microbesBulk straw for commercial production
Autoclave sterilisation121 °C at 15 psi60–90 minEverything, including sporesNothingSpawn production, supplemented sawdust for shiitake, agar work
Chemical pasteurisationCold water + lime or hypochlorite12–18 hMost competitorsBeneficials partiallyStraw where heat is unavailable
Lime soakpH 12 for 12–18 h—Many moulds and bacteriaMycelium can colonise after pH fallsStraw in warm climates

Pasteurisation heat calculation — straw substrate

You want to pasteurise 100 kg of wet straw in a hot water bath, from 25 °C to 70 °C.
Straw specific heat capacity ≈ 1.6 kJ/kg·K.
Heat required = 100 × 1.6 × (70 − 25) = 7 200 kJ.
Over 60 minutes (3 600 s), that is 2 kW of heating power minimum.

Add 30 % for losses through the tank walls and lid, and for bringing the water itself up to temperature:
Plan for 4–5 kW heating capacity per 100 kg batch.

A 3 kW immersion heater plus 2 hours of warm-up will pasteurise a 100 kg batch. Do not shortcut the hold time — hold at 70 °C for a full hour, not just reach it.

Temperature probe, not your hand

Pasteurisation fails most often because the centre of the substrate never reaches temperature. Use a long probe thermometer pushed into the middle of the batch and wait for the centre to read 70 °C, then start the hour count. If you have not measured it, you have not pasteurised it.

4. The clean work area

Inoculation is the moment of highest contamination risk. Do it in the cleanest place you can make.

LevelSetupCostSuitable for
Kitchen tableNothingFreeHobby only; expect high loss
Still air box (SAB)A clear plastic tote with two arm holes, upside down; working through the holesVery lowSmall-scale spawn and agar work
Clean roomA small, sealed room with HEPA filter, positive pressure, smooth washable surfacesMediumSerious small-scale production
Laminar flow hoodHEPA-filtered laminar airflow across the work surfaceHighCommercial spawn production

Still air box — the cheapest big win

  1. Take a clear 60–80 L plastic storage tote. Cut two 100 mm holes in one side, 200 mm apart, at working height.
  2. Turn it upside down on a clean table so the lid is now the base.
  3. Clean the inside with 70 % isopropyl alcohol and let it air dry.
  4. Place tools and substrate inside, close the lid (now the bottom), and work through the arm holes.
  5. Move slowly. Air currents from fast movement carry spores.
  6. Wipe everything with alcohol before starting. Wipe again between items.

Why slow movement matters

Airborne particles settle at roughly 0.1–0.3 m/s under gravity alone. Any draft from a cough, a quickly moved arm or an open door can lift settled spores and carry them across the work surface.

Rule of thumb: inside a still air box, work at less than half normal speed, and never pass your hands over open substrate without cleaning them first.

5. Sterile technique during inoculation

  1. Shower and put on clean clothes. Tie back hair. No watches, rings or bracelets.
  2. Wash hands to the elbow with soap, then wipe with 70 % alcohol and let dry.
  3. Clean the work surface, the still air box and every tool with 70 % alcohol.
  4. Bring only what you need into the work area. Extra items are extra contamination.
  5. Work from clean to dirty: spawn first, then substrate, then any suspect material last.
  6. Flame-sterilise metal tools between uses; alcohol-wipe everything else.
  7. Open the spawn bag for the shortest possible time. Fold it closed between scoops.
  8. Seal inoculated bags immediately. Do not stack warm bags on top of each other.
  9. Clean up with alcohol as you go — do not leave a contaminated surface for later.
  10. Record the batch: date, substrate, spawn lot, number of bags, any issues.

Test your filters

Fit a simple manometer (or a Magnehelic gauge) across the HEPA filter. When the pressure drop rises by 50 % over the clean value, it is loaded and needs replacement. Without a gauge you will replace filters too early (waste) or too late (contamination).

9. When contamination appears

  1. Do not open it. A closed contaminated bag is a contained problem. An open one is a room-wide problem.
  2. Bag the whole thing — bag, shelf liner and all — in a plastic sack. Seal the sack.
  3. Remove it from the room and from the property. Do not compost on-site.
  4. Clean the area it touched with a sporicidal disinfectant (peracetic acid, hydrogen peroxide, or a 10 % bleach solution).
  5. Check neighbouring bags for early signs. If two or three bags in one row are affected, suspect the substrate batch or the spawn lot.
  6. Record the event: date, batch, position, contaminant, action. Patterns in the record will point to the source.

Cost of contamination — worked example

50-bag batch, 5 kg each = 250 kg wet substrate.
Substrate + spawn + labour cost: 60.
Expected yield: 75 kg mushrooms at 8/kg = 600 gross.

If contamination hits 20 % of bags (10 bags):
Yield loss = 10 × 1.5 kg = 15 kg = 120 lost revenue.
Plus disposal time, plus risk of spread. That single batch lost 20 % of its gross.

Every 1 % reduction in contamination rate is worth about 6 in this example. Cleaning supplies, filters and gloves cost a fraction of that. Contamination control is not an expense — it is the highest-return investment in a mushroom operation.

Assignment 9

  1. Name the five contaminants you are most likely to see. For each, state one specific control.
  2. State whether you will pasteurise or sterilise your substrate, and the exact temperature and hold time.
  3. Calculate the heat energy required to pasteurise your typical batch size. Show the arithmetic.
  4. Describe your clean work area. If you are using a still air box, draw it.
  5. Write out your inoculation procedure in 8 steps or fewer.
  6. Produce a one-page cleaning schedule with daily, weekly, between-crop and monthly tasks.
  7. Specify your filter arrangement: insect screen, pre-filter, HEPA — with grade and service interval for each.
  8. Write your contamination response plan in 5 steps. State where contaminated material is removed to.
  9. Start a contamination log for your next 10 batches. After 10, calculate your contamination rate and identify the single largest cause.
