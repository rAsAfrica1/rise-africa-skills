Module 9 — Method 8: Root Cellars and Cool Storage

Storing crops for months without power — using earth, insulation and ventilation.

Home › Preservation Buildings — 10 Methods › Module 9

Learning outcomes

  • Explain how cool storage preserves — respiration, dormancy and moisture.
  • Choose the right storage temperature and humidity for each crop.
  • Design a root cellar, clamp or pit for your climate and water table.
  • Manage ventilation, insulation and rodent control.
  • Calculate storage volume and crop loss.

1. How cool storage preserves

Root crops, tubers and some fruits stay alive after harvest. They respire — taking in oxygen, releasing CO₂, heat and water. Cool storage slows that respiration without killing the crop.

TemperatureEffect on stored crop
Above 15 °CFast respiration, sprouting, weight loss, rot
8–12 °CStill respiring actively; weeks of storage
2–6 °CSlow respiration, dormancy maintained; months of storage for most roots
0–2 °CVery slow respiration; suitable for cold-hardy crops (cabbage, apples, carrots)
Below 0 °CFreezing damage; destroys texture and often kills the crop

Why cooling multiplies shelf life

Respiration rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature (Q₁₀ ≈ 2).
A potato at 20 °C respires about 8× faster than at 0 °C.

If a crop keeps 2 weeks at 20 °C, it keeps roughly 14–16 weeks at 2 °C — the same physics every refrigeration engineer uses, here achieved with earth and airflow instead of a compressor.

Do not mix crops that dislike each other

Apples, pears and ripening tomatoes release ethylene gas, which triggers sprouting in potatoes and ripening in other crops. Store them in separate compartments or well apart. Never store apples in the same air space as potatoes.

Volume calculation

Target: store 2 tonnes of potatoes for 6 months.
Potato bulk density in a slatted crate: about 650 kg/m³.
Volume of crop = 2 000 / 650 = 3.1 m³.

Add 40 % for shelving, crates, air gaps and access:
Storage volume = 3.1 × 1.4 = 4.3 m³.

With 2.2 m usable height: floor area = 4.3 / 2.2 = 2.0 m².

Add a working aisle: total floor area 4–5 m² for a 2-tonne potato store. A cellar 2.5 × 2.0 × 2.2 m = 11 m³ gross is comfortable.

5. Managing ventilation seasonally

Ventilation is how you tune the cellar through the year. Get it wrong and you either freeze the crop or cook it.

SeasonLow inletHigh outletWhy
Early autumn (crop in, still warm)Open at night onlyOpen at night onlyCool the cellar slowly; do not shock the crop
Late autumnOpen at night, half-closed by dayOpen at night, closed by dayHold 2–6 °C as outside air cools
WinterClosed except on mild daysClosed except on mild daysPrevent freezing — the crop dies below 0 °C
Early springOpen at nightOpen at nightFlush accumulated CO₂ and moisture
Late spring / summerClosed by day, open briefly at night if outside is coolerClosedKeep warm air out; the earth mass holds the coolth

Ventilate at night, close by day

The habit that makes a cellar work: open the vents when outside air is cooler than the cellar, close them when it is warmer. In hot dry climates, a night flush of 2–3 hours is enough. In humid climates, ventilate less — the humidity outside may be higher than inside and you will add moisture instead of removing it.

6. Clamps and pits

Field clamp (potatoes, turnips, beets)

  1. Choose a site on high, well-drained ground, in partial shade.
  2. Spread a 100 mm layer of dry straw on the ground as a base.
  3. Pile the crop in a conical heap — maximum 1.5 m high, 2 m wide.
  4. Cover with 200 mm of clean dry straw.
  5. Cover the straw with 150–200 mm of soil, leaving a small vent at the top.
  6. Cut a drainage channel around the base.
  7. Open the vent on mild days, close it in freezing weather.

Clamps work best in cool, dry climates. In warm or wet climates they fail — the crop rots from the base up.

Pit storage (root crops in warm climates)

  1. Dig a pit 0.8–1.0 m deep, lined with clean sand.
  2. Layer crop and dry sand in alternate 100 mm layers.
  3. Finish with 200 mm of sand on top and a straw covering.
  4. Keep the pit shaded and the sand slightly damp (not wet).
  5. Dig out from one end as needed.

Pits work in warm climates because they hold a stable temperature and moisture and keep the crop out of the sun. They must be above the water table and well-drained.

7. Losses and monitoring

Even a good cellar loses some crop. Plan for it.

Cause of lossTypical % over 6 monthsPrevention
Weight loss from respiration5–8 %Cooler storage, correct humidity
Rot (fungal, bacterial)3–10 %Only store perfect crop; cure properly; keep dry
Sprouting0–5 %Correct temperature for the crop; do not store above 10 °C for potatoes
Rodent damage1–5 %Mesh every opening; keep a tight cellar
Freezing damage0–100 %Manage ventilation in cold weather

Inspect monthly

  • Walk the store with a torch. Remove any rotting crop immediately — one bad root rots the ones around it.
  • Check temperature: a simple max/min thermometer tells you what happened overnight.
  • Check humidity with a hygrometer.
  • Smell the air. A musty smell means poor ventilation. A sweetish smell means rot.
  • Check vents for blockages and rodent damage.

8. Additional features

  • Air lock entry — a small vestibule with two doors prevents warm air entering when you enter the cellar. Worth it in cold climates.
  • Lighting — a battery or 12 V LED lamp is safer than mains in a damp cellar.
  • Humidity control — for dry-storage crops (onions, garlic) you need dry air. Store them in a separate, drier room with more ventilation.
  • Rodent trap — a simple snap trap at the door is worth more than mesh alone.
  • Record book — date, crop, quantity in, quantity out, losses. This tells you whether the cellar is doing its job.

9. When you need refrigeration

Root cellars work in temperate and highland tropical climates, and in dry hot climates with a well-designed evaporative store. They do not work when:

  • Outside temperature stays above 15 °C year-round, with no cool nights.
  • Humidity is very high and there is no dry season.
  • You need to hold crops below 2 °C (some fruits, long-term meat).

In those cases, a small insulated room with a condensing unit — the same equipment as a cold room — is the correct solution. See the Cold Room course for the sizing method.
