Practical Reference

Root cellaring is not a hobby project â it is a margin play. Every kilogram of potato, onion, carrot, or beet that survives six months without spoiling is a kilogram you sell at the top of the price curve instead of dumping at harvest when the market is flooded. In most of sub-Saharan Africa, the gap between harvest-glut prices and lean-season prices for staple roots and tubers runs from 40% to well over 120%. A functioning cellar captures that spread for the cost of a shaded structure, a vent pipe, and disciplined crop handling. Treat the cellar as a business asset with a payback period, not as a storage shed.

The operating logic is simple: cold air is denser than warm air, so a low intake and a high exhaust stack create a continuous siphon as long as the outside air is cooler than the cellar air. No fan, no electricity, no running cost. What kills most African cellars is not the lack of a design â it is poor crop conditioning before storage, no humidity control, and no monitoring routine. Fix those three and the structure does the rest.

Storage Conditions by Crop

Different crops demand different microclimates. Putting onions next to potatoes is a classic beginner error: potatoes release moisture that pushes onions into neck rot, and onions release ethylene that triggers potato sprouting. Build separate bays or at minimum separate bins with a dividing wall.

â  SAFETY WARNING â MOULDS, MYCOTOXINS AND CHEMICAL TREATMENTS

  • Never eat or sell roots with visible mould, black dry rot, or a musty smell. Some storage moulds produce mycotoxins that are not destroyed by cooking. One contaminated sack can seed an entire bin â remove and destroy suspect produce immediately, outside the storage area.
  • Do not use agricultural pesticides, fumigation tablets, or unregistered sprout inhibitors inside a food storage cellar. Phosphine and similar fumigants are lethal in enclosed spaces and are restricted or banned for on-farm root storage in many African jurisdictions. Check with your national plant protection authority before using any chemical.
  • A root cellar is a confined space. Never enter a cellar that has been sealed for weeks without opening the door and ventilating for at least 15 minutes. Low oxygen and accumulated carbon dioxide from decomposing produce can cause rapid unconsciousness. Enter with a second person standing outside.
  • Wear gloves and a dust mask when sorting mouldy stock. Wash hands and forearms thoroughly afterwards. Do not handle stored food if you have open cuts on your hands.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Cure before you store. Potatoes need 10â14 days at 15â18 Â°C and high humidity to heal skin wounds; onions and garlic need 2â3 weeks of drying until necks are papery and tight. Storing uncured produce is the number one cause of cellar failure.
  • Grade hard at the door. Reject any root with cuts, bruising, greening, or disease. One rotting tuber can spread soft rot through an entire bin within two weeks.
  • Separate incompatible crops. Onions and garlic away from potatoes and apples. Ethylene producers and moisture releasers must not share air space.
  • Never store in direct contact with earth or concrete. Use slatted wooden bins raised 100 mm off the floor and 50 mm off the wall to allow air movement and prevent condensation rot.
  • Install a thermometer and hygrometer at produce height â not near the ceiling. Log readings twice weekly. You cannot manage what you do not measure.
  • Ventilate on cold nights only. Open the intake and exhaust when outside air is colder than cellar air. Close during hot days to keep the thermal mass cool.
  • Inspect every bin weekly for the first six weeks, then fortnightly. Remove and destroy any suspect produce the day you find it.
  • Keep a simple ledger. Record kilos in, kilos out, losses, and the price achieved per batch. This is the data that tells you whether to expand the cellar or change your crop mix.
