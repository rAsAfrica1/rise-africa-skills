When one chill room stops being enough — and which of the three growth paths to take.

1. Three ways to grow, and what each one actually solves

Most smallholders hit the same wall: one room chills, but it also stores, and it also holds carcasses waiting for a buyer who is late. The room fills with warm carcasses and everything already inside warms up. The problem is not capacity — it is conflicting duties.

Growth pathProblem it solvesDoes not solveTypical capital
A. Second chill roomSeparates holding from chilling; doubles throughput; lets you hold two buyers' orders apartLong-term storage; seasonal glut; no slaughter hygiene gainMedium
B. Freezer room / blast freezerStores meat for months; absorbs glut; enables value-added and out-of-season salesDaily throughput — it is storage, not chilling; higher running costMedium–High
C. On-farm abattoirControls slaughter hygiene, timing and cost per head; unlocks direct retailNothing about storage — you still need A and BHigh (and licensed)

Rule of thumb

Build in the order A → B → C. A second chill room pays back fastest because it removes the bottleneck you already have. A freezer creates new products. An abattoir creates new obligations.

2. Option A — The second chill room

Sizing

Do not copy the first room. Size the second room from the peak day, not the average day, because that is what you are building it for.

Worked sizing — second chill room

Peak slaughter: 4 beasts/day. Average warm carcass: 180 kg (beef).

Peak daily intake = 4 × 180 = 720 kg warm carcass

Chilling duty: pull 720 kg from 38 °C to 4 °C in 18 h.
Heat to remove ≈ 720 kg × 3.6 kJ/kg·K × 34 K ≈ 88 100 kJ over 18 h
= 88 100 ÷ (18 × 3600) ≈ 1.36 kW average chilling load

Add 25 % for door openings, lights, fan heat and infiltration → 1.7 kW

Plus room transmission: 12 m² surface × 0.25 W/m²K × 30 K ≈ 90 W → negligible but include it.

Choose a 2.5–3 HP condensing unit with a 25 % margin over the 1.7 kW duty. On a 24 h pull-down (safer for meat quality) the same unit runs comfortably.

Room dimensions from carcass geometry

Beef carcasses on rails need 1.0 m rail length per carcass (side-by-side) and 0.75 m of clear space either side of the rail line. Height: 2.6 m clear under the rail for a beef side, 2.2 m for goat or sheep.

Shared plant or separate units?

ApproachAdvantageDisadvantage
One large condensing unit, two evaporatorsCheaper, one service pointOne failure kills both rooms; rooms must run at the same temperature
Two independent unitsRedundancy — one room keeps working; each room set to its own temperatureHigher capital and two services

Recommendation: two independent units. The whole point of a second room is resilience. A shared plant reintroduces the single point of failure you just paid to remove.

3. Option B — Freezer room or blast freezer

These are different machines doing different jobs. Do not confuse them.

  • Blast freezer: −30 to −35 °C air, high air velocity, pulls a 180 kg carcass from 38 °C to −18 °C in 18–24 h. Small, expensive per m³, run only when loaded.
  • Freezer store: −18 to −20 °C, still air or low velocity, holds product for 6–12 months. Larger, cheaper per m³, runs continuously.

Worked sizing — blast freezer

Freeze 400 kg of product per batch, 38 °C → −18 °C in 20 h.
Sensible heat (above freezing): 400 × 3.6 × 38 ≈ 54 700 kJ
Latent heat of fusion: 400 × 250 ≈ 100 000 kJ
Sensible heat (below freezing, c ≈ 1.8 kJ/kg·K): 400 × 1.8 × 18 ≈ 13 000 kJ
Total ≈ 167 700 kJ over 20 h = 2.33 kW

Add 30 % for fan heat, defrost and infiltration → 3.0 kW. Select a 5–6 HP low-temperature condensing unit with a matched blast evaporator. Low-temperature units need larger displacement because refrigerant density drops — never derate a medium-temperature unit to do this job.

Defrost is not optional

Below 0 °C, moisture from the product freezes onto the evaporator coil. Without a defrost cycle (electric, hot-gas or off-cycle) the coil ices solid within days, airflow collapses and the room stops freezing. Budget for a defrost timer and a drain line that cannot freeze shut — heat-trace the drain if the room is below −20 °C.

4. Option C — On-farm abattoir (slaughter slab)

An abattoir is a hygiene building, not a meat room. Its job is to move animals in one end and clean carcasses out the other, with no path ever crossing back.

Minimum functional areas

  1. Lairage — holding pens, water, shade, 12–24 h rest. Sloped, washable floor, drained away from the kill floor.
  2. Stun and bleed — restraint crate, stun box, bleed rail over a drained blood collection trough.
  3. Dressing floor — hard, non-slip, washable floor; 3 m headroom; washable walls to 1.8 m; hose points; knife steriliser at hand.
  4. Offal and condemned room — separate, drained, fly-proof, with its own door to outside.
  5. Chill room — your existing room (or the new second room from Option A).
  6. Wash and change — separate hand-wash and boot-wash; staff changing on the clean side.
  7. Waste and effluent — blood, paunch contents, wastewater to a soakaway or biogas digester, never to surface water.

Power is the real constraint

Adding a freezer to a site with a 5 kVA supply is the most common scaling mistake. A freezer that thaws during a six-hour outage destroys more value than it ever created. Size the generator for locked-rotor starting current of the largest compressor — typically 3–4× running current — plus everything else running at once.

6. Licences and inspection

  • Abattoir: almost always requires registration or licensing with the national veterinary or food-safety authority, plus a designated meat inspector. Check before you pour concrete.
  • Freezer / cold store: usually a food-business registration, temperature records, and a written HACCP-style plan.
  • Second chill room: often falls under the same registration you already hold — confirm the change of capacity.
  • Effluent: discharge consent or proof of on-site treatment.
