Practical Reference

Preservation is a building problem before it is a food problem. A processor who dries 200 kg of mangoes on a tarpaulin loses the batch to a single afternoon of rain; the same processor with a raised solar drying shed and a concrete curing floor keeps the batch and sells it at three times the price of fresh fruit in the off-season. The figures below are drawn from FAO, PHC (Postharvest Consortium), and African market realities. Use them to size your building, set your throughput, and price your product before you pour the first slab.

â  SAFETY WARNING â FOOD SAFETY AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS

  • Botulism risk: Low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, fish) preserved in airtight containers without proper acidification or pressure canning can grow Clostridium botulinum. This is a lethal toxin. Never sell low-acid hermetic products without a validated process.
  • Smoke and PAHs: Traditional direct-smoke methods deposit polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons linked to cancer. Build indirect smoke chambers and keep smoke temperature below 80 Â°C.
  • Salt and nitrite: Curing salts (sodium nitrite) are toxic in excess. Weigh precisely; never use "a pinch." Store away from food preparation areas.
  • Pesticide residues: Never store preservation chemicals, fuels, or pesticides in the same building as food. Build a separate locked chemical store.
  • Aflatoxin: Damp drying floors and poor ventilation grow Aspergillus flavus on groundnuts, maize, and tree nuts. Aflatoxin is a liver carcinogen. Keep drying floors dry and produce off the ground.
  • Cold room refrigerant: Ammonia and Freon leaks are dangerous. Only certified technicians should install and service refrigeration.
  • Regulatory: Confirm all requirements with your national food safety authority (e.g., KEBS in Kenya, SAZ in Zimbabwe, NAFDAC in Nigeria, Ghana FDA) before processing any food for sale.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Site the building on high ground. Water runs downhill; so does contamination. A 30 cm plinth is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
  2. Separate wet and dry zones. Washing, brining, and boiling belong in one room; drying, curing, and packing in another. Cross-contamination kills batches and reputations.
  3. Size the drying floor for your worst week, not your average week. Mango and tomato harvests peak. If your shed cannot handle the peak, you will lose the peak.
  4. Build for airflow, not just heat. A solar dryer without a chimney and low-level vents is an oven, not a dryer. Air must enter low and exit high.
  5. Use food-grade, washable surfaces. Concrete, stainless steel, or food-grade plastic. No bare wood in contact with wet product.
  6. Install a thermometer and hygrometer in every processing room. You cannot manage what you cannot measure. Record daily.
  7. Lock the chemical store. Pesticides, fuels, and cleaning agents in a separate, ventilated, locked building at least 10 m from food handling.
  8. Register with your national food safety authority before you sell. Budget 2â6 months and USD 100â1,000 for licensing, depending on country and product.
