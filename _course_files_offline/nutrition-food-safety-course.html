Practical Reference

This section is your working toolkit. Everything below is designed to be printed, pinned to the wall of your processing room, and used during an actual inspection or buyer audit. In African food businesses, the difference between a product that sits on a shelf and one that moves into supermarkets, export markets, or institutional supply contracts (schools, mines, NGOs) is almost always documentation and consistency, not recipe. Buyers do not pay a premium for good intentions. They pay for verifiable control.

⚠️ SAFETY WARNING - FOOD, WATER AND CHEMICALS

Foodborne illness can kill. Never taste food to test if it is safe - pathogens like Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli O157:H7 are odourless and tasteless. Never mix cleaning chemicals (especially chlorine bleach with ammonia or acid) - the resulting gas causes lung injury. Store all sanitisers, pesticides and fumigants in a locked cabinet away from food, and label every container. In Zimbabwe, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa and across the continent, food handlers with open wounds, diarrhoea or jaundice must be excluded from production areas. Aflatoxin-contaminated grain cannot be made safe by cooking - reject it at intake. If you suspect a food safety incident, stop production, isolate the batch, and contact your national food safety authority immediately. Your reputation and your customers' lives depend on it.

Quick Reference Checklist

Print this. Tick it daily. A signed daily checklist is the single most powerful document you can show a buyer or inspector - it proves you run a controlled process, not a hopeful one.

  • Calibrate thermometers weekly - use the ice-point method (0°C) and boiling-point method (adjust for altitude; roughly 100°C at sea level, about 94°C at 1,800 m). A thermometer that reads wrong makes every other control worthless.
  • Log every CCP reading in ink - date, time, value, initials. Pencil can be erased; inspectors notice.
  • Separate raw and cooked physically - different surfaces, different colour-coded boards, different staff where possible. Cross-contamination is the number one cause of outbreaks in small processors.
  • Train every handler on the five keys to safer food - keep clean, separate raw and cooked, cook thoroughly, keep food at safe temperatures, use safe water and raw materials (WHO).
  • Verify supplier certificates - for grain, dairy and spices, keep the aflatoxin or mycotoxin test certificate on file per batch. No certificate, no purchase.
  • Run a mock recall twice a year - pick a batch code, trace it from raw material to customer in under 4 hours. If you cannot, your traceability system is a fiction.
  • Review labels against the law before every print run - ingredients, allergens, best-before, batch code, country of origin, net weight. A rejected print run costs more than the label design.
  • Keep a corrective action register - when a limit is breached, record what went wrong, what you did, and how you stopped it recurring. This register is your defence in any dispute.

Further Reading

  • FAO Food Safety and Quality - free technical guidance, HACCP manuals and Codex texts relevant to African value chains.
  • WHO Food Safety Fact Sheet - the Five Keys to Safer Food and global burden-of-disease data you can cite to buyers and staff.
  • Codex Alimentarius Commission - the international food standards most African national regulators base their rules on. Download the General Principles of Food Hygiene free.
  • USDA Agricultural Research Service - open nutrient data and food safety research useful for formulation and labelling.
  • African Union - Agriculture, Rural Development and Food Security - continental policy, the Africa Food Safety Index and regional harmonisation updates that affect cross-border trade.
