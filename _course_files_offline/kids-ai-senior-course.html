Practical Reference

This reference section is written for the learner, the parent, and the small business owner who wants to turn a working classifier into something that earns money or saves money. Anyone can train a model. The commercial advantage belongs to the person who can measure where it fails, price the risk of that failure, and ship a fix. Use the diagram, tables, and checklist below as your working bench.

A classifier is decision support, not a prescription. Never spray a pesticide, herbicide, or fungicide based only on a model output. Confirm every positive identification with the farmer, an extension officer, or a physical sample before any chemical is applied.

Always follow the label. Wear gloves, boots, a long-sleeve shirt, and eye protection. Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling agrochemicals. Store chemicals locked away from children, food, and animal feed. Observe the pre-harvest interval stated on the label. In case of suspected poisoning, contact your nearest clinic immediately and carry the product label with you.

Zimbabwe poison information: contact your nearest district hospital or the Medicines Control Authority of Zimbabwe (mca.org.zw). Regional guidance: FAO Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management (fao.org).

Read the tables together. Accuracy of 90% sounds excellent. The cost table shows that the 12% false negative rate is where the money bleeds â 7,920 USD per 100 fields versus 560 USD for false positives. That asymmetry is the whole business case for measuring recall, not accuracy. If you are selling a classifier to farmers, your pitch is not "90% accurate." Your pitch is "we cut missed infestations by half, and here is what that is worth per hectare."

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Define the positive class in writing. Before collecting a single image, write down exactly what "positive" means. Ambiguity here poisons every metric downstream.
  2. Collect at least 40 images per class, from more than one location and more than one day. A model trained on one farm on one afternoon will fail on the next farm.
  3. Split data before you train, not after. Hold out 20% as a test set. Never let test images leak into training. This is the most common beginner error.
  4. Report precision and recall, never accuracy alone. If your classes are imbalanced, accuracy lies. Always publish the full confusion matrix.
  5. Document your dataset bias. Who took the photos? Which region, season, phone camera, and lighting? Write it down and publish it with the model.
  6. Test the model on deliberately hard cases. Blurry photos, partial leaves, unusual lighting, different soil. If it breaks, you have found your next training batch.
  7. Price the cost of each error type. A false negative in crop disease costs far more than a false positive. Let that asymmetry drive your threshold choice.
  8. Set a monitoring plan before deployment. Decide who checks the model weekly, how errors are logged, and when retraining is triggered. A model with no monitoring is a liability.
