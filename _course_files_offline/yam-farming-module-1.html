Lesson 1

Why Yam Pays More Than Any Other Root Crop

In this lesson
  • Explain why yam out-earns cassava and sweet potato
  • Understand the ceremony and food-value premium
  • Recognise the cost barrier — seed yam

Yam (Dioscorea spp.) is the most expensive root crop per kilogram in West African markets. Fresh food yam sells at $0.60–1.50/kg, sometimes $2.00/kg at peak ceremony season. Pounded yam flour sells at $3.00–6.00/kg. Seed yam (small tubers for planting) sells at 2–3x food yam price.

The reason is food value, not nutrition. Yam is the ceremonial food of West Africa — weddings, festivals, funerals, Christmas. Demand is inelastic at the top of the market. Farmers who can supply consistently, in the right sizes, at the right times, earn incomes that no cassava farmer can match.

The trade-off is cost. Seed yam is expensive — 30–50% of total production cost. This is why the seed yam business is a business in itself, and why this course covers it as a separate opportunity.

Lesson 2

White Yam, Water Yam, Yellow Yam — the Differences That Matter

In this lesson
  • Distinguish the main yam species by use and market
  • Choose species for your rainfall and soil
  • Match species to the buyer

White yam (Dioscorea rotundata)

The most important African yam. Preferred for pounded yam and boiled yam. Cycle 8–10 months. Yield 10–25 t/ha. Requires deep, loose, fertile soil. Dominant in Nigeria, Ghana, Benin, Togo, Côte d'Ivoire.

Water yam (Dioscorea alata)

Also called greater yam. Higher yield (20–40 t/ha), more drought-tolerant, and easier to grow than white yam. Less preferred for pounded yam but excellent for flour and chips. Cycle 8–10 months. Important in the Caribbean and increasingly in Africa.

Yellow yam (Dioscorea cayenensis)

Yellow-fleshed; strong flavour; excellent for pounded yam. Common in West Africa and the Caribbean. Higher market price than white yam in some markets. Cycle 9–12 months.

Lesser yam (Dioscorea esculenta) and aerial yam (D. bulbifera)

Specialty species. Niche markets. Not the main commercial focus for African smallholders.

Choosing a species

  • Deep fertile soil, high rainfall: white yam
  • Marginal soil, drought risk: water yam
  • Premium pounded yam market: yellow yam
  • Flour and chips processing: water yam
Lesson 3

The Three Markets — Food, Seed, Processing

In this lesson
  • Understand the three markets and their price levels
  • Decide which market your operation serves
  • See how they can be combined

Market 1 — Fresh food yam

Urban markets, ceremonial buyers, restaurants, supermarkets. $0.60–1.50/kg, spikes to $2.00+ at festivals. Requires clean, uniform, well-shaped tubers. The largest market by volume.

Market 2 — Seed yam / sett multiplication

Other yam farmers, seed distributors, cooperatives. $1.20–3.00/kg (2–3x food price). Requires strict nematode and disease control, careful grading by size, and reputation for delivering clean stock. The highest margin business in the yam value chain.

Market 3 — Processing (flour, chips, poundable yam)

Urban food processors, supermarket private labels, diaspora export. Pounded yam flour sells at $3.00–6.00/kg. Chips and crisps at $4.00–8.00/kg. Requires processing equipment, food-safety compliance, and packaging.

Lesson 4

Yield Reality and the African Gap

In this lesson
  • Know the yield range for African conditions
  • Identify the four factors that close the gap
  • Set a realistic first-season target

African average yam yield is 8–12 t/ha. Achievable with clean seed, staking, and basic fertility: 15–25 t/ha for white yam, up to 30 t/ha for water yam. Under irrigation and good management, 40+ t/ha is recorded.

Four factors that close the gap

  1. Clean seed yam. Nematode-infected seed can cut yield by 40–60%.
  2. Deep, loose soil. Yam tubers can grow 1–2 m long. Compacted soil gives short, misshapen, low-value tubers.
  3. Staking. Proper staking with tall stakes increases yield 30–60% versus unstaked.
  4. Fertility. Yam is a heavy feeder. Well-rotted manure at planting plus a top-up of potassium at tuber formation.
Lesson 5

Choosing Your Entry Point

In this lesson
  • Match the entry point to your land and capital
  • Estimate capital and revenue per hectare
  • Scale up only after a successful first cycle

Three entry points

  • Home plot (200–500 m²): capital $150–500, revenue $200–800. Best for learning the crop.
  • Smallholder (0.5–1 ha): capital $800–2,500, revenue $1,500–4,000. Serious business.
  • Commercial (2–5 ha): capital $3,000–15,000, revenue $6,000–25,000. Requires hired labour and storage.

Yam is labour-intensive. A hectare needs 200–400 person-days over the cycle for planting, staking, weeding, and harvest. Start small and grow with your skill.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. Why does yam command a higher price than cassava?

Correct: ceremonial value. Festival demand is inelastic at the top of the market.

2. What is the highest-margin market in yam?

Correct: seed yam. 2–3x food price, but strict quality requirements.

3. Which yam species is preferred for pounded yam?

Correct: white yam. Also yellow yam — both are preferred for pounded yam.

4. How much does staking increase yam yield?

Correct: 30–60%. Yam is a climbing vine — staking is essential.

5. What is the African average yam yield?

Correct: 8–12 t/ha. Achievable is 15–25 t/ha.
