Practical Reference

This enrichment block is your field manual. The modules teach the method; this section gives you the numbers, the science behind them, and the checklists you will actually use on the floor. Keep it open next to your dryer. Every figure below is sourced so you can verify it and defend it to a buyer, a lender, or a health inspector.

Why the curve matters commercially. The first phase removes surface water fast and cheap. The second phase removes bound water slowly and is where your energy cost per kilogram climbs steeply. If you pull product at the wrong point on this curve, you either pay for water you did not need to remove, or you pack product that will mould in the warehouse. Both kill repeat orders.

The business decision hidden in Table 2. Solar cabinet dryers have the lowest running cost but the longest batch time and the highest weather risk. Electric cabinet dryers give you schedule certainty, which is what supermarkets and export buyers actually pay for. Hybrid systems are usually the best risk-adjusted choice for an African SME that wants to sell to formal retail, because you can promise a delivery date and still keep energy cost per kilogram under control.

Moisture testing without a laboratory

You do not need a lab to run a commercial operation, but you do need a repeatable method. The two practical options are:

  • Digital moisture meter (capacitance or resistance type). Fast, cheap, and good for relative readings once you calibrate against a known sample. Calibrate it against the oven method below at least once per product.
  • Oven method (reference standard). Weigh a 10 g sample, dry it at 105Â°C for 4-6 hours (or until constant weight), weigh again. Moisture % = (initial weight â final weight) Ã· initial weight Ã 100. This is the number you can put on a spec sheet.
The squeeze-and-snap field test. A correctly dried fruit slice bends without breaking and shows no free moisture when squeezed. A correctly dried vegetable snaps cleanly. If it bends like leather, it is still too wet to pack for ambient storage.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calibrate your moisture meter against an oven-dried reference sample for every new product before you set your pull-point.
  2. Record every batch: date, product, fresh weight in, dry weight out, temperature, hours, final moisture %. This log is your quality system and your bank's evidence of consistency.
  3. Pre-treat consistently. Blanch vegetables in steam or hot water; use citric acid or ascorbic acid dip on fruit to stop browning. Inconsistent pre-treatment is the number one cause of rejected batches.
  4. Load trays evenly and do not overload. Air must move through the bed. Overloading doubles drying time and creates mould pockets.
  5. Cool before packing. Packing warm product causes condensation inside the bag and instant mould growth. Cool to room temperature in a dry room.
  6. Use food-grade, moisture-barrier packaging with a correct label: product name, net weight, batch number, best-before date, and your contact details. This is a legal and commercial requirement in most African markets.
  7. Store on pallets, off the floor, in a dry, ventilated room. Never store dried food against a wall or on bare concrete.
  8. Run a cost-per-kilogram calculation every month covering raw material, energy, labour, packaging, and transport. If you do not know your cost, you cannot negotiate price.
