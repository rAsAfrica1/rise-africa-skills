Module 10 — Substrate Preparation and Pasteurisation

The input that decides 70 % of your yield — how to make it, wet it, heat it and cool it correctly.

Learning outcomes

  • Choose a substrate and supplement for your species.
  • Calculate the correct moisture content for pasteurisation and for spawning.
  • Pasteurise or sterilise substrate to the correct temperature and hold time.
  • Cool substrate safely before spawning.
  • Prepare and handle spawn to maximise colonisation speed.
  • Recognise the failure modes and how to avoid them.

1. Substrate selection

Mushrooms are grown on lignocellulosic material — plant matter made of cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin. Different species prefer different materials.

SubstrateBest speciesNotesCost
Wheat strawOyster, button (mixed)Chopped 30–50 mm; widely available; consistentLow
Rice strawOyster, straw mushroomSimilar to wheat straw; common in Asia and AfricaLow
Sawdust (hardwood)Shiitake, oyster, lion's maneFine grade, from untreated hardwood; must be aged or compostedLow–medium
Cotton seed hullsOyster, shiitakeHigh yielding; slightly expensive; good structureMedium

Never use treated or mouldy material

Timber treated with preservatives, painted wood, particle board with glues, and any straw or sawdust that is already mouldy will fail — or worse, will grow Trichoderma within days. Do not try to pasteurise your way out of a mouldy input. Start with clean material.

2. Supplements

Straw alone gives modest yields. Adding nitrogen-rich supplements (bran, meal) raises yields but also raises contamination risk — supplements are exactly what moulds like too.

SupplementRateEffectContamination risk
Wheat bran5–10 % dry weightBig yield increaseHigh if substrate is pasteurised; low if sterilised
Rice bran5–10 %Similar to wheat branHigh
Soybean meal2–5 %Higher nitrogen; strong yield responseVery high unless sterilised
Cotton seed meal2–5 %Slow release nitrogenHigh
Gypsum (calcium sulphate)1–2 %Not a nutrient — improves texture, buffers pH, reduces greasinessNone
Lime (calcium carbonate)1–2 %Raises pH; helpful against TrichodermaNone

Supplement decision rule

If pasteurising (65–75 °C): keep supplement below 5 % of dry substrate weight.
If sterilising (121 °C, 15 psi): supplement can go to 10–20 %.

Why: pasteurisation leaves some thermophilic bacteria alive, and they compete with mushroom mycelium when there is a lot of nitrogen. Sterilisation leaves nothing, so the mycelium gets all the nitrogen — but requires an autoclave and a clean room to keep the substrate clean after.

3. Moisture content

Substrate should be wet enough for the mycelium to grow, but not so wet that water pools and air is excluded. Target moisture for straw substrates is 65–70 % — that is 65–70 % water by total weight.

Moisture calculation

You have 20 kg of dry straw. Target moisture 68 %.

Let W = kg of water to add. Target: W / (20 + W) = 0.68
W = 0.68 × (20 + W)
W = 13.6 + 0.68W
0.32W = 13.6
W = 42.5 kg of water

Total wet substrate = 20 + 42.5 = 62.5 kg.

If supplemented with 10 % wheat bran (2 kg dry), add it to the dry weight first:
Dry = 22 kg. Target 68 %:
W = 0.68 × (22 + W) → 0.32W = 0.68 × 22 = 14.96 → W = 46.8 kg of water.
Total wet substrate = 68.8 kg.

Field test — the squeeze test

  1. Take a handful of wetted substrate.
  2. Squeeze hard in your fist.
  3. Correct moisture: a few drops appear between your fingers, but water does not stream.
  4. Too dry: no drops appear.
  5. Too wet: water streams out freely.
  6. Confirm with an oven-dry method once: weigh a sample, dry it at 105 °C until constant weight, weigh again. Calculate moisture %. Use this to calibrate your squeeze test.

Hot water bath — heat and time

Batch: 68.8 kg wet substrate, starting at 20 °C, target 70 °C.
Specific heat of wet straw ≈ 3.0 kJ/kg·K (higher than dry because of water).
Heat required = 68.8 × 3.0 × (70 − 20) = 10 320 kJ.

Over 90 min (5 400 s):
Power required = 10 320 / 5 400 = 1.9 kW average.

Add 30 % for losses: 2.5 kW heating capacity.

But you also have to heat the water itself. If the straw is submerged in 100 L of water, heating that water adds: 100 × 4.18 × 50 = 20 900 kJ. Add that, and the total heat is 31 220 kJ.
Over 90 min = 5.8 kW average.
Plan for a 6–8 kW heat source (or accept a longer ramp and hold the straw submerged only in just enough water).

Water bath is the easiest method

You do not need a purpose-built pasteuriser for small-scale production. A 200 L steel drum, a gas burner, and a probe thermometer work. Submerge the wetted straw in a mesh bag, heat the water to 70 °C, hold for 60 minutes with the drum covered, then drain and cool. Yields are comparable to steam pasteurisation at this scale.

5. Cooling

After pasteurisation, substrate must cool to spawning temperature — usually below 25 °C for oyster and below 30 °C for shiitake. Hot substrate kills spawn.

  1. Drain the substrate thoroughly. Excess water must not remain.
  2. Spread on a clean, sanitised, slatted surface in a clean area.
  3. Turn the substrate every 30 minutes so it cools evenly.
  4. Cover loosely with a clean sheet to keep airborne spores out but let heat escape.
  5. Measure the core temperature at three points until all read below 25 °C.
  6. Move to the inoculation area as soon as it is cool.

Cooling is the highest-risk window

During cooling, pasteurised substrate is warm, wet, and no longer protected by heat. This is when airborne Trichoderma spores land and start growing. Keep the cooling area as clean as the inoculation room, cool quickly, and do not let substrate sit overnight.

Spawn rate

How much spawn to use?

68.8 kg wet substrate, target spawn rate 8 % wet weight:
Spawn needed = 68.8 × 0.08 = 5.5 kg of grain spawn.

Higher spawn rates (10–15 %) colonise faster and reduce contamination risk, at higher cost. Lower rates (3–5 %) are cheaper but slower, and slow colonisation = higher contamination rate.

Rule: use more spawn in warm, humid conditions, and less in cool, dry ones. Never use less than 3 % for a first batch.

7. Mixing and bagging

  1. Sanitise the mixing surface and the mixing tool with 70 % alcohol.
  2. Break up the spawn into small pieces with clean hands (gloves) — do not crush it.
  3. Mix spawn through the cooled substrate at 3–10 % rate. Mix evenly — pockets of spawn-free substrate will be colonised by competitors.
  4. Fill clean bags. Pack to a consistent weight and density — usually 5 kg wet per bag for a standard bag.
  5. Insert a filter patch or make a small hole in the bag for gas exchange. Too many holes = contamination; too few = slow growth.
  6. Seal with a fold, clip or heat-seal. Label with a batch code and date.
  7. Move to the colonisation room at 22–25 °C (oyster) or 20–24 °C (shiitake).

Bag yield calculation

68.8 kg wet substrate per batch.
Bags filled at 5 kg each = 13.7, round to 14 bags.

Expected yield: 30 % biological efficiency on wet weight.
= 68.8 × 0.30 = 20.6 kg mushrooms per batch.

Per bag: 20.6 / 14 = 1.47 kg per 5 kg bag over 3 flushes.
If you sell at 8/kg, gross per batch = 20.6 × 8 = 165.

Cost: substrate 10, spawn 22, bag 3, heat and electricity 8, labour 15 = 58.
Margin per batch = 165 − 58 = 107. That is a 65 % margin.
