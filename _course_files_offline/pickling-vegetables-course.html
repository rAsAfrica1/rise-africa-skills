Acidity and Vinegar Strength: The Numbers That Decide Shelf Life

The single most common reason African pickle businesses fail is under-acidification. If the equilibrium pH of the vegetable plus brine sits above 4.6, you have created a low-acid environment where Clostridium botulinum can produce toxin. Below 4.0 you are safe from botulism and most spoilage. Aim for 3.5â3.9. Measure it â do not guess.

â  SAFETY WARNING â READ BEFORE YOU PICKLE ANYTHING

  • Never sell a jar that has not been pH-tested. Botulism toxin is odourless, tasteless, and invisible. A jar that looks and smells fine can kill. If you do not own a pH meter or narrow-range strips, buy them before your first batch.
  • Do not dilute vinegar "to taste." Commercial vinegar must be 5% acetic acid minimum. If your supplier cannot show a label stating acetic acid concentration, reject it.
  • Never use a swollen lid, cloudy brine, or fizzy jar. Discard the entire batch â not just the affected jar. Do not taste to "check."
  • Boiling water scalds cause serious burns. Use long tongs, wear closed shoes, and never fill jars more than 1 cm from the rim before sealing.
  • Chlorine washing water must be food-grade and rinsed. Never mix chlorine with vinegar â it releases toxic chlorine gas.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Buy a pH meter or 3.5â5.0 narrow-range strips before your first commercial batch. Budget US$15â40. Record every batch reading in a dated log book.
  2. Confirm vinegar strength in writing. Ask the supplier for the acetic acid percentage. 5% minimum; 6â7% gives more safety margin for high-water vegetables like cucumber.
  3. Weigh, do not scoop. Salt and sugar by weight, not volume. A kitchen scale accurate to 1 g costs under US$10 and prevents inconsistent brine.
  4. Sterilise jars in boiling water for 10 minutes and fill while hot. Cold jars crack and harbour mould spores in the headspace.
