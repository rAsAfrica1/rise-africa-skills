Lesson 1

Late Blight — the Field Killer

In this lesson
  • Identify late blight symptoms
  • Understand the weather conditions that trigger it
  • Act before it spreads

Late blight (Phytophthora infestans) is the most destructive disease of potato worldwide. It is the same pathogen that caused the Irish potato famine. In African highlands, an unmanaged crop can be destroyed in 7–14 days.

Symptoms

  • Water-soaked grey-green patches on leaves, usually starting at the tips or edges
  • Patches turn brown-black within days
  • White fuzzy growth on the underside of leaves in humid conditions
  • Whole plant collapse
  • Tubers develop firm brown patches and rot in storage

Trigger conditions

  • Cool (15–22°C) and humid (above 85% RH) weather
  • Rain or heavy dew for 6+ hours
  • Extended fog
  • Overhead irrigation in cool weather

Speed of spread

In warm, wet conditions, late blight can destroy a hectare in 7–14 days. In cool, wet conditions, it is slower but still severe. There is no cure — only prevention and rapid response.

The rule: late blight is prevented by weekly scouting and timely fungicide. Once you see it spreading, it is too late to save the crop. Every week without a spray in wet weather is a week of growing risk.
Lesson 2

The Blight Spray Programme

In this lesson
  • Choose the right fungicides
  • Time sprays correctly
  • Rotate actives to prevent resistance

Preventive vs curative

  • Preventive (contact) fungicides: mancozeb, chlorothalonil, copper. Cover the leaf surface before spores arrive. Cheap, effective if applied before infection.
  • Curative (systemic) fungicides: metalaxyl, cymoxanil, dimethomorph, propamocarb. Absorbed into the plant, work after infection. More expensive but effective once blight has started.

Resistance management

Fungal pathogens develop resistance quickly, especially to systemic fungicides like metalaxyl. Alternate actives every 2–3 sprays. Do not use the same active more than twice in a row.

Application technique

  • Spray in the morning so leaves dry before evening
  • Cover the underside of leaves — that is where the fungus enters
  • Use a knapsack sprayer with a fine nozzle
  • Spray 300–500 L of spray mix per hectare
  • Respect pre-harvest intervals (usually 7–14 days)

Lesson 3

Bacterial Wilt — the Soil Sentence

In this lesson
  • Identify bacterial wilt
  • Understand why it cannot be cured
  • Use resistant varieties, rotation, and clean seed

Symptoms

  • Sudden wilting of the whole plant, often starting on one side
  • Leaves stay green at first, then yellow and dry
  • Cut stem placed in water releases a milky bacterial ooze
  • Tubers show brown vascular ring and may rot

Why it is a soil sentence

Bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum) survives in soil for years, even without a host. Once present in a field, potato, tomato, pepper, and eggplant will get it. There is no chemical cure.

Prevention and management

  1. Clean seed. Buy only certified, tested seed. Never plant potato from an infected field.
  2. Rotation. At least 3 seasons away from potato and other solanaceous crops. Cereals and legumes are the best rotation partners.
  3. Resistant varieties. Some varieties carry partial resistance. Ask your research institute.
  4. Drainage. Bacterial wilt thrives in waterlogged soils. Raised ridges, drainage channels.
  5. Rogueing. Remove and destroy wilted plants immediately — do not compost them.
  6. Grafting. Grafting potato onto resistant rootstock is possible but not common at smallholder scale.
Lesson 4

Other Pests and Diseases

In this lesson
  • Identify the main insect pests of potato
  • Manage aphids (virus vectors) and tuber moth
  • Recognise early blight and common scab

Aphids

The main vectors of potato viruses (PVY, PLRV). A heavy aphid population can infect a crop in weeks. Control:

  • Yellow sticky traps for monitoring
  • Neem or soap spray for light pressure
  • Imidacloprid or thiamethoxam for heavy pressure — respect PHI
  • Roguing of infected plants to slow spread

Potato tuber moth (Phthorimaea operculella)

Larvae mine leaves and tunnel into tubers in the field and in storage. Worse in warm, dry conditions. Control:

  • Hill up thoroughly — exposes larvae to natural enemies
  • Harvest promptly; do not leave tubers in the field
  • Store in cool, dark, dry conditions with a 5 cm layer of clean straw or sand over the top
  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) sprays in the field

Colorado potato beetle

Striped beetle; larvae defoliate. Less common in Africa but spreading. Hand-pick adults and larvae.

Early blight (Alternaria solani)

Target-shaped brown spots on older leaves. Less devastating than late blight. Manage with balanced nutrition and, if severe, a fungicide containing mancozeb or chlorothalonil.

Common scab (Streptomyces scabies)

Rough, corky patches on tuber skin. Cosmetic damage. Worse in neutral or alkaline soils and with fresh manure. Manage with slightly acidic pH and rotation.

Lesson 5

Scouting and Threshold Decisions

In this lesson
  • Set up a weekly scouting routine
  • Decide when spraying is justified
  • Keep a spray log

Weekly scouting (20 min per hectare)

  1. Walk a W pattern through the plot
  2. At 10 points, check 5 plants: top leaves (for aphids), lower leaves (for blight), stems at base (for bacterial wilt), soil surface near plant (for tuber moth)
  3. Record observations with dates
  4. Take photos of uncertain symptoms; ask an extension officer

Spray decisions

  • Late blight: act immediately when spotted. Spray systemic, then follow 5-day interval.
  • Early blight: spray if lesions are on >10% of leaves.
  • Aphids: spray if heavy pressure — the risk is virus transmission, not the aphids themselves.
  • Tuber moth: Bt sprays weekly if larvae seen.

Spray log

Record every spray: date, product, active ingredient, rate, area sprayed, PHI, expected safe harvest date. Keep this log permanently. It is your defense against residue rejection and your record for next season.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. What weather triggers late blight?

Correct: cool and humid. 15–22°C, above 85% RH for 6+ hours.

2. Why can bacterial wilt not be cured?

Correct: no cure, persists in soil. Prevention with clean seed and rotation is the only strategy.

3. How often should fungicide be applied in wet weather?

Correct: every 5–7 days. Alternate contact and systemic.

4. What is the main risk from aphids in potato?

Correct: virus transmission. Viruses have no cure, so vector control matters.

5. What is the best strategy against late blight?

Correct: preventive + scouting. Blight is prevented, not cured.

Module 4 capstone

Write your disease plan: (1) main threats in your area; (2) your spray programme and products; (3) your rotation plan; (4) your resistant varieties; (5) your scouting schedule and spray log template.
