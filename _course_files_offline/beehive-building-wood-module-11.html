Module 11 — Harvesting, Extraction and Honey Handling

From capped frames to jars that pass inspection — the practices that protect the crop and its price.

Home › Beehive Building — Wood › Module 11

Learning outcomes

  • Judge when honey is ready to harvest and when to leave it.
  • Clear bees from supers safely and without harming the colony.
  • Uncap, extract and filter honey without damaging quality.
  • Measure moisture, handle crystallisation and store honey correctly.
  • Package, label and price honey to regulation.
  • Keep records that show crop, yield and provenance.

1. When to harvest

The single rule: harvest only capped honey. Uncapped honey is not ripe — it still has too much water, and it will ferment in the jar.

SignalMeaningAction
8 or more frames capped in the superRipe and readyHarvest the super
Less than 6 frames cappedNot ripeLeave 1–2 more weeks; re-check
Caps pale and white (newly capped)Very fresh cap; moisture still stabilisingWait another week
Caps dark and waxy, fully drawn over cellsWell curedReady to harvest
Uncapped but frames feel heavy and drip thicklyProbably ripe but unconfirmedCheck with a refractometer before harvesting

Moisture — the number that decides quality

Honey is stable below 18 % moisture. Above 20 % it will ferment in the jar.
At 18–20 % it is borderline — fine for local sale, risky for long storage.

Test with a refractometer at 20 °C. Two drops on the prism is enough. If your honey reads above 20 %:
• Leave the super on longer for the bees to cure it.
• Or, if already extracted, use a dehumidifier in a sealed room over the honey bucket until it reads below 18.5 %.
Do not sell honey above 20 % moisture.

2. Leaving enough for the bees

Rule of thumb: leave the colony at least 15 kg of stores (8–10 full frames) after harvest — more if you face a long dearth. Harvesting everything and then feeding sugar is worse for the bees and worse for the honey.

Do not harvest the brood box

The brood box is the colony's home. Its honey is their winter food. Harvest only from the supers. Even in a strong flow, if the brood box has fewer than 6 full frames of stores, harvest nothing.

3. Clearing bees from the super

You need the super bee-free before taking it away. Bees left in a stored super die or rob the stored honey.

MethodHowTimeAdvantagesDisadvantages
Bee escape (portable)Fit a one-way escape under the super and leave it 24 h24 hGentle, no smoke, no bee deathsSlow; requires a return visit; useless in cold weather
Bee blowerBlow bees off each frame with a low-pressure leaf blowerMinutesFast; no smokeStresses bees; can damage wings if pressure too high; weather dependent

Best for most small operations: bee escape

A portable bee escape (a metal or plastic one-way valve) costs very little and works reliably. Fit it in the afternoon, come back in 24 hours, and the super is empty. Nothing is stressed, no bees are lost, and the honey is clean.

4. Uncapping and extracting

  1. Work indoors, in a bee-tight room. Otherwise you will have bees in the honey within minutes.
  2. Warm the room gently to 25–30 °C — honey flows better and uncapping is easier.
  3. Uncap with a hot uncapping knife, a roller, or a fork. Cut only the cappings; do not slice into the comb.
  4. Save the cappings in a colander or bucket. They contain honey and can be drained and sold separately.
  5. Load the extractor — usually 2, 4 or 9 frames at a time depending on size.
  6. Spin slowly at first, then increase speed. Fast start throws the comb out of the frame.
  7. Turn the frames and spin the other side. Repeat until all honey is out.
  8. Drain the extractor through a coarse sieve into a settling tank or bucket.
  9. Let the honey settle 24–48 hours in a sealed container before filtering fine. Air bubbles and wax rise to the top.

Extractor capacity and time

A 4-frame manual extractor takes about 15 minutes to process 4 frames including loading, spinning, turning and unloading.

A full super of 10 frames = 10 / 4 = 2.5 extractor loads ≈ 40 minutes.
For 10 supers: 10 × 40 minutes = 7 hours plus uncapping time.

Uncapping adds about 1 minute per frame. 100 frames × 1 min = 1.5 hours.
Total realistic time for 10 supers: 8–10 hours over two days, with settling between.

5. Filtering and settling

FilterAperturePurposeWhen
Coarse strainer2–3 mmCatches wax, bees, propolisAs honey leaves the extractor
Medium strainer0.5–1 mmRemoves finer wax particlesDuring settling
Fine strainer0.2–0.5 mmVery clean honey for retailBefore jarring
Cheesecloth / muslin0.1–0.3 mmSlow fine filtering, retains pollenOptional, for cloudy vs clear choice

Never over-filter. Honey that is filtered too fine loses pollen and some aroma, and can taste flat. Most small producers stop at 0.5 mm — clear enough for retail, still retaining character.

6. Moisture, crystallisation and heating

Moisture

  • Measure with a refractometer at every batch.
  • Target below 18 % for long storage and for export.
  • Honey above 20 % must be blended with drier honey, dried in a dehumidified room, or used quickly (baking, mead, cooking).

Crystallisation

Crystallisation is not spoilage. All honey crystallises eventually; the rate depends on the sugar balance. Some honeys (rapeseed, sunflower) crystallise in weeks; others (acacia, tupelo) stay liquid for years.

SituationAction
Customer wants liquid honeyWarm gently to 40 °C in a water bath, then jar
Customer wants soft-set honeyStir crystallised honey to break up crystals — the seed crystals give a smooth texture
Honey has crystallised in the combDo not extract — leave it for the bees, or cut comb honey
Prevent crystallisation in the jarDo not over-filter; store cool but not cold; some honeys are simply prone

Never heat above 45 °C

Above 45 °C, honey enzymes begin to break down. Above 60 °C, HMF (hydroxymethylfurfural) forms rapidly — it is the standard indicator of overheated honey, and it will fail you at inspection. Heat only in a water bath with a thermometer, at 40–43 °C, and only as long as needed to make the honey flow.

Label contents (required in most markets)

  • Product name: "Honey" or a specific type (e.g. "Acacia honey")
  • Net weight
  • Your business name and address
  • Batch or lot code
  • Best-before date
  • Country of origin
  • Storage instruction: "Store at room temperature; crystallisation is natural"

8. Storage

  • Store honey in sealed containers at 18–24 °C, in the dark.
  • Honey is hygroscopic — it absorbs moisture from the air. A closed container is not optional.
  • Do not store in metal — honey is acidic and will corrode most metals. Glass, food-grade plastic or stainless steel only.
  • Keep away from strong-smelling substances — honey absorbs odours.
  • Cool storage delays crystallisation but does not prevent it.

Yield per hive and cost per kg

3 hives produced 132 kg of honey.
Yield per hive = 44 kg — good for a strong colony, modest for a top producer.

Costs for the season (per hive share):
Hive and equipment amortisation: 12
Feed and treatments: 8
Labour (inspection and harvest): 25
Packaging per kg share: 1.20

Total cost per kg = (12 + 8 + 25) / 44 + 1.20 = 2.22 per kg.

Retail at 8 per 500 g jar = 16 per kg → margin 86 %.
Wholesale at 5 per kg → margin 55 %.

Retail direct is worth the effort if you have the market. Wholesale is useful for volume.
