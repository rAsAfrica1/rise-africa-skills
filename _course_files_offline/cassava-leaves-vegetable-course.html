â  SAFETY WARNING â Cyanogenic Glycosides

Raw cassava leaves contain cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). Eating improperly processed leaves can cause acute cyanide poisoning â symptoms include headache, dizziness, vomiting, confusion and, in severe cases, respiratory failure and death.

  • Never sell or eat raw cassava leaves.
  • Always wilt, pound and boil â the traditional African method (wilt 24â48 hrs, pound, then boil 30â60 min with water discarded).
  • Do not reuse the first boiling water for other food or feed.
  • Sweet varieties still require processing â "sweet" refers to root taste, not leaf safety.
  • If you feel unwell after handling or tasting leaves, seek medical help immediately and report to your local health authority.
  • Train every worker on this protocol before they touch the product. Document the training.

Source: WHO, Cyanide in Drinking-water (2011); FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius guidance on cassava processing.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Source clean planting material. Use disease-free stem cuttings from certified or known-clean sources (e.g. IITA-improved varieties) to protect both leaf and root yield.
  2. Harvest only tender leaves. Take the top 3â4 fully opened leaves; leave the growing tip to keep the plant productive.
  3. Wilt in shade for 24â48 hours. This starts cyanide reduction and reduces bulk before pounding.
  4. Pound or mill thoroughly. Break cell walls so cyanogenic compounds are released and can be removed in cooking water.
  5. Boil 30â60 minutes and discard the water. Never skip this step. This is your legal and ethical safety obligation.
  6. Weigh and record every batch. Track fresh-in vs. dried-out yield (typically 100 kg fresh â 22â28 kg dried).
  7. Package for the buyer. Fresh leaves for same-day urban markets; dried or frozen for longer shelf life and institutional contracts.
  8. Reinvest 30% of gross margin into a second plot, a mechanical miller, or packaging improvements to break the hand-to-mouth cycle.
