Module 2

Site, Power and the Load-Shedding Question

Where the room goes, how the power behaves in your district, and what happens to 2,500 kg of meat on the seventy-second hour of a blackout.

Read time: 28 minutesThen: Module 3 — The Envelope
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Site the cold room on the chain from live animal to sold meat, not in isolation
  • Read the actual reliability of your district grid over a full month, not from memory
  • Distinguish three classes of power backup and price each of them against the value of the meat
  • Design the electrical installation so that a future upgrade does not require a rebuild
  • Plan drainage and water as one system, not two afterthoughts
  • Produce a marked site plan and a power strategy for your own cold room

Lesson 2.1

Site selection — the room is one link, not the whole chain

Every cold room failure I have walked past has one of two origins. Either the room itself was wrong — wrong insulation, wrong refrigeration, wrong drainage — or the room was right and everything around it was wrong. The second is far more common, and it is entirely a site decision.

A cold room does not function in isolation. It sits inside a working chain: live animal arrives, is held, is slaughtered, is dressed, is transferred to the room, is cooled, is broken down, is packed, is dispatched. Every one of those links has a physical location, and if the locations do not form a sensible path, the room underperforms no matter how good it is.

The four movements that decide the site

Draw the intended path of four things across your site:

  1. The live animal, on foot. From the road or holding pen to the kill floor. This should be short, on firm ground, and ideally downhill or level — an animal that has just been walked three kilometres arrives stressed with reduced meat quality.
  2. The hot carcass, on a rail or trolley. From the kill floor into the cold room. This is the single most important movement, and it must be under cover. A hot carcass carried twenty metres across an open yard in thirty-degree heat picks up surface bacteria and heat load that the refrigeration unit then has to remove. Both the quality and the power bill suffer.
  3. The chilled carcass or cuts, on a trolley or a shoulder. From the cold room to the cutting table, and from the cutting table to the dispatch point. This movement should be direct and, ideally, inside the same building envelope.
  4. Water and waste. In from the source, out through the drain. Wash-down water from a kill floor is heavy with organic matter. It must not pool under the cold room, must not run through the holding pen, and must not cross the path of clean meat.
The rule: if any of the four movements crosses another, or leaves cover, or runs uphill, the site is wrong. Redraw the plan until all four are clean.

The ideal site layout

A small 10-beast operation works best on a single line, from live animal at one end to dispatch at the other, with the cold room in the middle and every movement under a continuous roof.

ROAD ← delivery vehicles arrive and leave │ ▼ ┌─────────┐ │ HOLDING │ live animals wait here (shaded, water) │ PEN │ └────┬────┘ │ (on foot, 10–15 m) ▼ ┌─────────┐ │ KILL │ slab, drain, water point, hoist │ FLOOR │ └────┬────┘ │ (rail or trolley, UNDER COVER, 3–8 m) ▼ ┌─────────┐ │ COLD │ ← the room this course is about │ ROOM │ └────┬────┘ │ (trolley or shoulder, 0–5 m) ▼ ┌─────────┐ │ CUTTING │ table, clean water, packaging │ ROOM │ └────┬────┘ │ (to dispatch) ▼ ┌─────────┐ │DISPATCH │ loading bay, cold transport └─────────┘

Not every element exists on day one. But the layout should be drawn on day one even if you build the cold room first. The people who build the cold room and only then realise they have nowhere to slaughter cleanly end up carrying carcasses through the mud — and then wonder why their meat fails inspection.

Lesson 2.2

Reading the actual grid — not from memory

Every cold room project starts with the owner's confident statement of power reliability. In every case I have checked, the memory is wrong — sometimes wildly optimistic, sometimes overly pessimistic. Do not plan on either.

The only reliable way to know what power you actually have is to measure it. It takes one month and costs nothing.

The measurement, done properly

  1. Install a simple log. A notebook, a wall chart, or a spreadsheet on a phone. You need four columns: date, time of outage start, time of outage end, total minutes off.
  2. Log every outage, including short ones. Brief flickers of ten seconds matter — they trip controllers and compressors, and they add up. A month of flickers is worth more planning data than one long blackout.
  3. Log the start time of each outage. Patterns matter: many districts black out on a schedule, and knowing the schedule lets you plan around it. Others fail randomly, which requires a different strategy.
  4. Log the longest single outage. This is the number that decides your backup system, not the total hours off in the month.
  5. Ask two neighbours — the butcher, the welder, the shop with a fridge — what their longest outage was in the last twelve months. Their answer tells you the worst case you must design for.

After one month you can draw three conclusions:

If your log showsYour grid isYour backup strategy
Total outages under 6 hours per month, longest single under 2 hoursReliableSmall battery bank or a small generator. Enough to ride through short events.
Total 6–24 hours per month, longest single 2–6 hoursModerateProper battery bank sized for 12 hours, or a generator sized for full load and run on demand.
Total over 24 hours per month, longest single over 6 hoursUnreliableSolar + battery sized for 24 hours, plus a generator as second line. Or relocate the project.
Do not skip the measurement. A cold room designed for a reliable grid, built on an unreliable one, is a room full of rotten meat waiting for the first long blackout. The month you spend logging is worth more than the extra ,000 you might spend on a bigger battery.

Lesson 2.3

The three classes of backup, and what each one really costs

There is no single right answer for backup power. There are three classes of solution, and the correct one depends on the grid reliability you measured in Lesson 2.2 and the value of the meat you are protecting.

Class 1 — Small battery bank, no generator

A battery bank sized for 4–8 hours of refrigeration load, charged from the grid when it is available and discharged during outages. No generator. Simplest to operate, no fuel, no maintenance of a diesel engine.

Best for: grids with outages under 6 hours per month and rarely longer than 2 hours. Districts in urban or peri-urban areas with reasonably reliable supply.

Indicative cost: ,500–3,500 for a 5 kWh lithium battery bank plus inverter.

Limitation: a single 12-hour outage on a hot day will drain the bank and the room will warm up.

Class 2 — Generator, sized for full load

A diesel generator sized to run the refrigeration unit, lights and pumps. Starts manually or automatically when the grid fails, runs on fuel until the grid returns.

Best for: grids with outages of 2–8 hours per event, of moderate frequency. The generator runs for a few hours per week rather than permanently.

Indicative cost: ,000–6,000 for a 10–20 kVA generator plus automatic transfer switch, plus –400 per month in fuel at typical use.

Limitation: fuel is a running cost forever. Diesel price spikes hurt. The generator itself needs servicing, oil changes and eventual replacement. In areas with frequent short outages, diesel consumption is high.

Class 3 — Solar + battery + generator (hybrid)

Solar panels charge a battery bank daily. During the day the panels run the refrigeration. During outages the battery carries the load. During a long night-time outage, or if the battery is depleted, a small generator kicks in.

Best for: unreliable grids with long outages, or sites with no reliable grid at all. Also best for sites with high sun hours and expensive diesel.

Indicative cost: ,000–15,000 for a 5 kW solar array, 10–20 kWh battery, inverter and small backup generator.

Limitation: high capital cost. Solar panels must be cleaned and can be stolen. The system is more complex and requires a competent installer.

Which is right for a 10-beast room?

For a well-run 10-beast room in a temperate climate, the daily refrigeration load is roughly 30–60 kWh. That is the number that must be covered for 24 hours of autonomy.

Lesson 2.4

What actually happens during a blackout — the physics of a warming room

Understanding what happens inside the room during a power failure is the difference between a business plan that assumes everything is fine and one that plans for the worst hour.

A well-built cold room is not just a box with a refrigeration unit attached. It is a thermal battery. The insulation, the mass of the carcasses, the mass of the wall panels and floor — all of them store cold. When the power fails, the room does not warm up instantly. It warms up gradually, and the rate depends on four things:

  1. How full the room is. A full room warms more slowly than an empty one, because the carcasses themselves hold cold. Counterintuitively, an empty room is more vulnerable.
  2. How good the insulation is. The envelope is covered in Module 3. Panel thickness, panel condition and vapour barrier integrity decide the rate of heat gain.
  3. The outside temperature. A blackout in a 35°C afternoon gains heat three times faster than the same blackout at 20°C night-time.
  4. How many times the door is opened. During a blackout, every door opening is a fresh injection of warm, humid air. The discipline of "keep the door closed" during an outage is not a suggestion — it is the difference between an hour more or an hour less of safe temperature.

Indicative temperatures during a blackout on a full 10-beast room

Assume the room is at 2°C, the outside air is at 30°C, the room is fully loaded (2,500 kg of carcasses), the panels are 100 mm polyurethane, and the door is opened twice during the outage.

The numbers above are indicative. Real rates vary with the four factors listed. The important thing is the shape of the curve: the first 8 hours are almost free, the next 16 hours are critical, and beyond 24 hours the room is a loss either way.

The practical implication: your backup system does not need to keep the room cold forever. It needs to give you time to act. Eight hours of backup on a hot day is enough to ride out most events. Twelve hours is enough to move stock or find a market. Twenty-four hours of autonomy covers almost any blackout you will meet in Africa.

What to do during a blackout

  1. Do not open the door. Every opening costs you an hour of safe time. Post a note on the door. Tell the staff.
  2. Log the start time. Note it in the outage log.
  3. Switch on the backup. Manual or automatic — know which, and make sure everyone on site knows which.
  4. If the outage will clearly exceed your backup capacity, act early. Move meat to a nearby cold room. Sell at a discount. Turn into processed product. Do not wait until the meat is warm to decide.
  5. When power returns, do not open the door immediately. Let the unit pull the room back down to 2–4°C before you resume normal work. Opening the door during pull-down slows it and wastes the recovery.

Lesson 2.5

The electrical installation — done once, done properly

The electrical side of a cold room is where non-electricians most often get into trouble, and where a small mistake becomes an expensive one. Two principles govern the whole installation:

  1. The refrigeration circuit must be on its own dedicated circuit. Not shared with lights, not shared with the butcher's bandsaw, not shared with the house. A dedicated circuit means the compressor is not competing for supply, and a fault elsewhere does not take the cold room down.
  2. The installation must be sized for the future, not just today. If you might add a second room, a freezer or a bandsaw in three years, run cables that can carry that load now. Rewiring a cold room means demolishing walls.

The minimum electrical installation

Wiring the changeover correctly

This is where serious injury and death happen if done wrong. The generator must never be able to feed power back into the grid. Two ways to ensure this:

  • Manual changeover switch. Physically moves the load from "grid" position to "generator" position. When set to generator, the grid connection is open. This is the safe, simple, reliable option for a small operation.
  • Automatic transfer switch (ATS). Senses grid failure and starts the generator, switching load automatically. More expensive and more complex, but no one has to be on site when the power fails.

Whichever you choose, the changeover must be mechanically interlocked — meaning it is physically impossible for both inputs to be connected at once. A changeover switch that only looks like it isolates but can be put in a position where both are live is a lethal hazard to the utility's line workers and to anyone working on the circuit.

Do not do the electrical installation yourself unless you are qualified. This is the one part of the cold room build where the cost of getting it wrong is a life, not a crack. Use a licensed electrician. Require a certificate. Keep the certificate with your other documents — it is needed for insurance, for health inspection, and for any future sale.

Voltage stability — the quiet killer

Compressors are sensitive to low voltage. On a weak grid, or during peak load in a district with a long feeder, voltage can drop to 180 V or below on a nominal 230 V system. Running a compressor at 180 V causes overheating, premature failure and eventually burnout.

If your measured voltage frequently falls below 200 V, install a voltage stabiliser or an automatic voltage regulator (AVR) sized for the compressor. Cost: –800 depending on rating. It is cheaper than one compressor.

Lesson 2.6

Water, drainage and the waste path

Water and drainage are the two systems most often added as afterthoughts and most often the source of a cold room's eventual failure. Every wash-down of the room produces organic waste water that must leave the room and go somewhere safe. Every entry and exit of a carcass is a chance to bring in mud and bacteria.

Water supply

A 10-beast room uses roughly 400–800 litres of water per kill day for wash-down, plus the water used at the kill floor. That is a substantial daily demand. Before building, confirm your source can supply it year-round:

  • Borehole or municipal supply. Preferred. Confirm the yield and the pressure. A dry season test is worth more than a rainy season test.
  • Rainwater harvesting. Useful as a supplement, especially in areas with seasonal rain. A 5,000 L tank is a reasonable minimum. Must be filtered and, for final rinse, treated.
  • River or dam. Acceptable for wash-down, not for final rinse on food contact surfaces without treatment. Requires pumping, which ties back to your power strategy.

Install a simple sand and cartridge filter on the wash-down line if the source is surface water. Install a UV or chlorine dosing system on any line used for food contact surfaces. Both are cheap compared to a health inspection failure.

Drainage

The drainage design has three jobs:

  1. Remove wash-down water quickly. Standing water in a cold room becomes ice, which is a slip hazard and slows work.
  2. Prevent cross-contamination. The drain must not allow waste water from the kill floor or the animal handling area to enter the cold room.
  3. Discharge to a safe point. Never to a bare field, never to a watercourse, never near a well or borehole. To a grease trap then a soakaway, or to a septic system, or to an approved municipal discharge.

The drainage path, in order

COLD ROOM floor drain (with a strainer basket for solids) │ ▼ GREASE TRAP ← removes fats and oils (a simple two-chamber concrete box is enough at this scale) │ ▼ SOAKAWAY ← a pit filled with rubble, or SEPTIC SYSTEM, or approved discharge │ ▼ NOT to a well, borehole, watercourse, or open ground

Floor slope and drain position

The cold room floor must slope to the drain. A slope of 1:100 (1 cm per metre) is enough. The drain should be at the lowest point — usually the centre for a small room, or at one end for a long room. Never leave the drain at a high point or where it can be blocked by the door swing.

Insulated panel floors are flat and smooth. This is good for cleaning but bad for water run-off. If you are building on a slab rather than on panels, screed the slab to slope correctly before the insulation goes down. Getting this right once saves you wet floors forever.

The wash-down routine

During and after every kill day:

  1. Sweep out solids — bone fragments, fat, blood. Into a bin, not into the drain.
  2. Rinse the walls and floor with cold water.
  3. Wash with a food-safe detergent, working top-down.
  4. Rinse with clean water.
  5. Sanitise with a food-safe sanitiser (chlorine, peracetic acid or quaternary ammonium) at the correct contact time.
  6. Squeegee water toward the drain.
  7. Leave the room to dry and cool back to temperature before the next carcass enters.

Between kills, a light daily clean is enough. Between batches, a full deep clean. Module 7 covers this in detail.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. Why is a cold room built in isolation — without a clean kill floor and a covered rail to the room — a common failure?

Correct: hot carcass through open ground. Every metre of exposed transport adds heat load and surface contamination. The chain matters as much as the room.

2. What is the only reliable way to know how reliable your district's power supply actually is?

Correct: log for one month. Memory is unreliable. A written log of every outage duration and the longest single event is the only defensible basis for a backup plan.

3. During a blackout, which of these actions extends the safe time the most?

Correct: keep the door closed. Every opening injects warm humid air and costs an hour of safe time. Running a fan without a heat exchanger just moves warm air around.

4. What is the most dangerous mistake in the electrical installation of a cold room?

Correct: a non-interlocked changeover. Back-feeding kills utility line workers and anyone working on the circuit. The changeover must be mechanically interlocked so both inputs can never be live at once.

5. Where must wash-down water from a cold room finally discharge?

Correct: grease trap → soakaway / septic / approved discharge. Never to a watercourse, a well, a borehole or open ground near one.

Practical assignment — Module 2

Produce a site plan and a power strategy for your own cold room project. This is a paper exercise plus a two-week measurement. It does not require building anything.

Submit:

  1. A hand-drawn site plan showing the four movements from Lesson 2.1: live animal, hot carcass, chilled product, and water/waste. Label each movement with arrows. State in one sentence whether any movement crosses another or leaves cover.
  2. A photograph of the actual site, taken from the road, showing the slope and the intended building position.
  3. A two-week power log: date, time off, time on, duration, total minutes per day. Note the longest single outage observed and any short flickers.
  4. A one-page power strategy: which class of backup (1, 2, or 3) you will use and why, with indicative costs from at least two suppliers.
  5. A simple drainage sketch: cold room floor drain → grease trap → discharge point. State the discharge point and why it is safe.
  6. One paragraph (150 words) naming the single biggest site risk you have identified and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 3 and 6. The log is evidence. The risk paragraph is judgement. Everything else can be copied from a book; those two cannot.
