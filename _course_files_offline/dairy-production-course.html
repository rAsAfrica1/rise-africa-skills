Practical Reference

The chart above is your first business decision tool. It shows why the choice between indigenous animals, crossbreds, and improved dairy stock is a margin decision, not a sentimental one. A crossbred cow producing 3 litres per day at a farm-gate price of US.50 per litre earns about US$1.50 per day, or roughly US$45 per month before feed. An improved dairy cow producing 7 litres earns about US$3.50 per day, or US$105 per month. That difference of US$60 per month per animal is the entire gap between a hobby herd and a business. Everything that follows in this reference block is designed to protect that margin.

Read the break-even line carefully. The improved dairy cow has the highest absolute margin but also the highest break-even point. If your feed supply collapses, or mastitis cuts yield by half, the improved cow moves into loss first. The improved dairy goat has the lowest break-even point in the table, which is why goat dairy is often the smarter entry point for a first-time producer with limited capital and no cold chain. Goat milk also commands a premium in many African urban markets because of lactose-sensitivity demand and specialty cheese buyers.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Antibiotic Residues and Milk Handling

Never sell or process milk from an animal under antibiotic treatment or within the withdrawal period. Antibiotic residues in milk can cause severe allergic reactions in consumers, trigger antimicrobial resistance, and get your milk rejected by every processor and regulator in the region. One contaminated batch can end a supply contract permanently.

  • Record the drug name, dose, date given, and withdrawal period in your herd book. Withdrawal is typically 3â7 days for intramammary tubes and longer for injectables â confirm on the label, not from memory.
  • Label treated animals physically (leg band, coloured chalk) so no milker makes a mistake at 5am.
  • Milk from treated animals must be discarded, not fed to calves unless the label permits it, and never pooled with saleable milk.
  • Raw milk carries Brucella, Listeria, E. coli and Mycobacterium bovis. Pasteurise (63Â°C for 30 minutes or 72Â°C for 15 seconds) before any sale to consumers, and always before making yogurt or cheese.
  • Use clean, potable water for udder washing and equipment rinsing. Dirty water is a direct contamination route.
  • Wear gloves when treating mastitis and wash hands before handling milk. Zoonotic transfer is real and preventable.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm three local prices before you buy. Call three feed suppliers, three animal sellers, and three buyers. Record the dates. Prices shift with season and fuel cost.
  2. Cool milk to 4Â°C within two hours. If you have no chiller, use a chest freezer with ice packs or a wet-cloth evaporative cooler, and sell the same day.
  3. Run a CMT test on every milking animal weekly. Catch sub-clinical mastitis before it becomes clinical and before yield drops.
  4. Keep a herd book for every animal. Breeding dates, calving dates, milk yield, treatments, withdrawal dates. No record, no business.
  5. Balance the ration for energy and protein. Underfed cows drop yield within 48 hours. Monitor body condition score and keep it between 2.5 and 3.0.
  6. Pasteurise before processing. Yogurt, cheese and butter all begin with pasteurised milk. This is non-negotiable for food safety and shelf life.
  7. Secure at least two buyers before your first milking day. One processor and one direct route (neighbours, shops, restaurants) removes single-buyer risk.
  8. Reinvest 30% of monthly margin into feed and animal health. This is the compounding engine that moves you from 2 animals to 10+.
