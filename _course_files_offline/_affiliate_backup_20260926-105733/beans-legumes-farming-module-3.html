Lesson 1

The IPM Approach for Legumes

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Set the IPM order for legumes
  • Understand the critical spray windows
  • Avoid the trap of routine spraying

Legumes are attacked by a specific set of pests and diseases, and each has a critical window. Spraying at the wrong time is wasted money; spraying at the right time saves the crop.

The IPM order

  1. Resistant varieties. Always the first line.
  2. Rotation. Break pest and disease cycles with a cereal or a different legume.
  3. Sanitation. Remove crop residue; destroy infected plants.
  4. Scouting. Weekly walk-and-check through the whole season.
  5. Biological. Encourage natural enemies.
  6. Targeted spray only at threshold, only at the correct growth stage.

Lesson 2

Pod Borer — the Main Yield Killer

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Identify pod borer damage (Maruca, Helicoverpa)
  • Time sprays correctly — the flowering window is critical
  • Use Bt and other biological options first

Pod borer (Maruca vitrata) — cowpea, beans, pigeon pea

The larva webs flowers and pods together, feeding inside. Damage in cowpea can reach 80% if unmanaged. The insect has a 3-week life cycle and multiple generations per season.

African bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) — beans, groundnut, soybean

The larva bores into pods and eats the seed. Often 1 larva per pod, but heavy pressure can mean 20–40% pod damage.

Control

  1. Scout weekly from flowering. Walk and inspect 10 plants at 10 points. Count larvae per plant. A threshold of 1 larva per plant justifies action.
  2. Bt spray (Bacillus thuringiensis). Effective against young larvae. Safe, no PHI issues. Apply weekly during pod fill.
  3. Neem oil as a repellent and growth regulator.
  4. Chemical sprays at flowering. Effective actives: chlorantraniliprole, spinosad, emamectin, lambda-cyhalothrin. Rotate actives to prevent resistance. Respect PHI.
  5. Early harvesting as a last resort if pressure is extreme and the pods are close to maturity.

Lesson 3

Bean Fly and Stem Maggot

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify bean fly damage (dead heart in seedlings)
  • Use cultural and seed-treatment control
  • Time the seedling-stage scouting

Bean fly (Ophiomyia phaseoli)

A small black fly that lays eggs on bean leaves. The larva mines down the leaf and into the stem, where it feeds on the vascular tissue. Damage causes stunting, yellowing, wilting and death of young plants. Common in East and southern Africa.

Signs

  • Small yellow blotches and mines on leaves
  • Split stems showing tunnels and small larvae
  • Wilting or death of seedlings 2–4 weeks after emergence
  • Yellowed, stunted plants that do not recover

Control

  1. Seed treatment. Imidacloprid or thiamethoxam seed dressing controls early infestation. Applied before planting.
  2. Early planting. Bean fly pressure is highest in warm, dry periods. Planting early with the rains reduces attack.
  3. Resistant varieties. Some bean lines show tolerance. Ask research institutes.
  4. Foliar spray at 2–3 weeks. Only if scouting confirms high pressure. Dimethoate or similar.
  5. Sanitation. Remove and destroy crop residue after harvest.
Lesson 4

Storage Weevils

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Identify bean weevil (Acanthoscelides) and cowpea weevil (Callosobruchus)
  • Use hermetic storage and safe treatments
  • Decide between chemical and non-chemical storage

Damage

Stored legume seed is attacked within weeks by weevils. The female lays eggs on the seed surface; the larva bores in and eats the inside; the adult emerges leaving a round hole. Losses of 30–80% in 6 months are common with unprotected stored beans.

Control

  1. Hermetic storage (PICS bags, GrainPro). Best option. 100 kg bag costs $3–8, protects seed for 12 months without any chemicals. The sealed bag depletes oxygen, killing insects.
  2. Solarisation. Dry seed in a thin layer under clear plastic in full sun. The heat (60–70°C) kills weevil eggs and larvae. Do this before storage.
  3. Diatomaceous earth. Non-toxic powder mixed with seed. Abrades insect cuticle, causing death by desiccation. 1–2 kg per tonne of seed.
  4. Chemical fumigants (phosphine). Effective but require proper training and equipment. Not for home use.
  5. Botanical (neem, tobacco). Traditional methods. Mixed results — often reduce but do not eliminate.
  6. Freezing. Freeze small lots of seed for 2–3 days to kill all stages.

Lesson 5

Diseases — Angular Leaf Spot, Rust, Virus

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Identify the four main legume diseases
  • Use varietal resistance and rotation as first-line defence
  • Time fungicide sprays only when justified

Angular leaf spot (Phaeoisariopsis griseola) — beans

Angular brown spots on leaves, bordered by veins. Pods develop circular brown lesions. Worse in wet weather. Cause of 20–50% yield loss in susceptible varieties.

Control: resistant varieties (many improved bean lines), rotation, remove infected residue, fungicide (chlorothalonil or mancozeb) if wet weather persists.

Bean rust (Uromyces appendiculatus)

Small reddish-brown pustules on leaf undersides. Leaves yellow, dry, fall. Worse in humid weather and dense planting.

Control: resistant varieties, wider spacing, fungicide (triazole or strobilurin) if heavy.

Common bacterial blight (Xanthomonas)

Water-soaked spots that turn brown with a yellow halo. Seed-borne. Manage with certified seed, rotation, and avoiding overhead irrigation.

Bean common mosaic virus (BCMV)

Mottled leaves, curled, stunted plants, reduced pod set. Transmitted by aphids and seed. No cure.

Control: certified virus-free seed, resistant varieties, control aphids.

Groundnut-specific: Rosette virus

Stunted plants with bunched, yellow leaves. Transmitted by aphids. Can wipe out a crop. Manage with resistant varieties, early planting, and aphid control.

Groundnut-specific: Aflatoxin

A food-safety issue, not a disease of the plant in the field sense. Aspergillus fungi produce aflatoxin in groundnut, especially when the crop is drought-stressed or dried slowly. Export buyers test for aflatoxin and reject over the threshold. Manage with irrigation in dry spells, rapid drying after harvest, and proper storage.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. When is the critical spray window for pod borer?

Correct: flowering and pod fill. The larvae feed on flowers and pods.

2. What is the spray threshold for pod borer?

Correct: 1 larva per plant. Scout weekly from flowering.

3. How do you prevent bean fly?

Correct: seed treatment. Applied before planting, controls early infestation.

4. What is the best storage method for legume seed?

Correct: hermetic bags. Kills insects without chemicals, losses under 2%.

5. What is aflatoxin and why does it matter?

Correct: a toxin from Aspergillus fungi. Prevented by rapid drying and dry storage.
