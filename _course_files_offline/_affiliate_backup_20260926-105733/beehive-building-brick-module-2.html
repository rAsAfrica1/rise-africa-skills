Module 2

Site, Orientation and Sun Exposure

A fixed hive cannot be moved. Every mistake in siting is permanent. This module is the survey that decides whether masonry is right for a given piece of ground, and — if it is — how to place every hive on it.

Module 2 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Survey a site for the four conditions a fixed hive requires
  • Orient hive entrances to control sun and wind exposure
  • Plan hive spacing, working space, and bee flight paths
  • Design a drainage solution for a masonry hive on a slope
  • Use a simple water-level method to check for level ground
  • Produce a marked site plan for a 10-hive fixed apiary

Lesson 2.1

The four site conditions for a fixed hive

A masonry hive weighs 300–800 kg and cannot be moved. If the site fails any of the four conditions below, build elsewhere or use wooden hives on stands.

1. Level ground

The base slab must be level to within 10 mm across the whole hive footprint. On a slope, even 5%, the slab will settle unevenly and the hive will crack within 2–3 years. Level the pad before the first brick is laid.

2. High and well drained

The base of the hive must sit at least 150 mm above the surrounding ground, and the surrounding ground must fall away from the hive on all sides. Water pooling at the base of a masonry hive causes the bees to abscond and the mortar to soften.

3. Shade from afternoon sun

A brick hive in full afternoon sun reaches internal temperatures of 45 °C+. That is a death sentence for the colony in hot climates. The hive must have shade at 1–4 p.m., either from existing trees, a shade roof, or a wall on the west side.

4. Away from paths and dwellings

A bee flight path crosses 5–10 m at head height in front of the entrance. Do not point entrances at footpaths, doorways, water taps, or livestock pens. Point them at a hedge, a wall, or open field.

The four-condition rule: all four must be satisfied. If any one fails, either fix it or pick a different site. A fixed hive on a bad site is not a hive — it is an expensive lesson.

Lesson 2.2

Checking level ground with a water level

You do not need a surveyor's level. A clear plastic hose and water will give you millimetre accuracy for the cost of a few dollars.

The method

  1. Buy 5–10 m of clear plastic hose (aquarium hose, 8 mm internal diameter).
  2. Fill it with water, making sure no air bubbles are trapped. Tape the ends if needed to stop spillage while moving.
  3. Hold one end against a peg at your reference point. Mark where the water level sits.
  4. Walk the other end to a second peg. Wait for the water to stop moving (5–10 seconds). Mark where the water level sits at that end.
  5. The two marks are at exactly the same height above sea level. The difference between each mark and its own ground surface tells you the fall across the site.
  6. Repeat across the hive pad corners and the apiary area on a rough grid.

What the numbers tell you

Level difference across the hive padAction
Under 10 mmBuild directly on the ground with a rubble pad
10–50 mmLevel with a mortar bed before starting the first course
50–200 mmBuild a levelling plinth (small concrete base) and build the hive on top
Over 200 mmCut and fill to a level pad, or use a different site
Do not fill a slope with soil. Loose fill settles and cracks the hive. If the site is not level enough, either excavate the high side or build a concrete pad that is level on top.

Lesson 2.3

Orientation — sun, wind and bee flight

The entrance faces the direction the bees will fly in and out. Orientation affects three things: the hive's internal temperature through the day, the ability of the bees to defend the entrance, and the beekeeper's comfort during inspections.

Sun

North of the equator: entrance facing north or northeast. South of the equator: entrance facing south or southeast. This aims the entrance away from the midday sun and toward morning sun. In equatorial Africa (within 5° of the equator), most beekeepers choose east-facing entrances to catch morning warmth without harsh afternoon sun.

Wind

Do not face the entrance into the prevailing wind. Wind blows rain into the hive and drives warm air out. Face the entrance away from the prevailing wind, or provide a windbreak (hedge, wall, or thick vegetation) on the windward side.

Bee flight path

Point the entrance at a hedge or wall 5–10 m away. The barrier forces the bees to climb above head height before scattering. Point the entrance at open sky and the bees will fly across paths at head height.

Lesson 2.4

Spacing and working space

Fixed hives are laid out once. Get the spacing right or you will work with the consequences for decades.

Recommended spacing

  • Between hives: 2–4 m. Closer causes drifting and robbing. Wider means more walking.
  • Between rows: 4–6 m. Gives a bee corridor between rows so flight paths do not tangle.
  • Working space behind: 1 m minimum. You will stand here, suited up, with tools, at every inspection. Two metres is better.
  • Working space in front: 1 m minimum, then a hedge or wall.

Lesson 2.5

Drainage for a fixed hive

Even on a level pad, water arrives from rain, roof runoff, and surface flow. The hive base must not sit in water.

The three-layer defence

  1. Pad above ground. The concrete pad must sit 150 mm above the surrounding ground level. Raise it with compacted rubble underneath.
  2. Perimeter drain. A shallow V-drain around the pad, 150 mm deep and 200 mm wide, carried away from the hive to a soakaway or a natural slope. This is the layer most often forgotten.
  3. Roof overhang. The hive roof should overhang the walls by at least 100 mm on all four sides. This keeps rain off the walls, which keeps the brick dry, which stops rising damp.
The drainage rule: water must not stand anywhere within 1 m of the hive base. If you cannot achieve that naturally, build a drain. If you cannot build a drain, do not build the hive there.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. What are the four conditions a fixed hive site must satisfy?

Correct: level, drained, shaded, isolated from paths. Any one missing and the fixed hive will underperform.

2. In which direction should hive entrances face in East Africa (near the equator)?

Correct: east or northeast. Catches morning warmth, avoids midday and afternoon heat.

3. What is the correct spacing between fixed hives?

Correct: 2–4 m. Closer causes drifting and robbing; wider means more walking.

4. How much working space should be behind each hive?

Correct: 1 m minimum. You will stand there at every inspection with tools and a bee suit.

5. Why must a masonry hive pad sit at least 150 mm above ground?

Correct: keep the base dry. Water pooling at the base is the fastest way to lose the colony and the structure.

Practical assignment — Module 2

Produce a marked site plan and a levelling record for a real fixed-hive apiary.

  1. A plan drawing to approximate scale, north marked, showing boundaries, existing trees and structures, and the four-condition assessment.
  2. The water-level survey — at least 4 readings taken across the pad and around the apiary area, with the difference in mm at each point.
  3. The chosen pad level and how you will achieve it — direct build, mortar bed, plinth, or cut-and-fill.
  4. Hive layout for 10 hives showing spacing, working spaces, and bee flight paths.
  5. Windbreak or hedge on the windward side, and shade plan for the afternoon sun.
  6. Drainage plan — how the pad is raised, where the perimeter drain runs, and where it discharges.
  7. One paragraph (150 words) stating whether this site satisfies all four conditions and, if not, what you will do about it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 7. Anyone can draw a plan; the survey and the decision are the real test.
