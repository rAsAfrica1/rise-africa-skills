Module 4

The Insulated Ceiling and Vapour Barrier

The ceiling is where 60–70% of a mushroom house's heat flows. It is also where condensation forms and drips onto the crop. Get the ceiling right and the grow room works. Get it wrong and it fails, season after season.

Module 4 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Size ceiling insulation for your climate and species
  • Install a vapour barrier correctly on the warm side of the insulation
  • Prevent condensation drip onto the crop
  • Provide the correct air exchange rate without losing heat or humidity
  • Handle penetrations, lights and misting lines through the ceiling
  • Produce a ceiling specification for your own mushroom house

Lesson 4.1

Why the ceiling matters most

Heat flows from hot to cold. In a mushroom house held at 20 °C, the ceiling is where most heat either enters (from a hot roof) or escapes (in cool highlands).

The three heat flows through a ceiling

  1. Conduction through the ceiling material. A concrete or brick ceiling slab conducts heat. Without insulation, this is the largest flow.
  2. Radiation from the hot roof underside. The metal roof reaches 50–60 °C in sun. It radiates heat downward onto the ceiling.
  3. Convection — hot air rising in the room. Warm moist air from the grow room rises, hits the ceiling, and either condenses or escapes.

A well-insulated ceiling stops all three. A poorly-insulated ceiling lets all three through.

The ceiling rule: the ceiling is insulated; the walls are not. Walls provide thermal mass. Ceilings provide insulation. Confusing these two functions costs you the crop.

Lesson 4.2

Insulation sizing

By climate

ClimateCeiling insulationNotes
Hot tropical lowland75–100 mm PUR foam boardStops solar gain from a hot roof
Warm, dry75 mm PURPlus ventilated attic space
Cool highland100 mm PURStops heat loss on cold nights
Cold highland100–125 mm PURPlus an active heater as backup

Insulation materials

  • Polyurethane (PUR) board — best insulation per mm. Standard for mushroom houses.
  • XPS board — good, resists moisture better than EPS. Acceptable.
  • EPS board — cheap, but absorbs water over time. Not ideal.
  • Mineral wool — good thermal performance but absorbs moisture. Must be fully sealed behind a vapour barrier.
  • Straw bale ceiling — traditional, cheap, R-value equivalent of ~50 mm PUR. Works in dry climates. Not in humid conditions (mould).

For a small African mushroom house, 75–100 mm PUR board is the correct choice.

Lesson 4.3

The vapour barrier — warm side, always

The vapour barrier has exactly one job: stop warm moist air from reaching the cold side of the insulation, where it will condense into liquid water.

Where the warm side is

In a mushroom house held at 20 °C in a tropical climate:

  • Inside the house: 20 °C, 85–92% relative humidity (very moist).
  • Outside the house (above the ceiling, in the attic): can be 30–45 °C in the day, or 15 °C at night.

When the outside is hotter than the inside (daytime in the tropics), the warm side is outside. The vapour barrier goes on the outside of the insulation (in the attic space).

When the outside is cooler than the inside (cool highland nights), the warm side is inside the house. The vapour barrier goes on the inside face of the insulation.

The correct placement for tropical Africa

For most African mushroom houses, the warm moist air is inside the grow room, because the room is held at 85–92% humidity. The warm side is therefore the inside face of the ceiling. The vapour barrier goes on the inside of the insulation — between the insulation and the grow-room air.

Vapour barrier placement is determined by which side is moist, not which side is warm. In a mushroom house, the moist side is the grow room, so the barrier goes on the grow-room side of the insulation. Getting this wrong saturates the insulation within months.

Materials

  • Polyethylene sheet, 0.20 mm minimum, UV-stabilised. Standard.
  • Aluminium foil-faced insulation board — the foil serves as a vapour barrier if all joints are foil-taped.
  • Self-adhesive membrane for penetrations and junctions.

Lesson 4.4

Preventing condensation drip

Even with good insulation, the ceiling is the coolest surface in the room (except the walls). Moist air contacts it and condenses. Condensation dripping onto growing mushrooms is a contamination disaster.

Three defences

  1. Avoid the cold ceiling. Insulation keeps the ceiling surface near the room air temperature, which reduces condensation.
  2. Move the moist air. Slow air circulation prevents a layer of saturated air from building up at the ceiling.
  3. Catch what does form. A slight slope in the ceiling toward a collection point, or a thin absorbent layer on the ceiling surface.

The ceiling surface

The grow-room face of the ceiling must be:

  • Smooth and cleanable — food-grade insulated panels are ideal.
  • Slightly sloped — 1:200 toward one edge, so any condensation runs to the wall rather than dripping.
  • Light-coloured — a bright ceiling reflects light down onto the crop.

Air circulation

A small fan moving air slowly across the ceiling (0.2–0.5 m/s) prevents a saturated layer from forming. The fan runs continuously during fruiting. Not during spawn run (which needs higher CO₂ and less air movement).

Lesson 4.5

Penetrations and air exchange

Common penetrations through the ceiling

  • Misting water lines
  • Electrical cables for lighting and sensors
  • Exhaust fan ductwork
  • Air inlet ductwork
  • Thermometer/hygrometer cables

Sealing every penetration

Every penetration through the ceiling insulation is a hole in the vapour barrier. Rules:

  1. Group penetrations where possible — one hole for four pipes is better than four holes.
  2. Use a solid sleeve (PVC pipe) through the insulation. The pipe protects the insulation and gives a clean edge to seal to.
  3. Seal both sides of the penetration with self-adhesive membrane or a proper vapour-barrier boot.
  4. Never leave a raw hole with a wire through it. That is a leak in both the barrier and the insulation.

Air exchange — the balance

Mushrooms need fresh air to flush CO₂ down. But fresh air also carries heat and moisture away. The air exchange rate is the balance between these needs.

  • Fresh air rate: 4–8 air changes per hour during fruiting. 2–4 during spawn run.
  • Inlet: low on one side, screened, with adjustable damper.
  • Outlet: high on the opposite side, screened, with adjustable damper.
  • Fan: small, low-power. Runs continuously during fruiting.

Do not confuse ventilation (air exchange for CO₂) with circulation (moving air inside the room for humidity uniformity). Both are needed, but they use different fans.

The ceiling rule: thick insulation, warm-side vapour barrier, every penetration sealed, slow circulation, controlled air exchange. Five details. Miss one and the grow room either drips, loses heat, or fails to control CO₂ — all of which reduce yield.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. Where does the vapour barrier go in a mushroom house in tropical Africa?

Correct: grow-room side. The barrier goes on the moist side of the insulation, which is the high-humidity grow room.

2. Why is the ceiling insulated but not the walls?

Correct: different functions. Brick walls stabilise temperature by mass. The ceiling stops heat loss/gain by insulation.

3. What is the correct ceiling insulation thickness for a mushroom house in hot tropical lowlands?

Correct: 75–100 mm PUR. Stops heat gain from the hot roof underside.

4. What air exchange rate is correct during fruiting?

Correct: 4–8 ACH during fruiting. Enough to flush CO₂ down to under 800 ppm without losing heat and humidity.

5. How should every ceiling penetration be sealed?

Correct: solid sleeve + membrane both sides. Every penetration is a potential vapour barrier failure. Seal them properly or the insulation saturates.

Practical assignment — Module 4

Produce a ceiling specification for your own mushroom house.

  1. Ceiling insulation material and thickness, with reasoning based on your climate and species.
  2. Vapour barrier placement — inside or outside — with reasoning about which side is moist.
  3. The ceiling surface material — insulated panel, plastered concrete, or other — and its cleanability.
  4. Ceiling slope and where condensation runs.
  5. Circulation fan plan — size, position, airflow rate.
  6. Air exchange plan — inlet position, outlet position, fan size, air changes per hour.
  7. A list of every penetration through the ceiling and how each is sealed.
  8. Two supplier quotations for insulation board and vapour barrier membrane.
  9. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest thermal or moisture risk at your ceiling and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 9. Vapour barrier placement is the single most critical decision; the paragraph is where you commit to a strategy.
