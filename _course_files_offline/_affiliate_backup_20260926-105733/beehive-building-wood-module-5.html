Module 5

Hive Stands, Roofs and Rain Protection

A hive is not finished when the box is built. It needs a stand to keep it off the ground, a roof to keep rain out, and the details that decide whether it lasts ten years or two.

Module 5 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Build a hive stand that keeps the hive level and off the ground
  • Choose between single-hive stands, hive benches, and fixed platforms
  • Build a Langstroth roof and a top-bar hive roof to shed water properly
  • Provide shading and ventilation for hot climates
  • Prevent the three major causes of hive failure: rain ingress, termite attack, theft
  • Complete the hive stand and roof for your own build

Lesson 5.1

The stand — not optional

A hive on the ground is a hive in trouble. Ground contact causes three problems:

  1. Termites — every timber in contact with soil is a termite target. Untreated timber lasts 1–3 years. Even treated timber fails eventually.
  2. Dampness — the base of the hive stays damp, encourages mould, and rots the bottom board.
  3. Pests — ants, rodents, and some reptiles enter the hive more easily when it sits on the ground.

Height

At least 300 mm above ground. 400–500 mm is better — this is comfortable working height and keeps the hive clear of splashing rain and ground-level predators.

Stand types

TypeBest forAdvantagesDisadvantages
Single-hive trestle (4 legs + platform)Langstroth, top-barSimple, moveable, cheapEach hive needs its own
Hive bench (long table, multiple hives)Multiple LangstrothShares structure, quick to build manyCannot move individual hives
Fixed platform (masonry or concrete)Masonry hivesPermanent, termite-proof, levelVery heavy, cannot move
Suspended stand (chain or cable)High theft areasAnt-proof, theft-resistantMore complex, expensive

Stand construction (single Langstroth)

  • Legs: four, 50 × 50 mm treated timber or 40 mm steel angle, 500 mm long.
  • Cross beams: two, 505 mm × 50 × 50 mm, at the top of the legs.
  • Platform: 550 × 450 × 20 mm exterior plywood or timber planks.
  • Feet: each leg sits on a concrete block, ceramic tile, or a metal plate — not on soil.

Ant prevention

Ants are the single most common cause of colony absconding in African hives. Two defences:

  • Ant moat: a shallow tray of used engine oil or water around each leg. Kills or prevents ants from crossing.
  • Legs standing in tins: tins filled with oil, legs resting on a stone inside the tin. Ants cannot cross.

Ant moats must be checked weekly — if they dry out, ants cross freely.

The stand rule: no hive on the ground. Stand 300 mm+ high, legs on impermeable feet, ant moats in place. This is the cheapest insurance in the apiary.

Lesson 5.2

The roof — protecting everything below

The roof is the most important single piece of the hive. If the roof leaks, the bees move out. The whole hive is designed around the roof.

Roof dimensions (Langstroth)

PartDimensions
Top board560 × 460 × 12 mm (or 20 mm for durability)
RimFour sides, 540 mm and 440 mm, 80 mm tall, 20 mm thick
Metal coveringGalvanised sheet, wrapped down and under
Overhang50–80 mm on all sides

The metal covering — mandatory

Bare wood roofs rot within 2–3 years in tropical Africa. Galvanised sheet is the standard. Alternatively:

  • Aluminium sheet: lighter, does not rust, more expensive.
  • Bitumen felt: cheap but degrades in UV within 3–5 years.
  • Thatch: traditional, good insulation, needs annual maintenance, harbours insects.

Ventilation

A void of 20 mm between the top bars and the underside of the roof provides the bee space. In hot climates, add a ventilated inner cover with a screened hole or a slot with mesh. This lets hot air escape without letting bees out or robbers in.

Roof colour

Paint the roof white or light colour. Reflects heat. Reduces internal temperature by 3–6 °C in full sun. Free efficiency.

Lesson 5.3

Rain protection and drainage

Roof overhang

Overhang must extend 50–100 mm beyond the hive body on all sides. This keeps rain off the walls and keeps the joint between the roof and the top of the box dry.

Slope

Roofs should slope very slightly — 2–5° — toward one side or the back so water runs off. A perfectly flat metal roof can hold water at the seams in heavy rain.

Drip edge

A slight downward turn of the metal at the roof edge stops water clinging and running back under. Simple to add with a folding tool.

Hive level

A hive that tilts the wrong way collects water at the entrance. Tilt the hive slightly forward (2–3°) so any rain that does get in drains out the entrance. Do NOT tilt backward.

Site drainage

  • Ensure the apiary floor drains away from each hive.
  • In wet areas, build each hive on a raised pad 100–150 mm above surrounding ground.
  • Keep vegetation trimmed around the hive so air can circulate under it.

Lesson 5.4

Shade and heat management

Bees can survive at 45 °C internal temperature but they cannot work. A hive in full afternoon sun: - Produces less honey (bees spend energy fanning instead of foraging) - Swarms more often (heat stress triggers swarm impulse) - Has shorter comb lifespan (wax softens at high temperatures)

Shade options

MethodEffectivenessCost
Existing treesExcellentFree

Hive in long grass/windbreakModerateFree
No shadePoor$0

Shade roof design

  • Simple corrugated sheet on four posts, 2.2 m high at the front, 2.8 m at the back.
  • Overhangs the hive by 500 mm on the sunny side.
  • Open on all sides for airflow.
  • Light colour on top.

What NOT to do

  • Do not enclose the hive in a shed. Bees need open airflow. A closed shed in hot weather bakes the hive.
  • Do not plant dense trees directly overhead. Falling branches and dripping sap disturb the hive.
  • Do not use black shade cloth. It absorbs heat and radiates it back.

Lesson 5.5

Theft prevention and the finished hive

Theft — a real problem

Wooden hives are stolen for resale, firewood, or honey harvest. If theft is a serious risk in your area, masonry hives are the answer (see Beehive Building — Brick). For wooden hives, the defences are:

  • Brand or mark each hive with a unique identifier (painted initials + number). Makes resale harder.
  • Fixed stand bolted to the ground. Slows removal.
  • Apiary near the house. The best theft prevention is being there.
  • Dogs or livestock in the apiary area. A dog warns of night visitors.
  • Chain the hive to the stand or to a ground anchor. Adds 5 minutes to a theft attempt, which is often enough.

The finished hive — final checklist

  1. Box is square, joints tight, bee space correct.
  2. Frames lift out cleanly with 8 mm bee space at the box walls.
  3. Roof covered with metal, overhangs 50–100 mm, painted white or light.
  4. Inner cover in place with ventilation hole (hot climates).
  5. Floor solid, entrance slot cut, entrance reducer available.
  6. Stand 300–500 mm above ground, level, on impermeable feet.
  7. Ant moats installed and working.
  8. Shade provided for afternoon sun.
  9. Hive branded/marked with unique ID.
  10. Interior clean, no paint, no treated timber touching bees.
The completed hive rule: a hive is not finished until it is on a stand, has a metal-covered roof, has an ant moat, has shade, and is branded. All five. Miss one and you have a hive waiting to fail.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. What is the minimum height for a hive stand?

Correct: 300 mm. Keeps termites out, keeps the base dry, discourages ground pests.

2. What is the mandatory roof covering in tropical Africa?

Correct: galvanised metal. Bare wood roofs rot within 2–3 years in the tropics.

3. What is the simplest and most effective ant defence for a hive stand?

Correct: ant moats. Cheap, effective. But must be checked weekly — if they dry out, ants cross freely.

4. Which direction should the hive tilt?

Correct: slightly forward. Water that gets in drains out the entrance. Backward tilt traps water.

5. Why paint the roof white or light colour?

Correct: reflects heat. Free efficiency. Dark roofs bake the hive.

Practical assignment — Module 5

Complete the stand and roof for your hive.

  1. Your stand design with a dimensioned sketch. State the type (single trestle, bench, fixed platform) and why.
  2. Photographs of the stand after construction, with a level on it.
  3. Photographs of the ant moats — installed and filled.
  4. Photographs of the roof — top board, rim, metal covering, showing overhang all round.
  5. Your shade plan — how the hive will be protected from afternoon sun.
  6. Your hive marking (brand or paint) with unique ID.
  7. Your site plan showing hive position, stand, shade, water access.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the single biggest risk to your hive's long life and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 4 and 8. The roof is the most critical element of the hive's survival; the paragraph is where you commit to a risk decision.
