Lesson 1

Dehaulming and Skin Set

In this lesson
  • Understand what dehaulming does
  • Time it correctly
  • Avoid the mistake of harvesting too early

What dehaulming is

Dehaulming means cutting off the leaves and stems (the haulm) 10–14 days before harvest. The crop stops growing; the tuber skins thicken and set, which dramatically reduces damage during lifting and extends storage life.

When to dehaulm

  • When leaves start to yellow naturally (physiological maturity)
  • Or, for blight risk: immediately when blight appears and the crop is close to harvest
  • 10–14 days before planned harvest date

Why it matters

  • Skin set reduces bruising during lifting by 40–60%
  • Bruised tubers rot in storage
  • Thicker skins extend storage life from 2 months to 5+ months
The rule: never harvest immediately after dehaulming. Wait 10–14 days. Lifting early is one of the most common causes of storage rot in African potato farming.
Lesson 2

Harvesting Without Damage

In this lesson
  • Lift potatoes without bruising
  • Time harvest for cool, dry conditions
  • Handle tubers gently

Timing

  • Harvest in cool, dry weather — not after rain
  • Wait until skins are set (see Lesson 1)
  • If blight has struck, harvest early — the longer tubers stay in contact with blight spores, the more rot

Lifting

  • Use a fork to lift carefully from the side of the ridge, not on top
  • Do not push fork through the ridge — you will pierce tubers
  • Lift entire plant, shake off soil gently
  • Do not wash tubers in the field — brushing is enough

Handling

  • Place tubers gently into crates or baskets
  • Do not drop from height — even 20 cm causes bruising
  • Do not leave tubers in direct sun — they will green and go soft
  • Move to shade immediately
Lesson 3

Curing Before Storage

In this lesson
  • Cure tubers before storage
  • Understand the difference between curing and drying
  • Extend storage life by months

Why cure

Curing heals minor wounds and thickens skins. It converts a 2-month storage crop into a 5-month storage crop. Skipping curing is a common cause of storage rot.

Curing conditions

  • Temperature: 15–20°C
  • Humidity: 85–95%
  • Duration: 10–14 days
  • Ventilation: Good airflow to remove respiration heat

How to cure at small scale

  • A shaded, well-ventilated shed
  • Tubers in shallow crates or thin layers on a floor
  • Cover with straw or damp cloth to hold humidity
  • Turn carefully every 2–3 days

Signs of successful curing

  • Skins are firm and do not rub off
  • Small wounds have formed a dry, protective layer
  • No soft spots or new rot
Lesson 4

Storage — Two Systems

In this lesson
  • Choose between diffuse-light and dark-cool storage
  • Compare cost and shelf life
  • Prevent storage losses

System 1 — Diffuse-light storage (table potatoes)

Tubers are stored in a cool, dry, well-ventilated shed with indirect light. The light prevents sprouting and greening without making the tubers green — the light is diffuse, not direct.

  • Temperature: 10–15°C
  • Storage life: 3–5 months
  • Capital: low ($100–500)
  • Best for: table potatoes sold within 3–5 months

System 2 — Cold storage (processing/seed potatoes)

Refrigerated storage at 4–8°C. Long shelf life, sprout-free.

  • Temperature: 4–8°C
  • Storage life: 6–10 months
  • Capital: $3,000–20,000 for a cold room
  • Best for: processing potatoes, seed potatoes, off-season market

Traditional pit storage

Tubers are layered with dry sand or straw in a pit, covered with soil. Provides humidity and cool temperatures. Common in cool highlands. Losses 20–40% in 3 months if the pit is not properly drained.

Reducing storage losses

  • Never store damaged tubers
  • Do not store potatoes near onions, apples or ripening fruit — ethylene makes potato sprout
  • Keep storage dark, cool, dry, ventilated
  • Check monthly for rot and remove affected tubers
  • Sort and re-pack every 6 weeks for long storage

Lesson 5

Grading, Packing and Transport

In this lesson
  • Grade for the market
  • Pack to protect tubers
  • Transport without damage

Grading

GradeSizeMarket
Grade 160–90 mm, uniform, no blemishSupermarkets, restaurants
Grade 245–60 mm or minor blemishLocal market, retailers
Grade 3Small, misshapenProcessing, animal feed
Seed30–60 g whole tubersOther farmers

Packing

  • Use ventilated crates or net bags — not sealed plastic
  • Do not pack more than 20–25 kg per bag
  • Packing in the shade
  • Do not wash before packing — washing shortens shelf life

Transport

  • Load carefully; nothing heavy on top
  • Transport in cool hours (early morning or evening)
  • Cover with shade cloth if open vehicle
  • Deliver to buyer as soon as possible

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. What is dehaulming?

Correct: cutting off the haulm. Sets the skin and improves storage.

2. What is the ideal curing temperature?

Correct: 15–20°C. With 85–95% humidity for 10–14 days.

3. Which storage system keeps potatoes longest?

Correct: cold storage. 6–10 months, but high capital cost.

4. Why not store potatoes near apples or onions?

Correct: ethylene triggers sprouting. Keep stored potatoes isolated.

5. What size is grade 1 potato?

Correct: 60–90 mm. Uniform, no blemish — the supermarket grade.
