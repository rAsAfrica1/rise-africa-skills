Cold Room for 10 Beasts / Module 1 of 12

Module 1

The 10-Beast Cold Room Business

Sizing, capital, and the kill cycle that has to be true before a single panel is ordered.

Read time: 25 minutesThen: Module 2 — Site and Power
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • State in one paragraph why a small chilled room exists as a business, not as equipment
  • Size a chilled room for a specific number of carcasses at a specific weight
  • Distinguish the four categories of cost a cold room carries over its life
  • Decide whether 10 beasts is the right size for your kill cycle, or whether you need 5 or 20
  • Produce a one-page feasibility sketch before committing to the build

Lesson 1.1

Why a cold room exists — the real reason

A cold room is not a way to keep meat longer. It is a way to convert a single-day transaction into a multi-day market. That is the entire business case, and everything else in this course flows from it.

Consider what a butcher in a warm climate can do without chilled storage. He slaughters on Monday. The meat must be sold, cooked or spoiled by Wednesday. Every customer knows this. So every customer on Monday knows he has the upper hand — the butcher must sell at whatever price the market will pay today, because tomorrow it is a loss.

Add a chilled room holding 10 carcasses. Now the butcher slaughters on Monday, hangs the carcasses at 2–4°C, and sells from Monday to Saturday. Customers who come on Thursday get the same quality as Monday. The butcher no longer has to sell the entire week's meat on day one. He chooses who he sells to, when, and at what price.

Three commercial consequences follow, and each one is worth more than the room cost:

  1. Price control. The butcher can hold stock back when the market is weak and release when it is strong.
  2. Buyer reach. Restaurants, hotels, mines and institutions buy on weekly delivery schedules. Without chilled storage the butcher cannot supply them. With it, he can.
  3. Kill frequency. Slaughtering 10 beasts on one day and chilling them all is cheaper per beast than slaughtering 2 beasts on five different days. The chilled room is what allows a batch kill to be economical.
The rule: the cold room does not make meat. It rearranges when meat can be sold. Every figure in the business plan that follows is decided by that one fact.

Why "10 beasts"

Ten is not an arbitrary number. It is a natural size for a small, single-operator abattoir supplying a rural district or one hotel/restaurant cluster. Below about six, the fixed costs — power, refrigerant service, health inspection, staff — begin to overwhelm the revenue per beast. Above about twenty, the operator can no longer manage the room alone and the kill process becomes a full abattoir, which is a different course. Ten is the sweet spot for a one-man-plus-help operation.

Lesson 1.2

Sizing the room — the arithmetic, done once and carefully

Most first-time cold rooms are the wrong size. They are wrong because the owner sized them by walking into someone else's room and saying "like that, but bigger". The room must be sized to your carcasses, and carcass size is a decision you make before the room is drawn.

Step 1 — what does a beast weigh?

A "beast" in this course means a finished beef animal or a large cattle carcass. In southern and eastern Africa the typical carcass weights you will meet are:

Plan on the carcass weight, not the live weight. The dressing percentage is 50–58% of live weight depending on breed and finish. When you size the room, use the highest carcass weight you expect to handle in the next five years, not the average. A room built for 250 kg carcasses that has to accept a 320 kg carcass will not fit the rail.

Step 2 — how much length does each carcass take on a rail?

A hanging beef carcass occupies roughly 0.85–1.0 linear metres of rail when hung on the Achilles tendon. That length is for the carcass to hang without touching the one in front of it. Below 0.85 m the carcasses brush each other, which transfers surface bacteria and slows the surface chilling. Above 1.0 m you are wasting rail length you paid for.

Step 3 — single rail or two rails?

Ten carcasses can be hung in one of three arrangements:

  • Single straight rail. Requires a room at least 10 m long internally. This is rarely practical. It also forces long walking distances during loading and unloading.
  • Two parallel rails, ten carcasses split five and five. Room becomes roughly 3.0 × 5.5 m internal. The operator can walk between the two rails. Loading and unloading are efficient.
  • Loop rail — the carcasses hang around the perimeter of the room. Room becomes roughly 3.5 × 3.5 m internal with a curved rail returning to the door. The rail is unbroken and the operator stays in the centre. Best for a small room, but requires a rail bender or a fabricator who understands cold-room rails.

For most rural 10-beast rooms, the loop rail or two parallel rails arrangement is correct. The straight single rail is right for a processing line, not a storage room.

Step 4 — internal volume

Volume matters because it drives the refrigeration calculation in Module 4. Take the internal dimensions from Step 3 and multiply:

Volume = internal length × internal width × internal height

Two parallel rails, 5.5 × 3.0 × 2.7 m = 44.5 m³ internal. The loop rail, 3.5 × 3.5 × 2.7 m = 33 m³. The difference in volume is roughly 25%, and the difference in refrigeration requirement is proportional. That is a real cost difference over the life of the room. Do not over-size the room for the number of carcasses, unless you intend to hang more later.

Do not add a "just in case" extension. A room 30% larger than needed costs 30% more to build, 30% more to run, and 30% more to repair. If you want capacity later, build a second room later. The room you can pay for now and run correctly is worth more than the room you cannot.

Lesson 1.3

The four categories of cost — and which one people forget

Every cold room carries four cost categories. Three of them appear on the first invoice and the fourth does not. The fourth is the one that kills cold rooms.

Category 1 — Capital cost (the build)

Insulated panels, the refrigeration unit, the door, the rails, the floor, the vapour barrier, the electrical installation, the standby power, the drain. This is the number every first-time buyer counts and it is roughly 60–70% of the lifetime cost.

Category 2 — Commissioning cost (one-off, after the build)

Charging the refrigerant, testing at load, calibration of the controller, and the first health inspection. Often forgotten in the budget, and it is real. Budget –600 for commissioning on a 10-beast room, plus the health licensing fee.

Category 3 — Running cost (monthly)

Electricity or generator fuel, refrigerant top-up every 1–2 years, filter replacement, door seal replacement every 2–3 years, and the periodic defrost cycle. On a 10-beast room in warm conditions, expect –250 per month in electricity at typical African industrial tariffs, or –400 per month of diesel if you have no reliable grid.

Category 4 — Product loss during power failure (the killer)

This is the cost that most business plans ignore and it is the one that destroys a small cold room business. A full room of 10 carcasses at 250 kg each is 2,500 kg of meat. At a farm-gate value of .00/kg, that is ,500 of stock. A power failure of 24 hours in a well-built room will not spoil the meat, but a failure of 72 hours will. A business that loses one 72-hour event per year loses more than the cold room cost to build.

The rule of four: budget for capital, commissioning, running and at least one product-loss event in the first three years. A business plan without the fourth column is a business plan that fails in year two.

Lesson 1.4

Is 10 the right number for your kill cycle?

The room size is not chosen by how many beasts you wish you could kill. It is chosen by the kill cycle you will actually run. There are three patterns:

Pattern A — Weekly batch kill

You slaughter 10 animals every Monday. The room fills on Monday, empties over the week, and is empty by Sunday night. This is the ideal pattern for a 10-beast room. It matches:

  • Slaughter labour — one dedicated kill day per week
  • Refrigeration load — the room does one big pull-down per week, not continuous loading
  • Health inspection — one inspection visit per week
  • Cash cycle — one big purchase of live animals, one week of retail sales

If you can build this pattern, 10 is right. If you cannot, another size may be right.

Pattern B — Continuous kill, steady sale

You slaughter 2 animals per day, five days a week, and sell continuously. The room holds about 8–10 carcasses at peak, but the pull-down is smaller and more frequent. This pattern suits a busy urban butcher with a steady retail trade. The 10-beast room works, but the refrigeration sizing must account for more frequent door openings (Module 4).

Pattern C — Seasonal kill

You slaughter 30 animals over a two-week festival period, then almost none for two months. This is common around Christmas, Eid and major ceremonies. In this pattern, a 10-beast room fills to capacity twice a year and sits under-used the rest of the time. You are paying capital and running cost for peak capacity you use two weeks a year.

The right answer for Pattern C is a smaller room (5 beasts) plus a plan to sell faster during the peak, or a second temporary chiller rented for the festival. It is not to buy a 10-beast room you cannot fill.

The sizing rule: size the room for your typical week, not your peak week. Peak-week demand is solved by selling faster, hiring capacity, or scheduling slaughter, not by paying for capacity all year.

Lesson 1.5

Where the room fits in the kill chain

A cold room is one building in a chain. Understanding the chain tells you what else you must build, and prevents the most common mistake: building the cold room and then discovering you have nowhere to slaughter, no water, and no drained floor to wash it on.

The full chain, from live animal to sold meat, is:

  1. Holding pen. Where animals wait before slaughter. Should be near the kill floor but not on top of it. One day's holding capacity at minimum.
  2. Kill floor. Cleanable, drained, water-supplied. Slaughter happens here.
  3. Chilling corridor or hanging area. Where the carcass is transferred immediately after dressing. Ideally a small intermediate room or the door of the cold room itself. Do not carry a warm carcass through a hot yard.
  4. The cold room. The subject of this course.
  5. Cutting and packing room. Where carcasses are broken down for retail. Must be separate from the cold room if you are selling cuts.
  6. Dispatch and cold transport. How the meat reaches the buyer. A refrigerated vehicle or insulated boxes with ice, for anything beyond 30 minutes' travel.

Each of these is a cost. The cold room course can be built first, but the full chain has to exist for the business to work. Modules 2 and 7 return to this and identify what you can defer and what must be in place before the first carcass is chilled.

The minimum viable chain for a 10-beast room

You do not need to build every element at once. The minimum viable chain for a 10-beast operation is:

  • An existing shaded yard with a concrete slab (kill floor) and a water point
  • A hanging rail from the slab to the cold room door (30 seconds of carrying, no exposed walking)
  • The cold room itself
  • A clean, drained cutting table inside or adjacent to the cold room
  • Insulated boxes or a refrigerated vehicle for delivery

Everything beyond this — a full cutting room, a separate dispatch dock, a holding pen with feed and water — can be added as revenue grows. The first phase is: kill cleanly, chill quickly, deliver cold.

Lesson 1.6

Producing your one-page feasibility sketch

Before any money is committed, produce a single page. Not a business plan — a sketch. The following structure:

  1. Kill cycle. Which of the three patterns (A, B, C) will you run? How many beasts per week on average, and at peak?
  2. Carcass weight. What is the heaviest carcass you will hang in the next five years?
  3. Room size. Internal length × width × height, with the rail arrangement (loop, two rails, or single straight).
  4. Buyer list. Name three real buyers of the output — households, restaurants, butchers, processors — and their weekly demand in kg.
  5. Input supply. Where do the animals come from? Name two real suppliers and the price per live kg in your district today.
  6. Power. Grid, generator, solar, or hybrid? Note the reliability of the grid in your district.
  7. Capital. One indicative number for the build and one for the backup power, replaced by three quotations before commitment.
  8. Break-even. How many kg of meat sold per week covers the monthly running cost?
  9. One risk. The single biggest risk in your district — power, water, disease, theft, price collapse — and how you will reduce it.
  10. Decision. Build now, build smaller, or wait.
The rule: the feasibility sketch is the cheapest thing you will do on this project. Two hours of careful thought on one page is worth more than of wrong panels.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. What is the primary business function of a cold room?

Correct: multi-day market. The value of chilling is the ability to sell over many days instead of one, at prices the seller controls.

2. Roughly how much linear rail does one hanging beef carcass occupy?

Correct: 0.85–1.0 m. Carcasses must not touch. Below 0.85 m they transfer bacteria; above 1.0 m you are wasting rail length.

3. Which cost category is most often missing from a first cold room business plan?

Correct: product loss during power failure. A 72-hour blackout on a full room can destroy ,500 of meat. Businesses that ignore this column close in year two.

4. A butcher slaughters 30 animals in two weeks at Christmas and almost none for two months. What room size is correct?

Correct: a 5-beast room plus a peak plan. Size the room for the typical week, not the peak week. Fixed capacity you use two weeks a year is not worth the capital and running cost.

5. What is the minimum viable kill-to-sale chain for a 10-beast room?

Correct: slab + rail + room + table + cold transport. Everything else can be deferred until revenue grows.

Practical assignment — Module 1

Produce a one-page feasibility sketch for your own cold room, following the ten points in Lesson 1.6. It must be specific to your district, your animals, your buyers, and your power supply. Vague answers score nothing.

Submit:

  1. Your one-page sketch, photographed clearly, with all ten points filled in.
  2. One photograph of the site you intend to use, showing the ground, the water source, and the distance to the nearest power point.
  3. A single paragraph (150 words) naming the biggest risk you have identified and stating what you will do about it in the first month of the build.

Marks are weighted toward the risk paragraph and the buyer list. Anyone can size a room. The skill being tested is whether you can name a real buyer, at a real price, in real kilograms, and defend the number against the cost structure you are about to build.
