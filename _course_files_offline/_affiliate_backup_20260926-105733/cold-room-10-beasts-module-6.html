Module 6

Backup Power and the Blackout Discipline

Module 2 introduced the problem. This module is the solution: choosing between battery, generator and hybrid; sizing them honestly; wiring them safely; and the daily habits that keep a cold room alive through a week of load-shedding.

  • Size a diesel generator for the pull-down load, not the average load

Lesson 6.1

Sizing the load — the number everything else follows from

Backup power is sized on two numbers: the running load in kW, and the number of hours you want to cover. Everything else is arithmetic.

The running load

For a 10-beast cold room, the running load when the compressor is on is approximately the compressor's rated input power. A 5 kW rated cooling capacity compressor typically draws 1.8–2.5 kW of electrical input. Add fans, lights, controls, and the standby load on the evaporator, and the total continuous load is 2.2–3.0 kW.

But a cold room compressor does not run continuously at full load. In a well-built room the compressor cycles. In a poorly-built room, or on kill day, it runs continuously. Size for the worst case: assume 100% duty cycle.

The autonomy target

AutonomyTotal energy needed (at 2.5 kW)Typical outage covered
4 hours10 kWhShort evening outages
8 hours20 kWhOvernight, or scheduled load-shedding block
12 hours30 kWhMost single outages
24 hours60 kWhExtended outages, remote sites
48 hours120 kWhMulti-day outages, deep rural areas
The rule: size the backup for your measured worst case, not your average. Use the longest single outage you logged in Module 2, plus a 30% safety margin.

Lesson 6.2

Battery sizing — honestly

Usable capacity, not nameplate capacity

Lead-acid batteries should only be discharged to 50% depth-of-discharge (DoD) for long life. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries can be discharged to 80–90% DoD. That difference is enormous.

Battery typeUsable % of nameplateCycles at that DoDCost per kWh usableLifespan
Lead-acid (flooded)50%500–800Baseline2–4 years
Lead-acid (AGM / gel)50%600–1,000Slightly higher3–5 years
Lithium (LiFePO4)80–90%3,000–6,000Higher upfront8–12 years

For a 10-beast room in Africa, lithium iron phosphate is now the correct choice in most cases. The higher upfront cost is repaid by the longer life and higher usable capacity. Only use lead-acid if lithium is genuinely unavailable.

Sizing example

Target: 8 hours autonomy at 2.5 kW = 20 kWh usable.

  • Lithium at 85% usable: 20 ÷ 0.85 = 23.5 kWh nameplate. Round to 24 kWh.
  • Lead-acid at 50% usable: 20 ÷ 0.50 = 40 kWh nameplate. Round to 40 kWh.

The lithium bank is 40% smaller in physical size and weight, and lasts 3–4× longer. That is why lithium wins.

Inverter sizing

The inverter must handle the compressor's starting surge, which is 3–5× the running load for 1–3 seconds. A 2.5 kW compressor can pull 8–12 kW at startup.

  • Continuous rating: 3.5–4 kW
  • Surge rating (peak, 3 seconds): 10–12 kW
  • Type: pure sine wave (mandatory for compressors and electronics)

Never use a modified sine wave inverter for a cold room. It damages motors.

Lesson 6.3

Generator sizing and placement

Sizing

Diesel generator sizing follows two rules:

  1. Continuous rating ≥ running load × 1.5. For 2.5 kW, choose ≥4 kW continuous.
  2. Starting kVA ≥ compressor surge kVA + other loads. For a 10 kW surge and small other loads, choose ≥12 kVA peak capability.

A 5–7.5 kVA diesel generator is correct for a 10-beast room. Smaller generators will fail on compressor startup, stall, or burn out.

Fuel use

Diesel consumption for a 7 kVA generator at 75% load is approximately 1.5–2.0 L/hour. Over an 8-hour outage that is 12–16 L. At typical prices ($1.00–1.60/L), that is $12–26 per outage in fuel alone.

An operator who runs the generator for 200 hours a year spends $300–600 on fuel. Multiply by ten years and the fuel bill exceeds the original generator cost.

Fuel storage and theft

  • Store fuel in a locked, ventilated store away from the cold room.
  • Keep a fuel log: date, litres in, hours run, litres out.
  • Fuel theft is common. Locked tanks with gauges are the minimum.

Placement

  • Exhaust must exit to a ventilated area, never into a closed space.
  • Vibration isolation on a concrete pad.
  • Weather cover but open airflow.
  • At least 10 m from the room's air intake to prevent exhaust recirculation.

Lesson 6.4

Solar and hybrid configurations

Solar panels on a cold room roof do not drive the compressor directly. They charge a battery, which then drives an inverter, which powers the compressor. The solar array just reduces how much the battery is drawn down, or how much grid power is consumed when the grid is up.

Array sizing

Assume 4.5 sun hours per day (typical across much of Africa). To generate 20 kWh per day:

  • 20 kWh ÷ 4.5 sun hours = 4.4 kW array nameplate
  • Round up for system losses (15%) to 5.2 kW of panels

A 5 kW solar array is roughly 12 panels of 430 W each, occupying about 25 m². On the roof of a cold room this is easy to fit.

Hybrid configuration

The most resilient configuration for a rural African cold room is:

GRID ──► CHANGEOVER ◄── GENERATOR │ ▼ ┌────────────┐ │ INVERTER │◄──── SOLAR ARRAY │ (hybrid) │ └─────┬──────┘ │ ▼ ┌────────────┐ │ BATTERY │ └─────┬──────┘ │ ▼ COLD ROOM LOAD

In normal operation, solar charges the battery while the grid runs the load. On grid failure, the inverter switches to battery. If the battery runs low, the generator starts. The result is the minimum possible fuel consumption with maximum resilience.

The hybrid is worth the extra cost when: (a) your grid fails more than 24 hours per month, or (b) fuel is expensive or hard to source, or (c) theft or vandalism makes a permanently installed generator risky. Otherwise a simple generator is enough.

Lesson 6.5

The changeover — wiring it safely

Electrical installation must be carried out by a licensed electrician. This section tells you what to ask for, not how to do it yourself.

What a safe changeover must do

  1. Never connect grid and generator at the same time. If both feed the circuit, they will back-feed the grid and kill a utility worker, or destroy the generator.
  2. Mechanically interlocked. Not just labelled — physically impossible to place both inputs in the closed position simultaneously.
  3. Rated for full load. Including starting surge.
  4. Neutral switched or correctly bonded. Confirm with the electrician — this is a common mistake that causes nuisance tripping or floating neutrals.
  5. Clearly labelled. "GRID," "GENERATOR," "OFF."
  6. Lockable. To prevent accidental or unauthorised switching.

Manual vs automatic

TypeCostBest for
Manual changeover switch$80–250Small operations with someone on site at all times. Simplest, most reliable, cheapest.
Automatic transfer switch (ATS)$400–1,500Operations where outages happen at night or when nobody is present. Starts the generator automatically.

For a 10-beast rural operation, the correct starting choice is a manual changeover with a clear procedure posted on the wall. An ATS is a later upgrade.

The backup power procedure — posted on the wall

  1. Outage starts. Note time in the log.
  2. Wait 30 seconds. Grid may return.
  3. If grid still off, start generator (or switch to battery, if automatic).
  4. Switch changeover to generator position.
  5. Confirm compressor is running. Confirm room temperature is stable.
  6. Every hour: check fuel, check temperature, check no alarms.
  7. When grid returns: switch changeover back to grid. Run generator for 2 minutes on no load to cool down. Stop generator.
  8. Note restoration time in the log.

Lesson 6.6

The blackout discipline

Backup power is not the whole answer. During any outage — even a short one — the discipline of the staff on site is worth 30–50% additional safe time.

The blackout rules

  1. Do not open the cold room door during an outage. Every opening is a fresh injection of warm humid air. Post a note. Tell the staff. Repeat until it sticks.
  2. Do not switch the room light on unless you must enter. The light is a small heat load.
  3. Do not start the generator for a 15-minute outage. A cold room holds temperature for 4–8 hours without power. Running the generator for short outages wastes fuel and adds maintenance hours.
  4. Before the outage — if warned — pull the room down 1–2 °C below normal. A room at 1 °C rides through an outage one hour longer than a room at 3 °C. Only do this if the grid operator gives warning.
  5. Move meat before the room warms, not after. If the outage is going to exceed your autonomy, call the neighbouring cold room, the market, and the processors. Do not wait for the room to hit 8 °C to start selling at a discount.
  6. Log every outage and every recovery. Two columns. Date, time off, time on, minutes off, generator used. That log becomes your purchasing decision for the next system upgrade.
The 12-hour rule: if an outage will exceed 12 hours, act at hour 8. Move product, call buyers, or find secondary storage. At hour 12, the meat has warmed and your options are gone.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. What is the correct size relationship between the backup power system and your measured worst-case outage?

Correct: worst case + 30%. Average outages hide the events that kill stock.

2. Why is lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) usually the correct battery choice today?

Correct: usable capacity and cycle life. The higher upfront price is repaid by a much longer life.

3. What is the single most dangerous mistake in changeover switch installation?

Correct: non-interlocked changeover. Back-feed kills utility workers and destroys generators. The interlock is mandatory.

4. When should you act (move meat, call buyers) during an outage that will clearly exceed your backup capacity?

Correct: hour 8. Waiting reduces your options. Selling slightly early at a discount is better than losing the whole load.

5. Why should you NOT start the generator for a 15-minute outage?

Correct: not needed for short outages. Fuel and maintenance hours cost money. Reserve the generator for outages that matter.

Practical assignment — Module 6

Produce a written backup power specification and a blackout procedure for your cold room.

  1. Your measured worst-case outage (from Module 2 log) and the autonomy target.
  2. The running load (kW) and the total energy needed (kWh) for that autonomy target.
  3. Your chosen configuration — battery only, generator only, or hybrid. State the reasoning.
  4. Battery sizing (if applicable) — chemistry, nameplate kWh, usable kWh.
  5. Generator sizing (if applicable) — continuous kVA and starting kVA capability.
  6. Inverter sizing — continuous kW, surge kW, pure sine wave confirmed.
  7. Changeover specification — manual or ATS, interlock confirmed, location.
  8. Two supplier quotations for the backup power solution, including installation.
  9. Your written blackout procedure, following the 6 rules of Lesson 6.6.
  10. One paragraph (150 words) naming the biggest operational risk in your backup plan and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 3 and 9. The configuration is the strategy; the procedure is the discipline that makes it work.
