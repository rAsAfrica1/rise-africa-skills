Lesson 1

Weeding in the First Six Weeks

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Time the two weedings that decide most of the yield
  • Weed without damaging the ridge or the roots
  • Reduce labour with mulch

Sweet potato is slow to close canopy. In the first 6 weeks it is a weak competitor, and weeds will reduce yield by 30–60% if left. Two weedings are enough if done on time.

First weeding: 2–3 weeks after planting

The critical one. Remove all weeds before they compete for water and nutrients. Hand-hoe between the ridges and hand-pull within the ridge. Do not hoe deep — the roots are forming just below the surface.

Second weeding: 5–6 weeks after planting

The final pass. After 6 weeks, the vine canopy covers the soil and suppresses further weeds. The crop is now competing with weeds on equal or better terms.

Mulching

A 5–8 cm layer of dry grass or crop residue on the ridge between plants reduces weeding by 50% and cuts water loss by 30%. The mulch also keeps the roots cool, which improves root shape.

Lesson 2

Fertility — When and What to Apply

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Apply the right nutrients at the right stage
  • Understand the potassium requirement
  • Avoid the mistake of too much nitrogen

Sweet potato is a root crop. It responds to fertility differently from cereals:

  • Base fertility (pre-planting): 5–10 t/ha of well-rotted manure or compost incorporated into the ridge. This is the foundation.
  • Phosphorus: needed for root development. Apply DAP or TSP at 100–150 kg/ha at planting.
  • Potassium: critical for root swelling and starch content. Apply SOP or MOP at 100–150 kg/ha, split between planting and 4 weeks after.
  • Nitrogen: moderate. Urea or CAN at 50–100 kg/ha, applied 3–4 weeks after planting. Too much nitrogen produces vines and no roots.

The most common fertiliser mistake in sweet potato is too much nitrogen. A crop that receives 200 kg/ha of nitrogen will look spectacular and produce almost no marketable roots. The vine stays vegetative.

Organic alternatives

Well-made compost at 5–10 t/ha covers most needs. Wood ash is a good source of potassium — 1–2 t/ha, but must be applied at planting (a season ahead) to avoid pH damage. Do not rely on wood ash alone; it does not supply phosphorus or nitrogen.

Lesson 3

The Fodder Vine Harvest

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Harvest vines without hurting root yield
  • Market vines to livestock buyers
  • Decide whether fodder or roots is your primary income

Sweet potato vines are 12–18% protein on a dry-matter basis — one of the best green fodders available in African smallholder systems. They are eaten fresh by cattle, goats, sheep, pigs and rabbits.

How to harvest vines

  • Wait until the crop is established — 6 weeks after planting. Harvesting earlier damages the young plants.
  • Cut sections of 30–50 cm from the tips, not the base, leaving the plant to regrow.
  • Do not strip the plant bare. Leaving at least half the leaf area keeps the roots growing.
  • Harvest vines 2–3 times during the season, and a final time at root harvest.
  • Deliver fresh — vines wilt and lose quality within hours. Wrap in shade cloth or deliver in the cool of the morning.

Markets for vines

  • Dairy farmers — buy continuously for milking cows
  • Piggeries — buy in bulk, excellent for pig feed
  • Goat and sheep farms — especially in the dry season
  • Rabbit farmers — buy small quantities regularly
  • Urban livestock keepers — small but cash-paying

Fodder vs roots

Do both. The vines are harvested during the season and again at the end. The roots are harvested at 90–120 days. Together they give two income streams from the same plot. The only decision is how hard to cut the vines — the harder you cut, the lower the root yield. A reasonable balance: two light vine cuts during the season, plus the final full cut at root harvest.

Lesson 4

Irrigation and the Dry Season

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand sweet potato water needs
  • Use the dry season to produce off-season premium crops
  • Decide when irrigation pays

Water needs

Sweet potato tolerates drought better than most root crops, but a good crop needs 500–750 mm of water over the season, well distributed. Dry spells at root initiation (2–4 weeks) and root swelling (8–12 weeks) reduce yield sharply.

Irrigation methods

  • Furrow — simple, cheap, works if water is abundant
  • Sprinkler — good for larger plots
  • Drip — most efficient, especially with water scarcity; sweet potato responds well to drip at 90% efficiency

Key practice: sweet potato is sensitive to waterlogging. Do not irrigate heavily, and ensure the ridges drain properly. Over-watering causes root rot and misshapen roots.

Off-season sweet potato

Irrigated sweet potato in the dry season sells at 50–100% above the rainy-season price because supply drops. A small plot of irrigated sweet potato harvested in August (when the previous season's crop is finished and the next is not yet ready) can be the most profitable crop on the farm per square metre. Requires reliable irrigation water.

Lesson 5

The Daily and Weekly Routine

~9 min
In this lesson
  • Adopt a daily walk that catches problems early
  • Set the weekly tasks
  • Keep the records that make the season a business

Daily (10–15 min per 1,000 m²)

  • Walk the plot at the same time each morning
  • Check the weevil traps and vine condition
  • Water if irrigating (finger test)
  • Remove any obviously diseased or dying plants

Weekly

  • Hand-weed any escapes (small)
  • Check soil moisture
  • Fertilise if scheduled
  • Vine harvest if there is a buyer
  • Record sales, expenses, and observations

Monthly

  • Review records: yield, price, buyer
  • Plan next harvest and next planting
  • Check vine quality — remove any obviously virus-infected plants from the multiplication bed

Sweet potato is a forgiving crop. The daily walk still catches the two problems that matter — weevils and wilting — before they spread.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. When do the two critical weedings happen?

Correct: 2–3 weeks and 5–6 weeks. After 6 weeks the vine canopy suppresses most weeds.

2. What is the common fertiliser mistake in sweet potato?

Correct: too much nitrogen. A heavy-N crop looks spectacular and produces almost no roots.

3. What percentage of protein do sweet potato vines contain?

Correct: 12–18% on a dry-matter basis. One of the best African green fodders.

4. When is vine harvesting safe?

Correct: from 6 weeks. Earlier cutting damages young plants.

5. Why is off-season sweet potato more profitable?

Correct: 50–100% higher prices. Off-season supply shortages give irrigated crops a big premium.
