Lesson 1

Planting Date and Depth

In this lesson
  • Time planting to match temperature and rainfall
  • Plant at the correct depth
  • Understand the impact of late planting

Timing

  • East African highlands: plant with the long rains (March–May) or short rains (October–November)
  • North Africa: November–December (winter wheat)
  • Southern Africa: May–June (winter wheat, irrigated)
  • Sahel irrigated: November–December (cool dry season)

Late planting reduces yield by 20–40% because the crop runs out of cool weather before grain fill.

Depth

3–5 cm. Uniform depth gives uniform emergence and even maturity — critical for harvest and quality.

Method

  • Broadcast + harrow: quick, uses more seed, less uniform
  • Drilling (row planting): uniform depth, saves seed, higher yield — the standard for commercial wheat
  • Bed planting: for irrigated systems in some conditions
Lesson 2

Nitrogen Splits — the Yield Driver

In this lesson
  • Split nitrogen into the correct number of applications
  • Time nitrogen to growth stages
  • Avoid the mistake of all-at-once application

Total nitrogen need

Wheat needs 120–200 kg N/ha for a 5 t/ha crop. Barley needs 100–150 kg N/ha. Split this across the season — do not apply all at planting.

Split schedule

Stage% of total NProduct
At planting (basal)30%DAP or compound NPK
Tillering (3–4 weeks)40%Urea or CAN
Stem elongation (6–8 weeks)30%Urea or CAN

Why splits matter

A single application loses 30–50% of the nitrogen to leaching and volatilisation before the crop needs it. Splitting into three applications delivers the nitrogen when the crop requires it, increasing yield by 20–30% with the same total amount.

Late nitrogen

For bread wheat, a small late nitrogen application at heading (20–30 kg N/ha) raises grain protein — a premium for milling markets. For malting barley, avoid late nitrogen — it pushes protein above the malting spec.

Lesson 3

Phosphorus and Potassium

In this lesson
  • Apply phosphorus at planting
  • Apply potassium for stem strength and grain fill
  • Watch for micronutrient deficiencies

Phosphorus

Apply 60–100 kg/ha of P2O5 (as DAP or TSP) at planting. Phosphorus drives root development and tillering. Place in a band below the seed.

Potassium

Apply 40–80 kg/ha of K2O (as SOP or MOP) at planting. Potassium improves stem strength (lodging resistance) and grain fill.

Micronutrients

  • Zinc: common deficiency in alkaline soils. Foliar zinc sulphate at 0.5% at tillering if leaves show pale stripes.
  • Manganese: common in high-pH soils. Foliar manganese sulphate if leaves show grey speck.
  • Boron: rarely deficient in cereals, but can affect grain set.

Organic alternatives

  • 5–10 t/ha well-rotted manure or compost — the base of any soil improvement programme
  • Wood ash for potassium
  • Bone meal for phosphorus
Lesson 4

Irrigation Timing and Method

In this lesson
  • Understand the four critical irrigation stages
  • Choose the right irrigation method
  • Avoid the yield loss from late irrigation

Water need

Wheat needs 400–650 mm of water over the season. Barley needs 350–500 mm. In irrigated systems, apply 5–7 irrigations of 5–7 cm each, timed to growth stages.

The four critical irrigations

StageTimingImpact of missing
Crown root initiation (CRI)20–25 days after sowing20–30% yield loss — the most critical irrigation
Tillering40–45 days15–20% loss
Flowering / heading60–70 days20–30% loss
Grain fill (milk to dough)80–100 days15–25% loss; reduces grain size

Methods

  • Furrow: traditional, uses more water, labour-intensive
  • Sprinkler: efficient, but wetting the canopy increases rust risk — irrigate at dawn
  • Drip: most water-efficient, works best with bed planting
  • Basin / flood: used in Egypt and parts of North Africa; requires levelled fields
The rule: the crown root initiation irrigation (20–25 days after sowing) is the single most important irrigation. Missing it costs 20–30% of the crop, more than missing any other.
Lesson 5

Weeding and the Critical Window

In this lesson
  • Time the critical weeding window
  • Use chemical and mechanical weed control
  • Avoid weed competition during tillering

Critical weed-free window

The first 30–45 days after emergence. Weeds compete for water, light and nutrients during tillering. Yield loss from early weed competition can reach 30–50%.

Weeding methods

  • Hand weeding: 1–2 passes at 3 and 6 weeks. Practical for small plots.
  • Mechanical (harrowing): a light harrow pass at 3–4 weeks when crop is small enough to survive but weeds are established.
  • Herbicides: pre-emergence (pendimethalin) or post-emergence (2,4-D, metsulfuron). Follow label rates and crop safety.

Broadleaf vs grass weeds

Different herbicides target different weeds. Identify what is in your field before choosing a product. Broadleaf weeds (wild mustard, pigweed) respond to 2,4-D; grass weeds (wild oats, Phalaris) need a grass-specific herbicide.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. How should nitrogen be split for wheat?

Correct: 30/40/30. Splitting raises yield 20–30% with the same total nitrogen.

2. Which irrigation is the most critical?

Correct: CRI. Missing it costs 20–30% of the crop.

3. How much phosphorus does wheat need?

Correct: 60–100 kg/ha P2O5. At planting, banded.

4. Why avoid late nitrogen on malting barley?

Correct: raises protein. Malting spec requires protein below 12.5%.

5. What is the critical weed-free window?

Correct: first 30–45 days. Weeds compete for water and nutrients during tillering.
