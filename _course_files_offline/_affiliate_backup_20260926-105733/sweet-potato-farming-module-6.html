Lesson 1

Reading the Sweet Potato Price Calendar

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Map the seasonal price cycle in your market
  • Plan production for the peaks, not the gluts
  • Use storage and staggering to hit high-price months

Sweet potato prices follow the same seasonal curve as other staples — but the curve is shallower because sweet potato stores longer than most crops. In most African markets:

  • Main harvest season (end of rains): lowest prices, glut
  • Post-harvest with storage: prices rise through the dry season
  • Late dry season / before next rains: highest prices, often 2–3x harvest price
  • Off-season irrigated crops: highest margins because supply is smallest

Two actions capture the price curve: cure and store (holds stock into the high-price months) and stagger plantings (spreads harvest across the season instead of one big glut).

Lesson 2

Staggering for Continuous Supply

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Design a staggered planting calendar
  • Match supply to your buyer's buying rhythm
  • Avoid the trap of everything maturing at once

Divide your sweet potato area into 3–4 blocks, planted 3–4 weeks apart. Each block produces a full harvest cycle. The result: instead of one big harvest of 6 tonnes in a single month, you get four harvests of 1.5 tonnes each across four months.

This matters commercially for three reasons:

  1. Price: you sell into steady demand, not a glut, so you get better prices throughout.
  2. Storage pressure: you don't have to store 6 tonnes at once. Each block's harvest can go quickly to market.
  3. Cash flow: steady income instead of one big payday and then nothing.

Example staggering (Southern Africa, rain-fed + irrigation)

  • Block A: plant October, harvest January–February
  • Block B: plant November, harvest February–March
  • Block C: plant December, harvest March–April
  • Block D (irrigated): plant March, harvest June–July (off-season)

If you have irrigation, prioritise the off-season crop. Off-season sweet potato sells for 50–100% more than the main season, and no other local farmer is competing.

Lesson 3

The Four Buyer Types

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Name and price the four main buyers
  • Match variety and grade to each buyer
  • Build loyalty with your best buyer

Buyer 1 — Market vendors and roadside stalls

Buy daily or a few times a week in small volumes. Cash on the spot. Prices vary with the market. Convenient, low risk, but lowest prices.

Buyer 2 — Urban households and supermarkets

Supermarkets buy on contract, weekly, in standard pack sizes. Pay 20–40% above vendors for uniform, well-graded roots. 30-day payment terms. Require business registration for regular supply.

Buyer 3 — Processors (flour, chips, puree)

Buy in bulk seasonally. Pay less per kg than supermarket (because they need volume and take any shape), but they absorb the entire off-grade portion of your crop. Cash or short credit.

Buyer 4 — Institutional (NGO, government, school feeding)

The best-paying buyer for OFSP. Buy to contract, pay 50–100% above fresh market, but require certification (sometimes organic or specific variety) and pay on 30–90 day terms. Contact the district nutrition office or the NGO active in your area.

Buyer 5 — Livestock farmers (for vines)

Dairy and pig producers buy vines year-round. Regular small orders. Prices $20–60/t fresh. Often the highest-margin product from the plot when demand is strong.

Costing a Sweet Potato Crop

The dominant cost is vines. If you produce your own vines in a multiplication bed, that cost drops to $100–300 per hectare after the first season. This is the single biggest cost reduction available to a sweet potato farmer.

Revenue at 15 t/ha

  • At $0.30/kg fresh = $4,500
  • At $0.50/kg fresh = $7,500
  • At $0.80/kg off-season premium = $12,000

Add vine fodder: 5 t at $40/t = $200

Net margin

$500–8,000 per hectare depending on price realisation and cost control. The difference is mostly (a) vine source cost, and (b) market timing.

Lesson 5

Scaling, Financing and the Year-Round Plan

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Write a 12-month sweet potato plan
  • Decide when to scale
  • Understand the risks of over-expansion

The 12-month plan

Build a calendar with: crop block, planting date, expected harvest, target buyer, price target. Every month should have either a planting or a harvest — not both clustered.

Example 12-month plan for a 1-hectare plot:

  • Oct: plant Block A (0.4 ha)
  • Nov: plant Block B (0.3 ha)
  • Dec: plant Block C (0.3 ha)
  • Jan–Mar: harvest A, B, C in sequence
  • Mar: plant Block D (irrigated, 0.3 ha)
  • Jun–Jul: harvest Block D (off-season premium)
  • Throughout: harvest fodder vines and sell to livestock buyers

When to scale

  • Two profitable cycles completed with records
  • Water supply confirmed for larger area
  • Buyers confirmed for the additional volume
  • Vine production capacity in place
  • Storage or curing capacity upgraded
  • Working capital for the larger inputs

When NOT to scale

  • After one good season
  • Before you can keep records on the current plot
  • Without a confirmed buyer for the additional volume
  • Before you know your true cost per hectare

Sweet potato scales gracefully — it can be grown at any scale from 200 m² to 10 hectares, with roughly proportional input and labour. The constraint is market and water, not land.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. When are sweet potato prices typically highest?

Correct: late dry season and off-season irrigated crop. 50–100% premium when supply is short.

2. Why stagger plantings?

Correct: continuous supply, better prices, easier storage, steady cash flow. Splits the crop into 3–4 harvests instead of one glut.

3. Which buyer pays the highest premium for OFSP?

Correct: NGO / government nutrition programs. 50–100% premium, 30–90 day terms.

4. What is the biggest cost reduction available?

Correct: own vine production. Cuts the biggest cost line ($600–3,000/ha) to $100–300/ha after the first season.

5. When is it safe to scale up?

Correct: after two profitable cycles with support in place. Over-scaling is the biggest risk in African smallholder farming.

Module 6 capstone

Write your 12-month sweet potato business plan. Include: (1) the four blocks and their planting dates; (2) expected harvest month for each; (3) the buyer for each block's harvest; (4) your vine production plan (own bed or purchase); (5) costing, revenue and net margin projection; (6) the two triggers that would make you scale or change crop next year.
