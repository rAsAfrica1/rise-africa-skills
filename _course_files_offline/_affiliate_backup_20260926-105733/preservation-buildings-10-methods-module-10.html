Module 10 — Method 9: Smoking and Smokehouses

Smoke, salt and heat — and the building that controls all three without poisoning the product.

Home › Preservation Buildings — 10 Methods › Module 10

Learning outcomes

  • Explain what smoke and heat actually do to preserve food.
  • Choose between cold smoking, hot smoking and smoke-drying.
  • Design a smokehouse with the correct firebox, chamber and vent arrangement.
  • Cure meat and fish safely before smoking.
  • Recognise the chemical hazards of smoking and how to control them.
  • Store and sell smoked product to regulation.

1. How smoking preserves

Smoking is not one process. It is a combination of four effects, and how much of each you get depends on the temperature and the wood.

  1. Drying. Smoke carries heat; the surface of the food dries. Less moisture = less microbial growth.
  2. Antimicrobial compounds. Smoke contains phenols, formaldehyde, acetic acid and other compounds that inhibit bacteria and moulds on the surface.
  3. Antioxidants. Phenols slow fat oxidation — the reason smoked products keep longer than fresh.
  4. Heat (in hot smoking). Cooking kills vegetative bacteria; a hot-smoked product is effectively cooked, and must be handled as a cooked product thereafter.

Smoke alone is not enough

Cold-smoked product is not cooked. It must be cured (salted) first, kept refrigerated throughout, and treated as raw product for regulatory purposes. Smoking without curing and without refrigeration is a botulism risk — Clostridium botulinum grows in the anaerobic, low-acid, moist interior of a smoked sausage that has been under-salted.

Product internal temperature is the safety number

Hot-smoked meat or fish must reach an internal temperature of at least 65 °C for 30 minutes (or equivalent pasteurisation) to be considered cooked.

Cold-smoked product must not exceed 30 °C at any point — if it does, the fat renders, the texture spoils, and food safety risk rises. Measure with a probe thermometer in the thickest part of the product, not in the air.

3. Curing before smoking

Curing is salt plus time. It draws water out of the food and creates an environment where spoilage organisms cannot grow. Every smoked product — especially cold-smoked — starts with a cure.

Cure methodDescriptionTypical timeBest for
Dry cureSalt and sugar rubbed onto the surface; draws moisture out1–7 daysBacon, salmon, some sausages
Wet cure (brine)Product submerged in salted water, often with sugar and spices12 h – 5 daysFish, poultry, larger cuts
Injection cureBrine injected into the muscle12–24 hLarge cuts, hams
CombinationDry rub, then brine or injection1–3 daysCommercial bacon and ham

Brine strength

Brine strength is measured on a salinometer (or by weight):
Fish brine: 70–80 % saturation (roughly 260–300 g salt per litre of water)
Meat brine: 60–70 % saturation (roughly 220–260 g/L)
Poultry brine: 40–60 % saturation (150–220 g/L)

Time in brine depends on thickness, not weight. A 25 mm thick fillet takes 3–4 h in a 70 % brine. A 50 mm thick piece takes 6–8 h. Do not guess — measure thickness with a ruler.

Cure safety — nitrites

Traditional cures relied on saltpetre (potassium nitrate). Modern cures use sodium nitrite (cure #1) or a nitrate/nitrite blend (cure #2) for long-cured products. Their purpose is to:

  • Inhibit Clostridium botulinum.
  • Fix the pink colour.
  • Develop the characteristic cured flavour.

Nitrite is toxic in overdose

Follow the manufacturer's dose exactly, using a scale accurate to 0.1 g. Never substitute one cure for another (cure #1 and cure #2 are not interchangeable). Never use more than the label states "for safety". Never sell cured products until you have taken a recognised food safety course and confirmed the local rules on nitrites and nitrates.

4. The smokehouse

Design principles

  1. Offset the firebox. Never put the fire directly under the product. Heat and flame char the food, and the smoke carries ash.
  2. Give the smoke a path. A 1–2 m duct between the firebox and the chamber lets the smoke cool and drop soot and tar. Cold-smokers need 2–4 m of duct.
  3. Vent at the top. Smoke and moisture must leave. A high adjustable vent controls draw and temperature.
  4. Control the air inlet. The firebox inlet controls the burn rate; the vent controls the draw. Between them you set the temperature.
  5. Insulate the chamber. Even in a hot climate, an uninsulated chamber loses heat fast and temperature swings ruin the product.
  6. Use food-safe materials throughout. Stainless steel for the chamber interior, food-grade or brick for the firebox, and no galvanised metal in contact with smoke.
  7. Drain and clean. The chamber floor should slope to a drain and be removable for cleaning. Creosote builds up over time and is a fire risk.
  8. Never use treated or painted timber or resinous softwood in contact with smoke. The compounds come through in the product.

Never smoke with softwoods

Pine, fir, spruce and other resinous woods produce creosote and tarry compounds that give a bitter, petrol-like flavour and are potentially harmful. Also avoid: any treated timber, any painted wood, any wood with glue (plywood, MDF, chipboard). Use only untreated hardwood, split small, and dry.

5. Temperature control

Temperature is the single most important variable. Too hot and you cook the surface before the smoke penetrates; too cool and you get no smoke flavour and risk bacterial growth.

MethodChamber tempHow to hold it
Cold smoke15–30 °CSmall fire, long duct, cool weather; sometimes ice pans in the chamber
Warm smoke30–50 °CModerate fire, insulation, controlled vent
Hot smoke60–90 °CLarger fire or gas burner; vent to regulate

Use a dual-probe thermometer

One probe in the chamber (to read air temperature near the product) and one in the thickest part of the product (to read internal temperature). This is the difference between guessing and knowing. A wireless dual-probe thermometer costs little and makes every batch predictable.

Chamber sizing — worked example

Target: 30 kg of sausage per hot-smoking batch.
Hanging density for sausages on rails: about 25 kg per metre of rail at 60 mm spacing.

Rail length needed = 30 / 25 = 1.2 m.
With 2 rails in the chamber: 0.6 m each, so a chamber width of 0.9 m is comfortable.

Height: rails at 200 mm, 400 mm and 600 mm from the top gives a chamber height of about 1.5 m with a drip tray and floor space.

Chamber: 1.2 m wide × 0.9 m deep × 1.5 m high = 1.6 m³.

Insulated with 50 mm board and a low firebox, that holds 60–80 °C easily with a modest fire.

7. Hygiene and cleaning

  • Clean the chamber after every batch: scrape residue, wash surfaces with hot water and a food-safe detergent, rinse.
  • Remove and clean drip trays and racks in hot water.
  • Clean the duct and firebox flue quarterly to remove creosote build-up. Creosote is flammable.
  • Do not use caustic or strong chemical cleaners inside the chamber — the residual smell and compounds transfer to the next batch.
  • Inspect for rust and replace any corroded parts immediately.
  • Keep a cleaning log. Inspectors will want to see it.

Label contents

  • Product name.
  • Ingredients in descending order (including salt, cure, spices).
  • Net weight.
  • Your business name and address.
  • Batch code and production date.
  • Best-before or use-by date.
  • Storage instruction: "Keep refrigerated below 4 °C".
  • Allergen declaration if any applies.

9. Regulatory considerations

  • Smoking is a food-processing activity. Most countries require food business registration and inspection of the smoking premises.
  • Cold-smoked and hot-smoked products are treated differently by regulators. Confirm the rules for each product type.
  • Nitrite and nitrate use is regulated. Follow the local limits and label requirements.
  • Water activity or pH testing may be required for shelf-stable smoked products.
  • Every batch should be traceable: raw material source, cure, smoke time, temperature record, product internal temperature.
