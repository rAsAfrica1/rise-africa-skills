Module 3

The Striga War, Weeding & Dryland Feeding

Striga — the parasitic witchweed — is the single weed that can end a sorghum crop. This module is the whole strategy against it, plus the weeding and dryland feeding discipline around it.

Lesson 1

What Striga Is and Why It Is Different

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Describe Striga's life cycle and why it is not a normal weed
  • Identify Striga-infested fields by sight
  • Understand why hand-pulling alone cannot solve the problem

Striga — witchweed — is not a weed that competes. It is a parasite that attaches. It germinates only in the presence of a host-root signal from sorghum, millet, maize, rice or a small number of other grasses. It then sends a root-like organ into the host's root, fuses with it, and draws water and nutrients directly out of the crop. By the time you see the purple flowers above ground, the damage is done below.

A single Striga plant produces 50,000 to 500,000 seeds. Those seeds survive in soil for 10 to 20 years. That is the number that ends the argument about quick fixes: Striga is a multi-season campaign, not a single weeding.

Signs of Striga infestation

  • Crop stunting in patches, often in strips following soil movement or drainage lines
  • Purple or pink small flowers on 15–30 cm stems, appearing 6–10 weeks after sowing
  • Yellowing of the crop that does not respond to nitrogen
  • Worst in fields that have grown sorghum continuously for more than 3 years

Why hand-pulling alone fails

Hand-pulling removes the above-ground plant and stops that plant setting seed. It does not remove the attachment already draining the crop, and it does not remove the seedbank in the soil. Pulling is one line of a defence, not the whole defence.

Lesson 2

The Stacked Defence — Four Lines at Once

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Deploy four Striga-control lines at the same time
  • Choose a Striga-resistant variety and understand what "resistant" means
  • Use a trap crop or a false-host to reduce the seedbank

Striga cannot be beaten by any single tactic. It is beaten by stacking four lines at once, every season, for several seasons.

Line 1 — Resistant or tolerant variety

Some released sorghum and millet varieties resist Striga attachment, and some tolerate it (they still attach, but their yield does not collapse). Ask the release document for the resistance rating. Resistant varieties suppress new Striga seed production; tolerant ones do not, but they protect the yield.

Line 2 — Fertility

Striga does worst where the soil is fertile. Well-fed sorghum competes better with Striga and is less attractive to Striga as a host. Manure or compost applied at the station is the first input a Striga-infested field needs, before any pesticide.

Line 3 — Legume rotation

A season of cowpea, groundnut, soybean or pigeonpea forces Striga seed to germinate in the absence of a host and die. One full season in a Striga-infested field can reduce the Striga seedbank by 50–80%. This is the most powerful single tactic available.

Line 4 — Hand-pulling before seed set

Pull Striga plants before they flower, twice: once at 6–7 weeks, once at 9–10 weeks. Every plant pulled before setting seed removes up to half a million future Striga from the field. A small extra labour cost per season, for a permanent long-term gain.

Lesson 3

Weeding Discipline in the First Six Weeks

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Time the two weeding passes that decide most of the yield
  • Understand the critical weed-free period
  • Weed without damaging the crop or the tied ridges

A sorghum or millet crop is most vulnerable to weeds between emergence and six weeks. After six weeks, the crop's own canopy begins to suppress weeds and the competitive balance tilts in the farmer's favour. That window is the entire weeding priority.

First weeding

At 2–3 weeks after emergence, at the same time as thinning. Removes the emerging weeds while they are small, and combines the labour with thinning for a single pass. A hoe pass between rows; hand-weeding within the row.

Second weeding

At 5–6 weeks after emergence. The last pass before the canopy closes. This is the pass that decides how much weed pressure the crop carries to harvest.

Weeding around Striga

Weeding is also the opportunity to hand-pull Striga plants. Hand-pulling should be combined with the second weeding pass when Striga has emerged but not yet flowered. Two hours per hectare of extra labour is enough.

Tools and cautions

A hoe is the standard tool. Care is needed in tied-ridge systems not to break the ties while weeding — pass over the ties, not through them. In no-till systems, a hand-pull or a slasher is used instead of a hoe.

Lesson 4

Dryland Fertility — Manure, Micro-Dosing and Top-Up

~12 min
In this lesson
  • Apply manure at the station, not broadcast
  • Use micro-dosing to stretch expensive fertiliser
  • Time the nitrogen top-up for the biggest yield response

Manure

Well-rotted cattle manure at 3–5 t/ha placed in the station at sowing. Placement doubles the effective rate versus broadcast because the roots intercept it directly. On a station at 75 × 25 cm, a handful of manure per station equals about 3 t/ha total. That is a sustainable annual application for most smallholders with access to one or two cattle.

Micro-dosing

Broadcast nitrogen fertiliser on a dryland sorghum crop is largely wasted — the roots are concentrated beneath the station, and rainfall is uncertain. Micro-dosing puts a small quantity of DAP or NPK directly in the station at sowing, or a small top-up of urea next to each plant at tillering. Rates are tiny: 2–4 g per station of DAP, or about 30–60 kg/ha total. The yield response is 60–80% of a full-rate application at 15–25% of the cost.

Top-up nitrogen

A single top-up of urea or ammonium nitrate at tillering — around 50 kg/ha — lifts yield by 0.5–1 t/ha on responsive soils. The timing is 3–5 weeks after emergence, just before the crop's rapid growth phase. Top-up later is largely wasted.

Lesson 5

Water and Moisture Management

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Capture and hold the limited rainfall you receive
  • Protect the soil from drying through mulch and residue
  • Know when supplementary irrigation pays and when it does not

Rainfall capture

Tied ridges are the primary technique — described in Module 2. Secondary techniques include half-moons (small semicircular bunds around each planting station) in the driest zones, and planting basins (zaï pits) that catch runoff and hold both water and compost.

Soil cover

Residue retained on the surface — from the previous cereal crop or from a legume intercrop — reduces soil temperature, slows evaporation, and reduces runoff. In no-till systems, this is the primary moisture-conservation practice. A 2–4 cm residue layer can hold the equivalent of 10–20 mm more water in the topsoil than bare ground.

Supplementary irrigation

For sorghum and millet, supplementary irrigation is a decision, not a default. It pays when: (a) the crop is in flowering or grain-fill and a critical dry spell hits, (b) water is available at low marginal cost (gravity, a full tank, a working pump already paid for), and (c) the value of the saved yield exceeds the water and labour cost. In most dryland systems, supplementary irrigation is not economic unless (a) and (b) both hold. For those who farm in a valley with a river or a working borehole, one irrigation at flowering can save 30–50% of the yield of a drought-stressed crop.

Module 3 capstone

Walk your sorghum or millet plot and record: (1) how many Striga plants you can count in 100 paces, (2) the current stage of the crop, (3) your next weeding date in the calendar. Then plan the next two seasons around Striga: which season gets a full legume rotation, which variety you will sow in the sorghum season, and when you will pull Striga.

If you have no Striga on your own farm, visit a neighbouring farm that does, and write the field notes there. You will need them.
