Module 7

Hygiene, HACCP and the Health Inspector

A cold room does not sell meat. It sells confidence — the buyer's confidence that the meat is safe. This module is how you build and document that confidence, and what the health inspector will actually ask.

Module 7 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Identify the three contamination routes in a cold room and how to close each
  • Write a HACCP plan in the seven-step format recognised by regulators
  • Set up the records a health inspector will ask for
  • Design a cleaning schedule that actually gets done
  • Conduct a self-inspection before the official one arrives
  • Produce a documented food safety plan for your own cold room

Lesson 7.1

The three contamination routes

Meat contamination in a cold room comes from three directions. Close all three or the room cannot pass inspection.

1. Contaminated in — the incoming carcass

An animal slaughtered in a dirty yard arrives with bacteria on its surface. If it enters the cold room contaminated, the room is contaminated. Control is upstream — clean kill floor, clean water, clean hands, clean knife. The cold room cannot fix a dirty carcass.

2. Cross-contamination inside — from surfaces, hands, tools

Once inside, meat can be recontaminated by touching a dirty wall, a trolley wheel, a butcher's apron, or the drain. Every surface in the room must be cleanable and cleaned.

3. Growth inside — the slow multiplication

If the room does not hold temperature, bacteria multiply. The 2 °C target is not cosmetic. Bacteria in meat double every 30–60 minutes at 20 °C and every 4–8 hours at 2 °C. A room at 8 °C grows bacteria 5–10× faster than a room at 2 °C.

The rule: contamination is prevented, not corrected. Every step of the kill-to-sale chain either adds or removes risk. The cold room is one step in that chain, and it can only do its job if the steps before and after also do theirs.

Lesson 7.2

HACCP in seven steps

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. Every food regulator in the world recognises this system. Even if your country does not formally require HACCP, adopting it makes inspection easy and buyers confident.

The seven HACCP principles

  1. Conduct a hazard analysis. Identify every step where contamination can happen.
  2. Determine the Critical Control Points (CCPs). The steps where a specific control prevents the hazard.
  3. Establish critical limits. The value that must be met (e.g., 2 °C, or 70% alcohol, or 200 ppm chlorine).
  4. Establish monitoring procedures. How you check the limit is being met, and how often.
  5. Establish corrective actions. What you do when the limit is breached.
  6. Establish verification procedures. How you confirm the system is working.
  7. Establish record-keeping and documentation. The written evidence.

Lesson 7.3

The records an inspector asks for

Inspection is 30% looking at the room and 70% reading your records. Prepare the following, in a single binder, updated weekly.

The seven records

  1. Temperature log. Twice daily, plus any alarm events. Format: date, time, temperature, initial.
  2. Cleaning schedule with signatures. What was cleaned, when, by whom.
  3. Sanitiser concentration log. Chemical used, dilution, test result.
  4. Pest control log. Bait stations, traps, any pest sightings, action taken.
  5. Staff health declaration. Each worker declares fitness to work daily (no diarrhoea, vomiting, infected wounds).
  6. Supplier record. Where animals come from, when, and any inspection certificate.
  7. Dispatch record. What went out, when, to whom, at what temperature.

The room's other required documents

  • Business registration certificate.
  • Health certificate for the premises (issued by the district health office).
  • Employee medical certificates (food handler medicals).
  • Water test certificate (annual).
  • Vaccination certificates for staff where required (e.g., typhoid).
  • Insurance certificate (recommended, not always required).
The record rule: if it is not written down, it did not happen. An inspector who sees perfect records will be lenient on minor physical issues. An inspector who sees no records will find the physical issues.

Lesson 7.4

Cleaning — the schedule that works

Most cleaning schedules fail because they are too ambitious. A schedule that gets done matters more than a schedule that is theoretically complete.

The three-tier schedule

TierFrequencyTasks
DailyAfter each kill, and end of daySweep solids; rinse walls and floor with cold water; wash with detergent, top-down; rinse; apply sanitiser at correct concentration; squeegee to drain
WeeklyEvery Sunday or quiet dayDeep clean the evaporator coil (with unit off); clean door gasket; clean drain strainer and grease trap; wash strip curtains; check and clean light fixtures
MonthlyFirst of monthFull drain flush; pest control check; check alarm function; check temperature logger calibration with an ice bath; inspect panels and vapour barrier for damage

Cleaning chemicals

  • Detergent — food-safe, alkaline, removes protein and fat.
  • Sanitiser — chlorine at 100–200 ppm, or quaternary ammonium, or peracetic acid. Contact time 30 seconds minimum.
  • Acid cleaner — weekly, removes mineral scale on the coil and floor.

Do not mix chlorine with acid — it releases toxic chlorine gas. Store separately. Mix only as much as needed for one day.

The boot wash

A tray of sanitiser at the door. Every person entering steps through it. This one feature stops more contamination than any other single control.

Lesson 7.5

The self-inspection

Conduct a self-inspection the week before the health inspector is due. Use the same checklist the inspector will use.

The 20-point self-inspection

  1. Room holds 2–4 °C, verified with a calibrated thermometer.
  2. Temperature log complete for the last 30 days.
  3. Alarm functions when tested.
  4. Door gasket intact, seals all round.
  5. Internal door release works, from inside, in the dark.
  6. Strip curtain in place and clean.
  7. Floor drain flows freely, strainer in place.
  8. Grease trap cleaned within last 30 days.
  9. Walls, floor, ceiling clean, no cracks, no crevices.
  10. Panels intact, no punctures in the vapour barrier visible.
  11. Rails clean, no rust, hooks in good condition.
  12. Light fixtures sealed, working.
  13. No condensation dripping on product.
  14. Pest control: no rodent or insect signs in last 30 days.
  15. Staff medical certificates current.
  16. Cleaning chemicals in original labelled containers.
  17. Sanitiser concentration correct (tested today).
  18. Records binder complete and up to date.
  19. Waste disposal to a covered bin, emptied daily.
  20. Water source potable, tested within last 12 months.

Any "no" is an item to fix before the inspector arrives. Fixing before inspection is free. Fixing after inspection may involve a closure notice.

The inspector rule: your inspector is not your enemy. A clean, well-documented room gives the inspector an easy report to write. Give them that, and they will leave you alone for another year.

Knowledge check — Module 7

1. What are the three contamination routes in a cold room?

Correct: three routes. Every control point either prevents incoming contamination, stops cross-contamination, or slows bacterial growth.

2. What does HACCP stand for?

Correct: Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. The international food-safety system recognised by every regulator.

3. What is the minimum chlorine concentration for a food-contact sanitiser?

Correct: 100–200 ppm. With 30 seconds minimum contact time.

4. What must never be mixed with chlorine sanitiser?

Correct: acid cleaner. Chlorine + acid = chlorine gas. Lethal in an enclosed space.

5. Why keep a self-inspection before the health inspector arrives?

Correct: fix before, not after. A closure notice stops the business. A week of preparation prevents it.

Practical assignment — Module 7

Write a food safety manual for your cold room.

  1. Your HACCP plan in the seven-principle format. List every step from carcass-in to dispatch-out, the hazard at each step, the CCPs (mark them clearly), the critical limits, monitoring procedures, corrective actions, and verification.
  2. Your cleaning schedule in the three-tier format (daily, weekly, monthly). Post it on the wall as a written schedule.
  3. Your records system — the seven records, where they are kept, who updates them, and the frequency.
  4. Your sanitiser product and its correct working concentration, tested with what method.
  5. Your self-inspection checklist, adapted to your room.
  6. Your staff training plan — what each person must know before working in the room.
  7. A photograph of your records binder cover, or a sketch of what will be in it.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest food safety risk in your operation and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 1 and 8. The HACCP plan is the framework; the paragraph is where you commit to a specific control decision.
