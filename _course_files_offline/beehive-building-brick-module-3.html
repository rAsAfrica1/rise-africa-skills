Module 3

The Brick Hive — Walling and Internal Cavity

A brick hive is a fixed cavity surrounded by masonry. Its internal dimensions match a top-bar or frame hive; its walls are brick; its roof is removable for harvest. This module is the full build.

Module 3 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Lay a correct footing and base for a masonry hive
  • Build walls to the correct internal cavity dimensions
  • Provide for comb guides or top-bar supports
  • Leave correct bee-space gaps at every joint
  • Construct the roof so it can be removed for harvest

Lesson 3.1

Designing the cavity

A masonry hive has two essential dimensions: the internal cavity (where the bees live) and the top-bar or frame opening (where the beekeeper works). The cavity is a rectangular volume with an entrance at one end and a removable roof above.

What makes masonry different from wood

  • Walls are not perfectly flat. Bricks vary in dimension. Design the internal cavity 10 mm wider than the wooden equivalent to allow for the roughness.
  • Corners are bulky. The internal corner of a masonry hive wastes 40–60 mm of usable space on each corner. Top bars must be cut shorter than the wooden equivalent.
  • Humidity is higher. Masonry absorbs and holds moisture. Ventilation matters more than in wood.
  • Temperature is more stable. This is the advantage. Day/night swing is halved compared to a wooden hive.

Lesson 3.2

Footing and base

Footing

The footing is a concrete strip 200–300 mm wide, 200 mm deep, laid around the perimeter of the hive footprint. Below the footing, a 100 mm hardcore layer compacted into the subsoil.

Base slab

The base slab is a concrete slab laid inside the footing, 75–100 mm thick, sloped 1:200 toward the entrance end for drainage. Smooth finish inside.

Protection from rising damp

Between the base slab and the first course of brick, install a damp-proof course (DPC). A 0.5 mm polyethylene sheet is enough. This stops moisture rising from the ground into the hive cavity.

The base must be exactly level

If the base is out of level, every brick laid on top will be out of level, and the top bar openings at the top will not align. Check with the water level (Module 2). Correct before laying any brick.

Lesson 3.3

Walling

Brick choice

  • Fired clay brick — best. Hard, water-resistant, food-safe, does not support mould.
  • Cement-stabilised soil block — good. Cheaper, but needs good roof overhang to protect from rain.
  • Concrete block — acceptable. Hollow blocks must be filled or sealed to prevent bees nesting inside the block cores.
  • Do not use unfired mud block. It dissolves in the first wet season.

Mortar

A 1:4 cement-to-sand mix for the walling. Weather-struck joints (pointed as laid) for the exterior, and a flush joint on the interior to leave a smooth surface for the bees to propolise.

Laying discipline

  1. Lay a base course along all four walls with a level and string.
  2. Build up in courses to the top, keeping vertical joints staggered.
  3. Keep the internal cavity dimensions constant. Measure every 3–4 courses and correct if drifting.
  4. Leave the entrance opening at the base of one end wall. A 300 × 20 mm slot.
  5. Stop at the top of the wall. The top bar notches are cast or cut at the top course.

The top bar notch

The top course of the wall must have notches where the top bars sit. Two options:

  • Cut the notch in the top brick with an angle grinder. 30 mm wide, 20 mm deep. Repeat every 35 mm along the length.
  • Cast a concrete lintel with the notches pre-formed. More precise but requires shuttering.

Cutting the notches is faster and works well enough. But the spacing must be exact — 35 mm centres — or the bee space fails.

Lesson 3.4

Ventilation and the roof

Ventilation openings

Masonry holds moisture. A hive without ventilation stays damp, mouldy, and unsuitable for the colony. Provide:

  • Two ventilation slots at the top of the walls, opposite each other, 200 × 10 mm, covered with mesh.
  • An additional ventilation gap at the roof line, blocked in winter (in cool climates) or kept open (in hot climates).

Do not over-ventilate. Bees manage humidity themselves, but they cannot if the hive is wide open. Two slots plus a small roof gap are enough.

The roof

The roof must be removable for harvest. Two designs:

  • Flat slab roof. A concrete or metal slab, resting on the top of the walls, removable by two people. Heavy but simple.
  • Corrugated metal roof. A sheet of galvanised iron on a wooden frame, resting on the wall top. Light, easy to lift, needs an overhang of 200 mm on all sides.

Most smallholders use a metal roof with a wooden frame. Overhang of 200 mm protects the walls from rain and keeps the sun off the top of the walls.

Alignment

The roof must not block the top bar notches. Leave a 20 mm gap between the top of the top bars and the underside of the roof. This is bee space for the top of the hive.

Lesson 3.5

Curing, painting and finishing

Curing

Fresh mortar is alkaline. It must be allowed to cure for at least 28 days before the hive is inhabited. During curing, the interior may release a slight lime smell, which the bees dislike. Wash the interior with clean water before use, and let dry fully.

Painting

  • Exterior only. Paint the outside walls with masonry paint (not treated timber stain). Light colour reflects heat.
  • Do not paint the interior. Bees propolise it and paint is not food-safe.
  • If you must treat the interior (uncommon), use only clean water or a very weak beeswax solution.

Cracks

Any crack in the masonry is a place where ants enter and moisture collects. Fill all cracks with fresh mortar before the hive is inhabited. On the exterior, seal with weatherproof sealant or render.

The four curing rules: cure 28 days, wash interior, paint exterior only, fill every crack. Break any one and the colony will struggle in its first season.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. Why must a masonry hive cavity be 10 mm wider than a wooden equivalent?

Correct: allow for irregularities. Masonry is rougher than planed timber, and top bars must still fit and hang correctly.

2. How long must fresh mortar cure before the hive is inhabited?

Correct: 28 days. Fresh mortar is alkaline and bees dislike the smell. Wash the interior and let cure fully.

3. Where should ventilation openings be located in a masonry hive?

Correct: two top slots opposite. Allows moist air to rise and exit while keeping the entrance dry.

4. What is the correct top-bar spacing for a masonry hive?

Correct: 32–35 mm. Bee space — same as any other hive. Wrong spacing and the bees propolise or build burr comb.

5. Why should every crack in the masonry be filled before the bees move in?

Correct: ants and moisture. A crack is a permanent doorway for ants and a place where damp collects.

Practical assignment — Module 3

Build one brick hive on a real site.

  1. Your hive's internal cavity dimensions and top-bar spacing. State whether you are following a top-bar pattern or another design.
  2. Photographs of the footing and base slab, with a water level in shot showing the base is level.
  3. Photographs at three stages of walling — after 4 courses, after the top course, and with the notch cutting complete.
  4. The internal cavity measurements at the base and at the top, showing they match within 5 mm.
  5. Photographs of the entrance with a tape measure, showing the exact height and width.
  6. The roof — photographs of the frame and the metal covering in place.
  7. Curing record — dates of mortar completion, washing, and the first day you intend to introduce bees.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) on the single most difficult detail of the build and how you solved it.

Marks are weighted toward points 4 and 8. Internal cavity consistency is the difference between a hive that works and one that does not.
