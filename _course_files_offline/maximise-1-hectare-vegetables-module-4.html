4. Irrigation and Water Management

Water determines everything on a vegetable farm. Too little, plants die. Too much, roots rot. Delivering the right amount at the right time is the single biggest skill you can develop. This module covers the four irrigation methods, how to choose, and how to save water without losing yield.

Module 4 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 4.1 — Crop water requirements

Different crops need very different amounts of water. Watering them all the same wastes water and money.

CropWater need (L/ha/day)Critical stageDeficiency symptom
Tomato50,000–60,000Flowering and fruit fillFlower drop, small fruit
Pepper45,000–55,000Flowering and fruit setBlossom drop, sunscald
Cabbage30,000–40,000Head formationSplit heads, small heads
Onion30,000–40,000Bulb swellingThin, small bulbs
Carrot25,000–35,000Root enlargementWoody, split roots
Kale/rape25,000–35,000ContinuousSmall, tough leaves
Lettuce35,000–45,000Head formationBolting, bitter leaves
Herbs (basil)20,000–30,000ContinuousReduced oil content, wilting

How to know if your soil is too dry:

  1. Finger test. Push a finger 5 cm into the soil. Damp = no water needed. Dry = water today. Wet = skip.
  2. Plant indicators. Slight wilting at midday = stress beginning. Damp-wilting in the morning = severe stress.
  3. Tensiometer (for commercial). A $40–$100 instrument that reads soil moisture directly. Worth it above 0.5 hectares.
Practical exercise 4.1: Do the finger test in 3 different beds each day for a week. Note which beds dry out faster. This tells you which soil types or sun exposures you have.

Lesson 4.2 — Drip irrigation — the gold standard

Drip irrigation delivers water directly to each plant's root zone through small emitters. It is 30–50% more efficient than sprinklers and 60–70% more efficient than furrow irrigation.

FeatureValue
Water efficiency90–95% (vs 60–75% for sprinklers)
Cost per hectare (basic)$600–$1,500
Cost per hectare (pro with filtration)$1,500–$4,000
Lifespan5–10 years with care
Water savings per season$200–$800 (depends on water cost)
Labour savings2–4 hours per day
Best forTomato, pepper, onion, cabbage, herbs
Not suitable forCarrot, beetroot (need even surface moisture)

Basic drip system components:

  1. Water source and pump — borehole, dam, tank with gravity feed.
  2. Filtration — sand filter and/or screen filter. Critical — emitters clog without it.
  3. Mainline — 50–75 mm PVC or poly pipe from source to field.
  4. Sub-main and laterals — 16–32 mm poly pipes running along beds.
  5. Emitters — 1, 2, or 4 L/h per plant, spaced along laterals.
  6. Valves and pressure regulator — control flow to different zones.

Installation cost breakdown per hectare:

ComponentCost
Filtration$150–$400
Mainline (100 m of 63 mm)$200–$400
Laterals (6,500 m of 16 mm)$250–$500
Emitters and fittings$150–$300
Valves and regulators$80–$200
Installation labour$200–$400
Total$1,030–$2,200
The drip rule: Drip pays back in water savings, labour savings, and yield increase within 1–2 seasons on a tomato crop. On carrots and beetroot, use sprinklers instead.
Practical exercise 4.2: Get quotes for a drip system for 0.2 hectares. Install it yourself — the labour is 1 day for a small plot. Compare yield to a sprinkler-irrigated control plot next season.

Lesson 4.3 — Sprinkler irrigation — for root crops and pasture

Sprinklers spray water into the air and let it fall like rain. They are the best option for crops that need uniform surface moisture — carrot, beetroot, radish, and leafy greens.

TypeCost per hectareWater efficiencyBest for
Hand-move aluminium$400–$1,00065–75%Small plots, root crops
Wheel-move$800–$2,00070–80%0.5–3 hectares
Solid-set (fixed)$1,500–$3,50075–85%Commercial vegetables
Micro-sprinkler$600–$1,50080–90%High-value vegetables

Advantages of sprinklers:

  • Uniform coverage of a large area from one position
  • Cooling effect in hot weather — reduces heat stress
  • Can be moved easily between plots
  • Best option for carrots, beetroot, leafy greens

Disadvantages:

  • Water loss to evaporation and wind (15–40% in hot dry conditions)
  • Leaf wetness increases fungal disease
  • Not suitable for tall crops (tomato, pepper) because foliage blocks water
  • Higher energy cost to pump water to pressure
Practical exercise 4.3: Test a sprinkler system on a 10 × 10 m plot. Place 8 cups across the area during a 30-minute run. Measure water collected in each cup. If variance is over 30%, adjust sprinkler spacing.

Lesson 4.4 — Furrow irrigation — cheapest but wasteful

Furrow irrigation floods water down shallow channels between crop rows. It is 2,000 years old, requires no equipment, and is still the most common method in rural Africa.

FeatureValue
Capital cost$0–$100 (hand tools only)
Water efficiency40–60%
Labour2–5 hours per hectare per irrigation
Best forRow crops on level or gently sloping land
Not suitable forSandy soil (too much loss), sloped land (erosion), high-value crops

How to lay out furrows:

  1. Furrows 30–40 cm wide, 20–25 cm deep, 60–90 cm apart.
  2. Furrow length: 30–100 m depending on soil and slope.
  3. Slope: 0.1–0.5% for even water flow.
  4. Water flow rate: enough to reach the far end in 1–3 hours without ponding.
  5. Cut-off: stop water when it reaches the far end. Do not let it run for hours.

Why furrow loses money in the long run:

  • 50% of water is wasted — deep percolation and evaporation.
  • Uneven application — the top of the furrow gets more, the bottom less.
  • Labour intensive — moving water, cutting off, repairing furrows.
  • Not compatible with drip or sprinkler upgrades — you have to rip out the furrows.
The furrow rule: Use furrow irrigation only for low-value crops (maize, beans) or when you have no capital. For vegetables, upgrade to drip or sprinklers as soon as cash flow allows.
Practical exercise 4.4: If you currently use furrow, measure the flow rate and time it takes to reach the end of the furrow. Then measure how much water actually infiltrated by digging 30 cm down after irrigation. You will find 40–60% loss.

Lesson 4.5 — Watering schedule by crop and season

Water at the wrong time and you damage the crop. Water at the right time and the same amount produces 30% more yield.

CropDry season frequencyRainy season frequencyBest time of day
TomatoEvery 2 days, 6–8 mmEvery 4–5 daysEarly morning
PepperEvery 2 daysEvery 4–5 daysEarly morning
CabbageEvery 3 daysSkip if rainingEarly morning
OnionEvery 4 daysSkip if rainingEarly morning
CarrotEvery 3 daysSkip if rainingEarly morning
KaleEvery 3 daysSkip if rainingEarly morning
LettuceEvery 2 daysEvery 4 daysEarly morning

Critical watering rules:

  1. Water in the early morning. Evening watering leaves foliage wet overnight — fungal disease.
  2. Water deeply, less often. Shallow daily watering makes weak, shallow roots. Deep watering every 2–3 days grows deep roots.
  3. Never water at midday. Half evaporates before it reaches the roots.
  4. Reduce water 1–2 weeks before harvest. More flavour, better storage life.
  5. Stop watering 2 weeks before harvest for onion and garlic. Prevents rot in storage.
Practical exercise 4.5: Set a watering calendar for your main crops. Post it near the water source. Follow it for 2 weeks and adjust based on plant response.

Lesson 4.6 — Water harvesting and conservation

Rainwater is free. Capturing it is the cheapest way to reduce irrigation costs.

MethodCapacityCostBest for
Rooftop water tank5,000–20,000 L$200–$1,500Small plots, nursery water
Ground catchment dam100,000–1,000,000 L$800–$8,000Irrigation of 0.3–1 hectare
Percolation pondDepends on soil$200–$2,000Recharging boreholes
Swales / contour trenchesVariable$100–$500Sloped land — slow runoff, infiltrate
MulchingN/A$50–$200 per hectareReduce evaporation 30–50%

Mulching — the simplest water saver:

  • Organic mulch: dry grass, straw, chopped maize stalks, leaves. 5–10 cm layer on beds.
  • Benefits: 30–50% less evaporation, weed suppression, soil cooling, organic matter when it breaks down.
  • Never use fresh green material — it attracts termites and can spread disease.
  • Never use material with seed heads — you are planting weeds.

Water storage calculation:

A 10,000 L tank collected from a 50 m² roof during one 50 mm rain event captures 2,500 L. Over a rainy season with 600 mm of rain, you collect 30,000 L — enough to irrigate 500 m² of vegetables for 3 weeks.

The mulch rule: Mulch every bed as soon as seedlings are established. It is the single cheapest way to reduce your water bill and improve yield. Cost: $0 if you use on-farm materials.
Practical exercise 4.6: Mulch all your beds this week with 5 cm of dry grass or straw. Measure soil moisture at 5 cm depth before and after mulching. You will see the difference in 3 days.
