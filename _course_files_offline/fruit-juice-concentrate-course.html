â  SAFETY WARNING â Food Safety, Chemicals & Legal Liability

Juice is a high-risk food. Unpasteurised or under-pasteurised juice can carry Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, Listeria and Cryptosporidium, and has caused documented outbreaks and deaths worldwide. If you sell juice that makes people ill you face criminal liability, closure and permanent market loss.

  • Never sell juice that has not been pasteurised or otherwise validated for safety. Target 90â95Â°C for 15â30 seconds for single-strength juice, and hot-fill at â¥85Â°C. Verify with a calibrated thermometer every batch, not by eye.
  • Use only potable water. If you chlorinate wash water, keep free chlorine at approximately 2 ppm and rinse with potable water afterwards. Never use untreated river or borehole water on food-contact surfaces.
  • Handle sanitisers, caustic cleaners, chlorine and food preservatives (e.g. sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate, sulphur dioxide) strictly per label. Wear gloves and eye protection, never mix chlorine with acid, and store chemicals away from fruit, bottles and finished stock. Preservative limits are legally enforced under Codex and national standards â exceeding them is adulteration.
  • Do not use spoiled, mouldy or fallen fruit. Mycotoxins such as patulin in mouldy apples and aflatoxin in poorly dried fruit survive pasteurisation.
  • Label every bottle with batch code, production date, best-before date and allergen/ingredient list. Keep batch records for at least the shelf life plus six months â this is your legal defence and your buyer's audit trail.

If you are unsure whether your process is safe, do not sell the batch. One recall costs more than a year of production.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test before you contract. Run a 20 kg trial on each fruit lot and record yield in litres per kg. Contract farmgate volumes only against measured yield, not guesswork.
  2. Calibrate three instruments weekly: refractometer (Brix), pH meter and pasteuriser thermometer. Uncalibrated instruments are the most common cause of rejected batches.
  3. Lock your recipe card. Write down Brix, pH, acid and preservative dose per 100 L. If two staff can produce two different-tasting batches, you have no brand.
  4. Record every batch: fruit kg in, litres out, pasteurisation log, fill temperature, bottle count, batch code. This is your traceability and your bank's evidence of production.
  5. Price from cost, not from competitors. Recalculate Table 2 with your own quotes monthly; if fruit cost rises above 50% of total, renegotiate supply or switch fruit.
  6. Secure packaging early. Bottles, caps and labels are typically 25â30% of cost and are the most common cause of production stoppage in Africa. Hold at least two suppliers.
  7. Build cold-chain or shelf-stable options. If you cannot guarantee cold distribution, focus on hot-filled, preservative-compliant, ambient-stable product or concentrate instead of fresh juice.
  8. Get compliant before you scale. Confirm your national food standards authority registration, labelling rules and preservative limits. Export buyers will demand a HACCP-based food safety plan.
