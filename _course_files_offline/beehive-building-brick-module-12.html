Module 12 — Pests, Diseases and Colony Health Records

Health management in a permanent apiary — where movement is limited and records carry the load.

Learning outcomes

  • Recognise pests and diseases of honey bees by sight and symptom.
  • Apply health management when hives cannot easily be moved.
  • Manage structural pests — termites, ants, rodents — through design.
  • Keep records that track colony health and let you make decisions from evidence.
  • Plan for the worst: what you would do if AFB appeared on a fixed site.

1. The main colony health problems

The biology is the same as in any hive. Learn these eight; the difference in a brick apiary is how you respond.

AFB on a permanent site is a serious situation

The hive cannot be moved away, burned in a field, and replaced. The masonry chamber is a permanent structure. If AFB is confirmed:

• The bees and all comb are destroyed — by law.
• The chamber must be cleaned and sterilised thoroughly. Approved methods include scorching with a flame, or washing with a sporicidal disinfectant (peracetic acid, hydrogen peroxide, or a strong bleach solution).
• The chamber should be left empty for at least 12 months before reuse, unless a veterinary or bee inspector certifies it as safe.
• The plinth and base should be examined and replaced if necessary.
• Every hive nearby should be inspected by the same inspector.

This is why inspecting for AFB early and often is more important in a permanent apiary than anywhere else.

2. Varroa management in a fixed apiary

Varroa is the default chronic problem. In a permanent apiary, options that require moving hives or splitting are harder, so the emphasis shifts to in-place controls.

Monitoring

MethodFrequencyThreshold
Sticky board under a screened base (if fitted)Every 4 weeks in the active seasonMore than 10 mites/day
Sugar roll on 300 beesEvery 4 weeksMore than 3 per 100 bees
Alcohol wash on 300 beesMonthlyMore than 3 per 100 bees
Drone brood checkMonthlyMore than 5 mites per 100 drone cells

In-place IPM measures

  1. Screened base. Design this into the brick hive from the start — mites drop through and cannot climb back.
  2. Drone brood removal. Cut out sealed drone comb at regular intervals during the build-up season and freeze it before the mites emerge.
  3. Brood break. If you have a liftable crown board, caging the queen for 21 days breaks the mite reproduction cycle. Practical and effective.
  4. Treatment. Oxalic acid dribble or vaporisation during a broodless period; formic or thymol pads during the active season. Rotate actives between seasons.
  5. Requeening. A young queen from a varroa-tolerant line builds a more resistant colony over time.

Varroa treatment decision — worked example

Month 1 sugar roll: 2 mites / 100 bees — below threshold, monitor.
Month 2: 4 mites / 100 — above threshold. Treat now.
Month 3: 1 mite / 100 — treatment worked, monitor.

Two consecutive rising counts (e.g. 2, 4, 8) mean treatment is overdue. The mite population can double every 3–4 weeks in the build-up season.

3. Structural pest control

Brick hives face pests that a wooden hive does not — termites attacking the plinth, ants climbing the walls, rodents living in the base cavity.

PestApproachAction
TermitesBuild a physical barrier at the base of the plinth (stainless mesh or graded stone); treat soil with an approved termiticideQuarterly inspection; keep the plinth clear of vegetation; treat any tube immediately
AntsKeep the plinth face smooth; use a 50 mm overhang at the top of the plinth; oil or sticky barrier around the base joinAddress the underlying colony weakness — a strong colony keeps ants out
RodentsBase sealed and bee-tight; entrance reducer fitted in cold weather; metal guard on vulnerable entrancesBait or trap away from the bees; remove food sources
Small hive beetle larvaeGravel or hard surface under the plinth; no fallen comb; screened base if fittedSweep the plinth and ground weekly; remove fallen comb immediately
Lizards, toadsClear grass around the plinth; raise the entrance to 300 mm minimumInspect at dusk when these predators are most active

4. Recognising brood disease — visual check

Field test for AFB

  1. Insert a matchstick into a suspect capped cell.
  2. Stir and withdraw gently.
  3. If a brown, glue-like rope stretches 20 mm or more, suspect AFB.
  4. Smell the cell. AFB has a foul, rotting smell distinct from EFB's sour smell.
  5. If AFB is suspected, close the hive, mark it, stop working and call your inspector.
  6. Do not visit other hives until you have washed thoroughly and changed clothes.

5. Record keeping for a permanent apiary

Records matter more on a fixed site, because you cannot move problems away — you have to see them early and manage them in place.

RecordFrequencyPurpose
Inspection logEvery inspectionBrood, stores, queen, pest and disease signs, actions
Varroa monitoringMonthly in seasonTrend and treatment decisions
Treatment logEvery treatmentProduct, dose, date, withdrawal period
Structural inspectionQuarterlyTermites, damp, cracks, plinth condition
Harvest recordEvery harvestYield per hive, moisture, batch code
Movements of materialEvery eventFrames, boxes, queens brought onto the site

The value of a written record

Suppose two hives on your plinth row start showing patchy brood. Without records, you cannot tell whether this is normal or unusual. With records, you can see that both had below-threshold varroa counts two months ago and that both were fed from the same drum of syrup. The pattern points to a shared source. Without records, you guess; with records, you investigate.

6. Biosecurity for a fixed apiary

  • Dedicated tools for the site; do not bring tools from another apiary without cleaning.
  • Clean tools between hives with washing soda solution or a flame.
  • Never bring frames, boxes or queens from a source you cannot verify.
  • Do not accept swarms of unknown origin onto a permanent site.
  • Visit the healthiest hives first; suspect hives last; wash and change afterwards.
  • Keep a visitor log — who came, when, and whether they had their own bees.

7. Closing — course review

You have now designed, built and learned to run a brick hive system: plinth, base, walls, roof, ventilation and thermal mass, hive stands and apiary layout, feeding, harvesting and colony health. The material in this course and the Wood course covers the same biology; the difference is the building.

Brick hives suit hot, dry, high-swing climates and permanent apiaries where you can invest up front and manage for decades. They suit humid lowland climates less well unless paired with a wooden super and roof.

The next steps in your operation are usually one of:

  • Scale up hive numbers. The fixed positions on your apiary were planned for expansion in Module 9 — use them.
  • Move to queen rearing. A permanent apiary and reliable records are the foundation for queen rearing, which rewards both.
  • Value-add. Comb honey, wax products and propolis are natural extensions.
  • Teaching. A permanent, well-run brick apiary is an excellent teaching site.

The habit that matters most

Write it down. Every inspection, every count, every treatment, every harvest. A fixed apiary depends on records, because you cannot move a problem — you can only catch it early. The record is what catches it.
