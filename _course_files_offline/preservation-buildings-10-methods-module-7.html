Module 7 — Method 6: Sugar Preservation and Jam Kitchens

Water activity, pectin and pH — and the building that lets you control all three.

Home › Preservation Buildings — 10 Methods › Module 7

Learning outcomes

  • Explain how sugar preserves food — water activity, not sweetness.
  • Select the correct sugar concentration for jam, syrup, candied fruit and preserves.
  • Balance acidity and pectin to get a reliable set.
  • Lay out a jam kitchen with the correct process flow and equipment.
  • Test set, fill, seal, cool and store product safely.
  • Recognise and prevent the faults that cause spoilage and lost batches.

1. How sugar preserves

Sugar does not kill microorganisms. It removes the water they need. Dissolved sugar binds water molecules so tightly that bacteria, yeasts and moulds cannot draw on them.

The measure is water activity (aw), which runs from 0 (bone dry) to 1.0 (pure water).

awWhat growsProduct type
0.95–1.00Most bacteria, including pathogensFresh fruit, weak syrup
0.90–0.95Most yeasts, some bacteriaLight preserves, low-sugar jam
0.85–0.90Some yeasts and mouldsTraditional jam at 65 % solids
0.75–0.85Halophilic moulds and osmophilic yeasts onlyProper jam, marmalade, candied fruit
Below 0.75Almost nothingFruit leather, candied peel, syrups at 70 %+

The number that matters

Jam sets and keeps reliably at 65–68 % soluble solids measured on a refractometer at 20 °C. Below 63 % you are making fruit sauce, not jam — it must be refrigerated and eaten within weeks. Above 70 % you get crystallisation and a hard, sticky texture.

Target: 65 % for jam, 68 % for marmalade, 70–72 % for candied fruit and heavy syrup.

Add sugar in stages

Dumping all the sugar in at once slows evaporation and lengthens the boil, which destroys colour and pectin. Add it in three lots, waiting for the boil to recover between each. Total boil time drops and the set is better.

3. Acidity and pectin — the setting pair

Pectin needs acid to gel. Without acid, even high-pectin fruit will not set. Target pH 3.0–3.4 for jam.

FruitNatural pectinNatural acidityWhat to add
GuavaHighMediumLittle or nothing; maybe a squeeze of lime
Sour apple / crab appleVery highHighNothing — often used as a pectin base for other fruit
Mango (ripe)LowLow–mediumPectin + 15 mL lime or lemon juice per kg
PapayaLowLowPectin + 20 mL lemon juice per kg
StrawberryLowMediumPectin + 15 mL lemon juice per kg
Citrus peelHigh (in pith)HighNothing; boil peel to soften
Tomato (green)MediumHighSmall amount of pectin for chutney body

Pectin types

  • HM (high methoxyl) pectin: sets with sugar and acid at 65 % solids. Standard for jam.
  • LM (low methoxyl) pectin: sets with calcium, works at 40–50 % sugar. Use for low-sugar and reduced-calorie products.
  • Liquid pectin: convenient, but add at the very end of the boil — prolonged heat destroys it.
  • Powdered pectin: whisk into a little of the sugar before adding, or it forms lumps.

4. The jam kitchen — building layout

Sugar preservation is a wet, hot, sticky process. The building must be washable, drained, and laid out so that raw fruit and finished sealed jars never share a surface.

Building requirements

ElementSpecificationWhy
FloorHard, non-slip, sloped 1:80 to a gullySugar syrup on a floor is dangerously slippery
WallsTiled or hard-rendered to 1.8 m, light colourWashable, shows dirt, reflects light
CeilingSmooth, no ledges; 2.8–3.0 m over boilersSteam rises; ledges collect mould
VentilationExtraction hood over each boiling pan, 10–15 air changes/hourRemoves steam, sugar aerosol and heat
WaterPotable, hot and cold, at every benchCleaning and product
DrainageGully with removable strainer, grease/sugar trapPrevents blocked drains from sticky residue
Lighting500 lux at the fill bench, shatterproof fittingsColour judgement and foreign-body detection
Jar storeSeparate, dry, dark, 15–20 °CLight and heat degrade colour and pectin

6. Worked recipes

Mango jam — 10 kg batch

Ripe mango pulp: 5.0 kg
Sugar: 5.0 kg (added in 3 lots)
Lime or lemon juice: 150 mL
HM pectin: 40 g (pre-mixed with 200 g of the sugar)
Water: 300 mL

Method: heat pulp and water to 60 °C, add pectin-sugar mix, bring to rolling boil. Add remaining sugar in three lots. Boil hard until 104–105 °C and 65 °Brix. Add lime juice in the last 2 minutes. Skim, hot fill at ≥ 85 °C, cap, invert 2 min, cool upright.

Expected yield: ~7.5 kg finished jam in 250 g jars = 30 jars.

Guava cheese (high-pectin, no added pectin)

Guava pulp: 4.0 kg
Sugar: 3.5 kg
Lemon juice: 100 mL

Simmer pulp, add sugar in three lots, stir constantly, cook to 105 °C / 70 °Brix. Pour into a lined tray 20 mm deep, cool, cut into blocks, dust with sugar, wrap. Keeps 6+ months at aw ≈ 0.75.

Heavy syrup for bottled fruit

2 : 1 by weight — 4 kg sugar : 2 L water → ≈ 67 % solids.
Heat to dissolve, do not boil hard. Fill over fruit at 85 °C, cap, process in a water bath at 90 °C for 20 minutes for jars under 1 L.

Candied citrus peel

Peel: 1 kg → blanch 3× in fresh water to remove bitterness.
Then simmer in progressively stronger syrups: 1:2 → 1:1 → 2:1 → 3:1, 20 minutes each.
Drain, roll in sugar, dry 24 h at 40 °C. Final aw below 0.75.

7. Testing the set

  1. Refractometer (best): place 2 drops on the prism at 20 °C. Read °Brix. Target 65 for jam.
  2. Temperature: jam sets at 104–105 °C at sea level. Subtract 1 °C per 300 m of altitude.
  3. Plate test: drop a teaspoon onto a chilled plate, wait 1 minute, push with a finger. Wrinkles = set. Runs = not yet.
  4. Sheet test: lift the spoon; the jam should fall in a sheet or flakes, not a thin stream.

Do not guess the set

Under-set jam is the single largest cause of spoilage and refunds in small jam businesses. A refractometer costs less than one lost batch. Buy one before your first commercial sale.

8. Filling, sealing and cooling

  • Fill hot — at least 85 °C at the jar centre.
  • Fill to 6 mm headspace for jam, 10 mm for chutney with chunks.
  • Wipe the rim clean; any syrup on the rim prevents a seal and grows mould.
  • Cap immediately and invert for 2 minutes for high-acid products — this sterilises the lid. Then cool upright.
  • Cool on a slatted rack with 300 mm clearance; never stack hot jars.
  • Check the seal after 24 h: the lid should be concave and give a clear "pop" when opened.
  • Label with product, batch code, date of manufacture and best-before.

9. Faults and how to prevent them

FaultCausePrevention

Regulatory basics

  • Food business registration and, for most markets, an inspection of the premises.
  • Product testing for pH, °Brix and microbiological safety at least once per recipe.
  • Batch records: date, recipe, fruit source, pH, °Brix, jar count, defects.
  • Labelling: product name, ingredients in descending order, net weight, manufacturer, batch, best-before, storage instruction.
  • Allergen declaration if you use sulphites or add nuts.
