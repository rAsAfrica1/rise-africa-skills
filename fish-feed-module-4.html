Lesson 4.1~12 min

Grow-Out Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Set grow-out protein, energy and lipid specifications for tilapia, African catfish and carp
  • Build a least-cost grow-out mix from African ingredients while holding the protein-to-energy ratio
  • Compare farm-made and commercial grow-out feed on cost per kg of fish produced

Grow-out is where the tonnes are eaten and where the money is made or lost. Feed is 50-70 percent of production cost, and at grow-out you are buying most of that feed. A ten percent improvement here is worth more than perfection anywhere else on the farm.

The specifications relax, which is the good news. Nile tilapia grow-out needs 25-32 percent crude protein at 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg digestible energy, giving a protein-to-energy ratio of 8-10 kcal per gram of protein, with 5-8 percent lipid and 0.3-0.8 percent EPA plus DHA. African catfish need 28-35 percent protein at 3,200-3,400 kcal/kg with 7-10 percent lipid and 0.5-1.0 percent EPA plus DHA. Common carp need 25-30 percent protein at 2,800-3,200 kcal/kg with only 4-6 percent lipid and a 9-11 kcal per gram ratio. Channel catfish need 25-28 percent at 2,900-3,200 kcal/kg. Phosphorus is 0.6-0.8 percent for tilapia and 0.6-0.7 percent for African catfish. Lysine falls to 1.2-1.5 percent for tilapia grow-out and 1.6-1.8 percent for African catfish.

The protein-to-energy ratio is the discipline that stops you wasting money. Below 8 kcal per gram of protein you deposit excess fat and waste feed. Above 10 the fish burns protein for energy, which is expensive and generates ammonia. A 30 percent protein diet at 3,000 kcal/kg is exactly 10 kcal per gram, at the top of the range and workable. If you raise energy by adding oil, you should be able to lower protein, and that is where grow-out savings come from.

Ingredients. The reference's regional profile for sub-Saharan Africa gives local artisanal fish meal at USD 50-150 per tonne, soybean meal at USD 350-450 imported, corn and millet bran at USD 150-200 local, and rice bran at USD 100-150. Baseline commercial pellet is USD 0.35-0.55/kg at mill gate and USD 0.60-0.80/kg retail. A low-cost on-farm alternative of rice bran at 5-10 percent, soy at 30-35 percent, groundnut cake at 15-20 percent, maize at 15-20 percent and 5-10 percent local fish by-product comes to USD 0.25-0.40/kg, but requires on-farm mixing and processing.

Worked example, a 1,000 kg tilapia grow-out mix at 28 percent protein near Kisii, Kenya. Soybean meal 320 kg at USD 0.40 = USD 128, protein 147 kg. Groundnut cake 180 kg at USD 0.45 = USD 81, protein at roughly 42 percent = 76 kg. Local fish by-product meal 70 kg at USD 0.90 = USD 63, protein at 50 percent = 35 kg. Maize 200 kg at USD 0.22 = USD 44, protein 18 kg. Rice bran 90 kg at USD 0.15 = USD 13.50, protein 11 kg. Soybean oil 45 kg at USD 1.00 = USD 45. Mineral premix 30 kg and vitamin premix 13 kg together about USD 80. DL-methionine 2.5 kg at USD 5 = USD 12.50 and L-lysine 3 kg at USD 3 = USD 9. Phytase at USD 0.06/kg of feed = USD 60. Total protein 287 kg, so 28.7 percent, inside the 25-32 percent band. Total cost USD 536, so USD 0.536/kg.

Now convert that into what matters. Feed cost per kg of fish produced equals feed price multiplied by FCR. At USD 0.536/kg and a tilapia grow-out FCR of 1.8, the feed cost per kg of fish is USD 0.96. A commercial pellet at USD 0.75/kg achieving FCR 1.6 because the starch is properly extruded gives USD 1.20 per kg of fish. The farm mix wins, but only by USD 0.24, and only if the home pellet actually holds together and the starch is cooked. If the home pellet is cold-pressed with raw starch, FCR drifts to 2.1 and the cost becomes USD 1.13, and the advantage nearly vanishes. Always compare feeds on price multiplied by FCR, never on price alone.

One mycotoxin caution for grow-out, where maize inclusion is highest. Keep maize under about 20 percent of the feed where fumonisin risk is high, and keep aflatoxin B1 under 5 ppb for tilapia and under 10-20 ppb for catfish. Bentonite as a binder doubles as an adsorbent. Groundnut cake is a common aflatoxin carrier in West and East Africa and should be sourced dry and used fresh.

Growth Rate and Feed Conversion

In this lesson
  • Calculate FCR correctly from your own feed and harvest records
  • Identify the six drivers that move FCR most on a small African farm
  • Convert an FCR improvement into USD for your own pond

Feed conversion ratio is the single number that tells you whether your farm works. It is total feed given in kg divided by weight gain in kg. Feed 1,000 kg and gain 600 kg of fish and your FCR is 1.67. Lower is better.

What to expect, honestly. Nile tilapia fingerlings at 20-50 g at 27-30C run 1.3-1.5; juveniles at 50-200 g run 1.5-1.8; grow-out at 200-500 g runs 1.6-2.0. Below 20C tilapia FCR collapses to 3.0-5.0. Channel catfish run 1.4-1.6 as fingerlings, 1.6-1.9 as juveniles and 1.8-2.2 at grow-out, and below 20C they run 3.5-6.0 and are effectively dormant. African catfish are the most efficient of the tropicals at 1.2-1.4 as fingerlings and 1.4-1.7 from 50 to 500 g, which is a large part of why smallholders across Africa favour them. Common carp run 1.5-1.8 as fingerlings and 2.0-2.5 at grow-out over a two to three year cycle.

Notice that FCR worsens as fish get bigger in every species. That is normal biology, not a fault, and it is one of the reasons harvest timing matters: the last hundred grams costs more feed than the first hundred.

Six things move FCR, and you control most of them.

  1. Starch gelatinisation. Ungelatinised raw starch costs 10-20 percent on FCR because digestibility falls 20-40 percent.
  2. Water temperature. Every 1C below optimal costs about 3-5 percent in efficiency.
  3. Stocking density. Above about 100 tilapia per cubic metre, FCR worsens 10-15 percent through stress and competition.
  4. Protein-to-energy imbalance. Too little energy or too little protein costs 10-20 percent, and excess protein adds ammonia stress on top.
  5. Water quality. Ammonia above 0.1 mg/L costs 5-10 percent; dissolved oxygen below 4 mg/L costs 15-30 percent.
  6. Feed quality and digestibility. A feed above 80 percent ingredient digestibility gives 1.3-1.5; a feed below 60 percent gives 2.0 or worse.

One accounting warning. FCR as normally calculated includes feed eaten by fish that later died. Survival below 90 percent makes your FCR look worse than your feed really is. Before you change feed brands, check your counts.

Worked example. Fatima grows 5,000 tilapia at Fayoum, Egypt from 60 g to 400 g. Weight gain is 5,000 x 0.340 = 1,700 kg. At FCR 1.9 she needs 3,230 kg of feed; at USD 0.72/kg that is USD 2,325.60. At FCR 1.6 she needs 2,720 kg, costing USD 1,958.40. The difference is USD 367.20 on one pond cycle, which she achieves not by buying a different bag but by cooking her starch, feeding four times a day instead of twice, holding oxygen above 5 mg/L and grading once. Her revenue at 2,000 kg of harvested fish and USD 1.70/kg is USD 3,400, so feed at FCR 1.9 is 68 percent of revenue, at the very top of the 50-70 percent band and marginal, while feed at FCR 1.6 is 58 percent and the farm has a margin. The same pond, the same fish, two different businesses.

Growth rate matters alongside FCR because time is money in a seasonal climate. A fish that reaches 400 g in six months instead of eight gives you a chance at a second cycle, and tilapia at 25-30C reach 500 g in six to eight months. Anything that slows growth without improving conversion, such as chronic mild ammonia at 0.02-0.05 mg/L costing 2-15 percent of growth, is silently stealing a production cycle from you.

Keep the record simple: every bag of feed that goes into a pond written in a book, a 30-fish sample weight every two weeks, and an honest fish count at harvest. Those three records give you FCR, and FCR gives you everything else.

Carcass Quality and Meat Composition

In this lesson
  • Explain how dietary energy and lipid change body fat and dressing percentage
  • Manage feed ingredients that affect flesh flavour and shelf life
  • Judge when a buyer's quality preference justifies a more expensive feed

Your buyer does not buy FCR. He buys a fish that looks right, cuts right and tastes right. Several feeding decisions that seem purely technical show up directly on the slab.

The first and biggest is dietary energy relative to protein. A protein-to-energy ratio below 8 kcal per gram of protein causes excessive fat deposition. In practice that means a fish that is heavy in the belly, loses more weight when gutted, and has visible fat around the viscera. Farmers usually get there by adding cheap oil or cheap starch to a diet without lowering protein, or by feeding a fingerling-energy feed to a grow-out fish. Holding the ratio at 8-10 is not just about feed economics; it is about what the fish looks like when it is cut.

The second is lipid quality, which affects both flavour and shelf life. Polyunsaturated oils, and especially fish oil at 15-25 percent EPA plus DHA, oxidise readily. A rancid feed produces flesh that goes off faster and tastes wrong, and it destroys the vitamins in the bag on the way. That is why vitamin E is raised to 200-400 mg/kg where dietary lipid exceeds 10 percent or EPA plus DHA exceeds 1 percent. It is also why rice bran at 15-18 percent lipid must be used fresh within two to three weeks unless heat stabilised. Grow-out tilapia need only 0.3-0.8 percent EPA plus DHA, so a diet built mainly on vegetable oil is both cheaper and more stable, at 88-92 percent digestibility against 95 percent for fish oil.

The third is pigmentation, which matters in some markets and not others. Spirulina at 60-75 percent protein carries astaxanthin and beta-glucans, chlorella and scenedesmus carry carotenoids, and 10-20 percent inclusion gives 90-100 percent of control growth in tilapia fingerlings. But microalgae cost USD 3-20/kg, five to twenty times soybean meal, so they earn their place only as a 3-5 percent broodstock or specialty inclusion at USD 0.05-0.20/kg of feed, not as a routine grow-out ingredient. If your market pays a premium for colour or for a health claim, do the arithmetic; if it does not, spend the money on amino acids instead.

Fourth, off-flavour. This is usually a water problem rather than a feed problem, arising from cyanobacteria blooms in over-fertilised ponds, and the prevention is to keep the fertiliser nitrogen to phosphorus ratio at 3:1 to 5:1 and to aerate. It becomes a feeding problem because overfeeding is over-fertilising: uneaten feed and excreted nitrogen drive exactly the blooms that taint flesh. Microcystin from Microcystis blooms also bioaccumulates in fish organs.

Fifth, mycotoxins. Aflatoxin B1 is carcinogenic, causes liver tumours and immunosuppression, and tilapia should be held under 5 ppb against under 20 ppb for channel catfish. Fumonisin causes fatty liver and necrosis, with tilapia safe under 50 ppb. Ochratoxin A damages kidneys, tilapia safe under 5 ppb. These are food safety issues as much as fish health ones.

Worked example. Charles supplies a hotel market at Entebbe, Uganda that pays USD 2.20/kg for firm, clean-tasting tilapia over 400 g, against USD 1.60/kg at the roadside market. On a 1,500 kg harvest the premium is worth USD 900. To hold that contract he switches to a stabilised vitamin C and raised vitamin E feed, costing perhaps USD 0.05/kg more; at FCR 1.8 that is 2,700 kg of feed and USD 135 of extra cost. He also halves his maize inclusion in favour of clean sorghum to cut aflatoxin risk, adding perhaps USD 0.02/kg, USD 54. Total USD 189 against a USD 900 premium. The decision is not close. But it only works if the premium is real, so confirm the price with the buyer in writing before you change the feed.

The general principle: quality feeding is worth exactly what your market pays for quality, and not one dollar more.

Species-Specific Feeding

In this lesson
  • Contrast tilapia, African catfish and carp on protein, energy, oxygen and FCR
  • Choose the pellet form and feeding pattern that suits each species
  • Select the species that best fits your water, market and capital

The three species most African farms will handle behave differently enough that a single feeding routine cannot serve all of them. Set them side by side.

Nile tilapia. Optimal at 25-30C, tolerating 15-35C, growing to 500 g in six to eight months above 25C. Omnivorous with a long gut, so it handles plant material and gelatinised starch well: 20-30 percent soluble carbohydrate and 10-15 percent fibre are acceptable at fingerling stage. Grow-out protein 25-32 percent at 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg, lipid 5-8 percent, phosphorus 0.6-0.8 percent, FCR 1.6-2.0. Wants dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L, critical at 2.0 mg/L, and unionised ammonia below 0.05 mg/L. Feeds well on floating pellet at 6-8 mm, which lets you watch appetite. The key economic advantage is fast growth on a cheap, largely plant-based diet built around soybean meal at 40-50 percent of the diet with rice bran at 5-10 percent. The key weakness is sensitivity: it is the most aflatoxin-sensitive of the group at under 5 ppb, and it breeds in the pond, filling grow-out with unsaleable recruits.

African catfish. Optimal at 20-28C but tolerating 8-42C, which is a genuine advantage in variable African conditions. Air-breathing, so it survives to about 1.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen although it grows poorly below 4 mg/L, and it needs minimal aeration if the pond is stocked lightly. Grow-out protein 28-35 percent at 3,200-3,400 kcal/kg, lipid 7-10 percent, EPA plus DHA 0.5-1.0 percent, phosphorus 0.6-0.7 percent, lysine 1.6-1.8 percent, arginine 1.4-1.6 percent. FCR is the best of the group at 1.4-1.7. Prefers sinking pellet at 5-6 mm, often fed floating first and sinking after satiation. Suggested feedstuff base is 10-15 percent fish meal plus about 40 percent soy plus 10 percent canola. Its weaknesses are cannibalism when sizes spread and a shorter, less starch-tolerant gut. Its published cost-optimised diet is the cheapest of the group at USD 0.30-0.50/kg on farm.

Common carp. Optimal at 18-28C, tolerating 0-38C, but slow: two to three years to market size, with grow-out FCR 2.0-2.5. Herbivorous to omnivorous with no stomach, so it accepts the highest carbohydrate load, 30-40 percent soluble carbohydrate and 15-20 percent fibre, and digests gelatinised starch at 85-95 percent. Grow-out protein is the lowest at 25-30 percent, energy 2,800-3,200 kcal/kg, lipid only 4-6 percent, and the P:E ratio 9-11. Suggested base is canola and soy at 35-45 percent with rice bran at 10-15 percent. Its virtue is that it turns the cheapest vegetable ingredients into fish; its vice is time.

Worked example. Grace has two ponds at Bukoba, Tanzania, a stream supply, no electricity and a market that pays USD 1.70/kg for tilapia and USD 2.00/kg for catfish. She models one pond of 500 kg output. Tilapia at FCR 1.8 on feed at USD 0.60/kg needs 900 kg of feed, USD 540, revenue USD 850, gross margin over feed USD 310, in about seven months. African catfish at FCR 1.55 on feed at USD 0.65/kg needs 775 kg, USD 503.75, revenue USD 1,000, gross margin over feed USD 496.25, and catfish will tolerate her lack of aeration far better. Catfish looks clearly better on this arithmetic. What the arithmetic hides is that catfish fingerlings are harder to source and produce, cannibalism will cost her fish if she does not grade, and her buyer may only absorb a limited quantity at that price. Run the numbers for your own prices, then check the assumptions with your actual buyer before you stock.

Many farms run tilapia and African catfish together in the same pond, using the catfish to control tilapia recruits. That is a stocking decision covered in Module 11, but note the feeding consequence: the two species want different pellet behaviour and different protein levels, so you are feeding a compromise and should expect FCR between the two published bands.

Seasonal and Temperature Adjustments

In this lesson
  • Adjust ration percentage and expected FCR as water temperature changes through the year
  • Plan stocking and harvest dates around the warm season
  • Cost the feed wasted by feeding at winter or cold-season rates

A cold-blooded animal in an unheated pond is a seasonal business. Growth, appetite and feed conversion all move with water temperature, and the farmer who feeds the same ration in July as in January is either starving fish or poisoning water.

The numbers are stark. Nile tilapia at 27-30C convert feed at 1.6-2.0 at grow-out. The same fish at 18-20C convert at 3.0-5.0, with growth slowed 50-70 percent and feed intake minimal. Channel catfish at 27-30C run 1.8-2.2 and at 15-18C run 3.5-6.0, effectively dormant, with feeding minimal for months. Common carp at 20-25C run 2.0-2.5 and at 10-15C run 3.0-4.0. Every 1C below the optimum costs about 3-5 percent in conversion efficiency.

Feeding rates follow. Tilapia at 27-30C take 2-3 percent of body weight at low density, 3-4 percent at medium and 4-5 percent at high. At 20-25C those fall to 1-2, 2-3 and 2-3.5 percent respectively. Channel catfish at 25-28C take 1.5-2.5, 2.5-3.5 and 3-4 percent, and at 15-20C only 1-1.5, 1.5-2 and 2-2.5 percent. The standing rule is to halve the ration below 20C.

Much of tropical and subtropical Africa never sees a true winter, but a great many farms do see meaningful seasonal swings: highland areas in Kenya, Ethiopia, Rwanda and Zimbabwe, the southern African winter, and the cooling effect of long rains and cold nights at altitude. Depth matters too, since a shallow pond swings more than a deep one, and cage culture on a large lake is buffered by the water body.

Worked example. Thabo farms tilapia near Polokwane, South Africa, where pond temperature runs about 28C in summer and falls to about 19C for roughly ten weeks in winter. He has 3,000 fish averaging 250 g, so 750 kg. In summer at 3.5 percent he feeds 26.25 kg a day at USD 0.70/kg, USD 18.38. If he keeps feeding that rate through winter, 70 days of 26.25 kg is 1,837.5 kg of feed, USD 1,286. But at 19C the fish are converting at perhaps 3.5 rather than 1.8, so that feed yields only about 525 kg of gain instead of 1,020 kg, and much of the surplus feed rots and raises ammonia. If instead he halves the ration to 13.1 kg a day, he uses 917 kg, costs USD 642, and the fish gain roughly 262 kg. He has given up about 263 kg of theoretical gain that he was never going to get efficiently anyway, and saved USD 644 of feed. He would also be right to consider feeding at maintenance and simply holding the fish through the cold, since gain bought at FCR 3.5 costs USD 2.45 per kg of fish against a market of USD 1.70.

That calculation leads to the real seasonal decision, which is timing rather than ration. Stock so that the fastest-growing part of the cycle lands in the warm season. If tilapia reach 500 g in six to eight months at 25-30C, and your warm season is eight months long, stock at the start of it, not in the middle. Harvest before the cold rather than carrying fish through it at FCR 3.5. If you must carry fish over, carry the smallest ones, because they will grow fastest when the water warms and you are not paying to maintain large biomass through the dead months.

A few practical rules for the cold season: halve the ration below 20C, feed once a day in the warmest part of the afternoon rather than at dawn, use a smaller quantity of a higher-quality feed rather than a large quantity of a cheap one, and stop feeding entirely for a day or two if the fish visibly refuse feed rather than leaving it to rot. Watch the pellets: uneaten feed at 15 minutes is the clearest signal that the water is too cold for the ration you have set.

Pre-Harvest Conditioning

In this lesson
  • Plan a purging and conditioning period before harvest
  • Decide when to stop feeding relative to netting and transport
  • Calculate the feed saved and the quality gained by conditioning correctly

The last two weeks before harvest are the cheapest place on the whole farm to improve the money you receive, because you are dealing with the largest biomass you will ever hold and the fish are about to be judged by a buyer.

Three things are happening in this window and they pull in different directions.

First, feed conversion is at its worst. Tilapia at 200-500 g convert at 1.6-2.0 and channel catfish at 1.8-2.2, against 1.3-1.6 for the same fish as fingerlings. The last hundred grams is the most expensive hundred grams of the cycle. If your market pays no premium above a certain size, every day you feed beyond that size is spent buying fish at your worst conversion ratio. Check the price ladder at your market before you decide to grow on.

Second, flesh quality is set now. Excess dietary energy relative to protein, below 8 kcal per gram of protein, lays down visceral fat, and the buyer sees it when the fish is gutted. If you have been adding oil or cheap starch to stretch a grow-out feed, stop in the final weeks and return to a properly balanced 8-10 ratio. Off-flavour from cyanobacteria in an over-fertilised pond is the other quality risk, and it is a feeding problem because overfeeding is over-fertilising. Ease back the ration and increase exchange if you can in the last fortnight, and keep the pond fertiliser nitrogen to phosphorus ratio at 3:1 to 5:1.

Third, handling stress is coming. Fish are about to be netted, crowded, held and transported. A fish with a full gut uses more oxygen, fouls the transport water faster, and dies more readily. So the conditioning routine is: reduce the ration in the final week rather than pushing for last-minute gain, then stop feeding entirely for about 24 hours before netting so the gut is empty, hold fish in clean water at 1-2 g/L salt which also halves nitrite toxicity, and move them in the coolest part of the day.

How long to purge is a judgement, not a fixed rule. A day without feed is enough to empty the gut of a warm-water fish and is the common commercial practice; longer holds in clean flowing water are used where off-flavour must be cleared, and are only worth doing if you have clean water at the right temperature. Long starvation costs weight, so do not extend it without a reason.

Worked example. Simon harvests 4,000 tilapia averaging 420 g at Mangochi, Malawi, so 1,680 kg of fish, on feed at USD 0.72/kg. In the last two weeks he would normally feed at 3 percent, which is 50.4 kg a day, 705.6 kg over fourteen days, costing USD 508. At a grow-out FCR of 1.9 that feed buys about 371 kg of gain, worth USD 631 at USD 1.70/kg, so the margin over feed on those two weeks is USD 123, which is thin. If instead he tapers to 2 percent for ten days and fasts for the final day, he uses about 336 kg of feed, USD 242, buying about 177 kg of gain worth USD 301, a margin of USD 59 but with lower ammonia, cleaner flesh and a much lower risk of transport mortality. If his usual transport loss is 4 percent, 160 fish, roughly 67 kg worth USD 114, and correct conditioning halves it, he recovers USD 57. The two options are close on paper, and the conditioning route wins on risk, on water quality in the final week, and on how the fish looks to the buyer.

One final commercial point that sits alongside Module 12's business content: know your price ladder before you set your harvest date. If your buyer pays USD 1.80/kg above 300 g and USD 1.10/kg below it, the value of pushing an already-400 g fish to 450 g is only USD 0.09 per fish, and at FCR 1.9 the feed to add 50 g costs about 95 g of feed, USD 0.068, plus the risk of holding the pond another fortnight. Grow-out ends when the price ladder stops paying, not when the fish stops eating.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. The optimal protein-to-energy ratio for grow-out fish is:

Below 8 causes excess fat deposition; above 10 the fish burns protein for energy and generates ammonia.

2. African catfish grow-out crude protein should be:

African catfish need 28-35 percent at grow-out, slightly above tilapia at 25-32 percent.

3. The correct way to compare two feeds is:

Feed cost per kg of fish produced is price times FCR; a cheap feed with poor conversion can cost more.

4. Feed represents what share of aquaculture production cost?

Feed is 50-70 percent of production cost, which is why grow-out formulation dominates farm profitability.

5. Maize inclusion should be limited where fumonisin risk is high to about:

Limiting corn to under 20 percent of the feed and adding an adsorbent such as bentonite is the standard mitigation.

6. FCR is calculated as:

FCR is total feed in kg divided by weight gain in kg; 1,000 kg feed for 600 kg gain is 1.67.

7. Which species has the best grow-out FCR of those covered?

African catfish run 1.4-1.7 at grow-out, better than tilapia at 1.6-2.0 and carp at 2.0-2.5.

8. Feeding raw, ungelatinised starch worsens FCR by roughly:

Digestibility falls 20-40 percent, costing 10-20 percent on FCR, which is why cooking starch pays.

9. Growing 1,700 kg of fish at FCR 1.6 instead of 1.9, with feed at USD 0.72/kg, saves:

3,230 kg minus 2,720 kg is 510 kg of feed; 510 x 0.72 = USD 367.20.

10. Survival below 90 percent affects reported FCR by:

Feed eaten by fish that later died still counts against surviving weight gain, inflating apparent FCR.

11. Excessive fat deposition in grow-out fish usually results from:

Below 8 kcal DE per gram of protein the surplus energy is stored as fat, lowering dressing percentage.

12. Vitamin E is raised to 200-400 mg/kg when:

Polyunsaturated oils oxidise readily, so high-lipid and high-HUFA feeds need elevated vitamin E.

13. Spirulina is generally limited to a small inclusion because:

At 60-75 percent protein it is nutritionally good but far too expensive for routine grow-out inclusion.

14. Off-flavour in pond fish is most often linked to:

Blooms from excess nutrients taint flesh; keeping the fertiliser N:P ratio at 3:1 to 5:1 and aerating prevents them.

15. Spending USD 189 on better feed to secure a USD 900 quality premium is justified provided:

Quality feeding is worth what the market actually pays, so confirm the premium in writing first.

16. Which species tolerates the lowest dissolved oxygen?

African catfish are air-breathers and survive to about 1.0 mg/L, though they still grow poorly below 4 mg/L.

17. Which species tolerates the highest dietary carbohydrate?

Carp juveniles take 30-40 percent soluble carbohydrate and digest gelatinised starch at 85-95 percent.

18. African catfish grow-out FCR is typically:

1.4-1.7, the best of the tropical species, which is a large part of their appeal to smallholders.

19. Which pellet form suits African catfish best?

Catfish are bottom feeders; they are often fed feed that floats initially and sinks after satiation.

20. Common carp's main disadvantage for a small farm is:

Carp grow slowly, with a two to three year cycle and grow-out FCR of 2.0-2.5, tying up pond capacity.

21. Tilapia FCR below 20C is typically:

Conversion collapses to 3.0-5.0 with growth slowed 50-70 percent and appetite minimal.

22. The standing rule when water falls below 20C is to:

Appetite falls sharply, so the ration is halved; uneaten feed in cold water is pure pollution.

23. Each 1C below the species optimum costs about:

Roughly 3-5 percent of feed conversion efficiency per degree below optimal temperature.

24. In the cold season the daily feed is best given:

Water and fish are warmest and most willing to feed in the afternoon, so intake and digestion are best then.

25. If you must carry fish through a cold season, it is best to carry:

Small fish cost less to maintain and grow fastest when water warms, so carrying them wastes least feed.

26. Why is the last stage of grow-out the most expensive?

Conversion worsens with size, so the final hundred grams costs more feed than any earlier hundred grams.

27. Fish should be fasted before netting for about:

About a day empties the gut, lowering oxygen demand and reducing fouling of holding and transport water.

28. Off-flavour risk in the final weeks is best reduced by:

Overfeeding is over-fertilising; easing the ration and exchanging water reduces the blooms that taint flesh.

29. Salt in holding and transport water should be about:

1-2 g/L roughly halves nitrite toxicity and eases osmotic stress, at almost no cost.

30. The decision to grow fish on beyond market size should be based on:

Grow-out ends when the price ladder stops paying for the extra weight, given FCR at that size.
