1. Macronutrients and Micronutrients for Sweet Potato

Sweet potato needs 16 nutrients to grow well. The big three are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Nitrogen makes leaves and vines grow strong. Phosphorus helps roots form and develop. Potassium is critical for tuber size, starch content, and storage quality. A typical sweet potato crop yielding 20 tonnes per hectare removes about 80 kg N, 40 kg P2O5, and 120 kg K2O from the soil. That means potassium is the most important nutrient for sweet potato. If your soil is low in potassium, you will get small, poor-quality tubers even if you add plenty of nitrogen.

Secondary nutrients are calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and sulfur (S). Calcium builds strong cell walls and helps root development. Magnesium is part of chlorophyll, so it keeps leaves green. Sulfur helps form proteins. In many African soils, sulfur is low because farmers use little organic matter. Micronutrients like zinc (Zn), boron (B), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and copper (Cu) are needed in tiny amounts but are essential. Zinc deficiency causes small leaves and short internodes. Boron deficiency leads to cracked tubers and poor storage. Iron deficiency shows as yellow leaves between green veins, common in high-pH soils.

To know what your soil lacks, take a soil test. In Kenya, KALRO and private labs charge about 2,000 to 4,000 KES per sample for a full analysis. In Nigeria, IITA and state agricultural offices offer testing. Collect soil from 15 spots in your field, mix in a clean bucket, and take a 500g sample. Test before planting. If you cannot afford a lab test, use a simple field kit or observe deficiency symptoms. But remember: symptoms appear after yield is already lost. Prevention is better.

Organic matter is your best friend. Compost, manure, and crop residues supply all nutrients slowly. A good target is 5 to 10 tonnes of well-rotted manure per hectare per season. Combine that with the right mineral fertilizers based on soil test. For example, if your soil test shows low K, apply 100 kg of muriate of potash (0-0-60) per hectare. If P is low, apply 50 kg of triple superphosphate (0-46-0). Never guess. Sweet potato is a heavy feeder, but over-fertilizing with nitrogen gives you huge vines and tiny tubers. Balance is key.

2. Fertilizer Types: Organic vs. Synthetic

Organic fertilizers come from plant or animal sources: compost, manure, poultry litter, green manures, and biochar. They release nutrients slowly, improve soil structure, hold water, and feed soil microbes. A tonne of cattle manure contains roughly 5 kg N, 2 kg P2O5, and 5 kg K2O. Poultry litter is richer: about 20 kg N, 15 kg P2O5, and 15 kg K2O per tonne. Organic matter also helps sandy soils retain nutrients and clay soils drain better. But organic fertilizers are bulky, low in concentration, and may carry weed seeds or pathogens if not composted well. They are best applied 3-4 weeks before planting.

Synthetic fertilizers are manufactured, concentrated, and fast-acting. Common examples for sweet potato include urea (46-0-0), triple superphosphate (0-46-0), muriate of potash (0-0-60), and compound blends like NPK 17-17-17 or 12-24-12. A 50 kg bag of urea gives 23 kg of actual nitrogen. Synthetic fertilizers are easy to apply, easy to store, and you can precisely match soil test recommendations. But they can burn roots if over-applied, leach into groundwater, and acidify soil over time. They also do nothing to improve soil organic matter.

The best strategy is integrated soil fertility management (ISFM): combine organic and synthetic. Start with organic matter to build the soil. Then use synthetic fertilizers to supply the exact nutrients your crop needs at the right time. For example, apply 5 tonnes of compost per hectare at land preparation. Then at planting, band 100 kg/ha of NPK 17-17-17. Six weeks later, side-dress with 50 kg/ha of urea. This gives both slow-release and quick-release nutrients.

Cost matters. In many African markets, a 50 kg bag of urea costs about 2,500 to 3,500 KES (or 20,000 to 30,000 NGN). Compost is cheap if you make it yourself. Poultry litter may be free or low-cost from nearby farms. Calculate the cost per kg of nutrient. Synthetic nitrogen from urea costs about 100 KES per kg N. Organic nitrogen from manure may cost less but you need more labour to transport and spread. Choose based on what is available, affordable, and safe for your soil.

Do not mix synthetic and organic randomly. Apply organic first, incorporate it into the soil, then apply synthetic fertilizers just before planting or as side-dress. Never leave synthetic fertilizer on the soil surface without coveringâsun and rain waste it. Store fertilizers in a dry, dark place, off the floor, away from children and animals. Use gloves when handling. Both types have a place. The smart farmer uses both.

3. Calculating Fertilizer Rates Based on Soil Tests

A soil test report gives you numbers like 'phosphorus: 8 ppm' or 'potassium: 0.2 cmol/kg'. These are meaningless unless you convert them to fertilizer bags per hectare. First, know your target yield. For sweet potato, a realistic good yield is 20-30 tonnes per hectare. The crop removes about 4 kg N, 2 kg P2O5, and 6 kg K2O per tonne of fresh tubers. So for 25 tonnes, you need 100 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, and 150 kg K2O per hectare.

Next, subtract what your soil already provides. Soil tests usually report available nutrients in kg/ha or ppm. If your test shows 20 kg/ha of available P2O5, you only need to add 30 kg/ha (50 - 20). For nitrogen, soil tests are less reliable because N moves. Assume your soil supplies about 30 kg N/ha from organic matter. So you need 70 kg N/ha. For potassium, if your test shows 100 kg/ha K2O, you need 50 kg/ha extra.

Now convert nutrient needs to fertilizer products. Urea is 46% N. To get 70 kg N, divide 70 by 0.46 = 152 kg urea per hectare. Triple superphosphate (TSP) is 46% P2O5. To get 30 kg P2O5, divide 30 by 0.46 = 65 kg TSP per hectare. Muriate of potash (MOP) is 60% K2O. To get 50 kg K2O, divide 50 by 0.60 = 83 kg MOP per hectare. If you use compound NPK 17-17-17, each 100 kg gives 17 kg of each nutrient. To get 30 kg P2O5, you need 176 kg of NPK 17-17-17. That also gives 30 kg N and 30 kg K2O. Then you need extra urea for N and extra MOP for K.

Work in small plots first. If your field is 0.5 hectare, divide all rates by 2. So you would need 76 kg urea, 33 kg TSP, and 42 kg MOP for half a hectare. Weigh fertilizers with a simple kitchen scale or a spring balance. Do not guess by handfuls. A handful of urea weighs about 50g, but that varies. Calibrate your hand: fill a 500ml bottle with urea and weigh it. That gives you a measuring cup.

Always adjust for soil type. Sandy soils leach nitrogen and potassium quickly, so split applications. Clay soils hold nutrients better but may fix phosphorus. If your soil pH is below 5.5, add lime firstâ1 to 2 tonnes per hectare of agricultural limeâbecause acidic soil locks up phosphorus. If pH is above 7.5, phosphorus may also be unavailable. Test pH along with nutrients. Repeat soil tests every 2-3 years. Keep records of what you applied and your yields. That is how you learn your field's true needs.

4. Application Methods: Broadcast, Band, and Side-Dress

Broadcasting means spreading fertilizer evenly over the whole field, then incorporating it into the top 10-15 cm of soil with a hoe or plough. It is fast and good for phosphorus and potassium, which do not move much in soil. But broadcasting is wasteful for nitrogen because it can volatilize or leach. For sweet potato, broadcast all your phosphorus and potassium fertilizers plus half your nitrogen at land preparation, 2-3 weeks before planting. Use a cyclone spreader or broadcast by hand. A good rate: 100 kg/ha of TSP and 150 kg/ha of MOP. Incorporate immediately.

Banding means placing fertilizer in a narrow strip 5-10 cm to the side and 5-10 cm below the planting ridge. This concentrates nutrients near the roots, reduces waste, and is especially good for phosphorus and potassium. For sweet potato, band 50 kg/ha of NPK 17-17-17 at planting. Open a furrow, drop the fertilizer, cover with 2-3 cm of soil, then place your vines. Never let fertilizer touch the vines directlyâit will burn them. Banding is more labour-intensive but gives higher use efficiency. On a 0.5 ha plot, you need only 25 kg of NPK, which is easy to carry in a bucket.

Side-dressing means applying fertilizer alongside the growing plants, usually 4-6 weeks after planting. This is the best way to give nitrogen because the vines are actively growing and roots are established. Use urea or ammonium sulphate. Apply 50 kg/ha of urea (23 kg N) in a band 10-15 cm from the plant base, then cover lightly with soil. Do not broadcast urea on the surfaceâup to 30% of the nitrogen can be lost to the air within a week. If you have sandy soil, split the side-dress into two applications: 25 kg/ha at 4 weeks and 25 kg/ha at 8 weeks.

For organic fertilizers, broadcasting is common. Spread 5-10 tonnes of compost or manure per hectare and incorporate it 2-4 weeks before planting. Poultry litter can be banded at 2-3 tonnes per hectare. If using fresh manure, apply at least 4 weeks before planting to avoid burning roots. For liquid organic fertilizers like compost tea, apply as a side-dress using a watering can, 1 litre per plant, diluted 1:5 with water.

Tools you need: a hoe, a bucket, a weighing scale, and a measuring cup. For banding, a simple stick can mark the furrow. For side-dressing, a hand hoe to make a shallow trench. Always cover fertilizer with soil. Never apply on wet leaves or before heavy rain. If rain is expected within 24 hours, wait. The goal is to get nutrients into the root zone, not into the air or runoff. Match the method to the nutrient: broadcast P and K, band starter NPK, side-dress N. That is the sweet potato way.

5. Timing Fertilizer Applications for Maximum Uptake

Sweet potato has a 3-5 month growing cycle. Nutrient uptake peaks between 6 and 10 weeks after planting, when vines are rapidly growing and tubers begin to form. If you apply all fertilizer at planting, much of the nitrogen will be gone by the time tubers need it. If you apply too late, you get vines but no tuber bulking. Timing is everything.

At land preparation (2-4 weeks before planting): Apply all organic matterâcompost, manureâand all phosphorus and potassium fertilizers. Incorporate into the top 15 cm. This gives slow-release nutrients and builds soil structure. If you use lime, apply it now, 4-6 weeks before planting. Lime needs time to react. For a 0.5 ha plot, spread 1 tonne of lime and 50 kg of TSP plus 75 kg of MOP.

At planting: Apply a starter fertilizer banded near the vines. Use NPK 17-17-17 at 100 kg/ha (50 kg for 0.5 ha). This gives a small amount of nitrogen to get vines started, plus phosphorus for root development. Do not use urea at plantingâit is too strong and can burn young roots. If you have no NPK, use 50 kg/ha of DAP (18-46-0) banded. DAP is high in phosphorus, which is what young roots need most.

At 4-6 weeks after planting: Side-dress with nitrogen. This is when vines are running and the plant needs a boost. Apply 50 kg/ha of urea (or 100 kg/ha of ammonium sulphate). Place it in a band 10-15 cm from the plant base and cover with soil. If your soil is sandy, split this into two: 25 kg/ha at 4 weeks and 25 kg/ha at 8 weeks. On a 0.5 ha plot, that is 25 kg of urea total.

At 8-10 weeks after planting: If your soil test showed low potassium, apply a second dose of MOP at 50 kg/ha. Potassium is critical for tuber bulking. This is also a good time to foliar spray micronutrients if you saw deficiency symptoms. Use a knapsack sprayer with 2g of zinc sulphate and 1g of borax per litre of water. Spray in the early morning or late evening.

Do not apply nitrogen after 10 weeks. Late nitrogen encourages vine growth at the expense of tubers. Also, stop all fertilizer 3-4 weeks before harvest. This improves tuber quality and storage life. Keep a calendar: mark planting date, then 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 10 weeks. Set reminders on your phone. Rainfed farmers: apply fertilizer when soil is moist but not waterlogged. If drought, wait for rain. Fertilizer without water is wasted money. The right nutrient at the right time is worth more than double the amount at the wrong time.

6. Avoiding Nutrient Deficiencies and Toxicities

Nutrient deficiencies show up as visual symptoms on leaves and tubers. Learn to read them early. Nitrogen deficiency: older leaves turn pale green to yellow, vines are thin and short. Phosphorus deficiency: leaves are dark green with purple or reddish tints, especially on undersides; roots are small and fibrous. Potassium deficiency: leaf edges scorch and turn brown, starting on older leaves; tubers are small and may have dark internal spots. Magnesium deficiency: yellowing between veins on older leaves, often called 'interveinal chlorosis'. Calcium deficiency: new leaves are distorted, curled, and may die at the tips; tubers have cavities. Boron deficiency: growing points die, leaves are thick and brittle, tubers crack and have brown patches inside.

To correct deficiencies, act fast. For nitrogen, side-dress 50 kg/ha of urea and water it in. For phosphorus, band 50 kg/ha of TSP. For potassium, side-dress 50 kg/ha of MOP. For magnesium, spray 2% Epsom salt solution (20g per litre) on leaves. For calcium, apply lime or gypsum at 500 kg/ha. For boron, spray 0.1% borax solution (1g per litre) â but be careful, boron toxicity is easy. Never exceed 2g per litre.

Toxicity happens when you over-apply. Nitrogen toxicity: dark green, lush vines with few tubers; leaves may be brittle. Potassium toxicity: causes magnesium and calcium deficiency because K competes for uptake. Boron toxicity: leaf edges turn yellow then brown and die; this is common if you apply too much borax. Manganese toxicity: occurs in very acidic soils (pH below 5.0); leaves show brown spots and crinkling. The best cure for toxicity is prevention: follow soil test recommendations, never double the dose, and always mix fertilizers well into the soil.

Soil pH is the master key. Sweet potato grows best at pH 5.8 to 6.5. If pH is below 5.5, lime it. If above 7.5, add elemental sulfur or organic matter like compost. At high pH, iron, zinc, and manganese become unavailable, causing deficiency. At low pH, aluminum and manganese become toxic. Test pH every year. A simple pH meter costs 5,000 KES or a test kit 2,000 KES. It is worth it.

Organic matter prevents most deficiencies and toxicities. It chelates micronutrients, buffers pH, and feeds soil life. Aim for 2-3% organic matter in your soil. If you see symptoms, do not guess. Take a soil sample and send it to a lab. In the meantime, foliar feed with a balanced micronutrient mix. Keep a notebook: record symptoms, what you applied, and the result. Over time, you will become your own soil doctor. Your sweet potato yield will thank you.

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is one of Africa's most strategic food and cash crops. It matures in 3â5 months, tolerates marginal soils, and returns more calories per hectare per day than maize or cassava under good management. But the crop's reputation for "growing anywhere" has cost African farmers dearly. Yields across sub-Saharan Africa average 4â6 t/ha, while well-managed commercial fields in Egypt, South Africa, and Kenya reach 25â40 t/ha. Sweet potato is a root crop, so the economics of fertilization differ sharply from cereals: excess nitrogen produces vines at the expense of roots, while potassium drives root bulking and starch accumulation. Getting the balance right is a margin decision, not just an agronomic one.

4. Application Methods: Broadcast, Band, and Side-Dress

Broadcast: Spreading fertilizer uniformly and incorporating 10â15 cm deep. Best for phosphorus and potassium on low-fertility soils and for bulky organic manures. Downside: high volumes, and nitrogen can be lost to volatilization if left on the surface.

Band: Placing fertilizer in a furrow 5â8 cm to the side and 5 cm below the planting ridge. This concentrates P near the developing roots, reducing fixation in acidic or high-iron soils common in the African tropics. Banding typically saves 20â30% of P compared with broadcasting.

Side-dress: Applying N (usually urea or CAN) alongside the ridge at 4â6 weeks after planting. This matches the vine-growth peak without pushing excessive vegetative growth.

5. Timing Fertilizer Applications for Maximum Uptake

Sweet potato has three distinct uptake windows:

  1. Week 0 (planting): Full P and half of K banded at planting. P is immobile and must be near roots from day one.
  2. Weeks 4â6 (vine establishment): Split N (urea or CAN) side-dressed. This fuels canopy without triggering excessive vine growth.
  3. Weeks 8â12 (root bulking): Remaining K applied as MOP side-dress or fertigation. K drives starch translocation into roots.

Stop all nitrogen by week 8. Late N produces vines, not roots, and delays maturity â a direct hit on your harvest calendar and market window.

6. Avoiding Nutrient Deficiencies and Toxicities

Deficiencies are easier to prevent than cure. Routine soil testing every two seasons, combined with visual scouting, catches problems early. Toxicities â usually from over-application of a single nutrient â are harder to reverse and costlier. Excessive K blocks Mg and Ca uptake; excessive P induces Zn deficiency; excessive N invites aphids and sweet potato weevil.

On acidic soils (pH below 5.5, common in the humid tropics), lime at 1â2 t/ha before planting. On saline soils (common in irrigated schemes in Egypt, Sudan, and northern Nigeria), avoid MOP and use potassium sulphate instead.
