1. First Conjugation Verbs

In Russian, most verbs in the present tense follow two main conjugation patterns. First conjugation verbs are the most common. They typically end in -, -, or - in their infinitive form. For example, (to read), (to walk), and (to be ill). To conjugate, you drop the infinitive ending and add the present tense endings: -/-, -, -, -, -, -/-. 3. Negation and Questions

Negation in Russian is simple: just add before the verb. For example, (I work) becomes (I don't work). (She speaks) becomes (She doesn't speak). The word is pronounced 'nye' and is unstressed. 4. Verb Aspects: Introduction

Russian verbs come in pairs: imperfective and perfective. The imperfective aspect focuses on process, repetition, or ongoing action. The perfective aspect focuses on completion or a single result. In the present tense, only imperfective verbs are used. Perfective verbs have no present tense form; they have future meaning when conjugated like present. For example, (imperfective, to read) vs (perfective, to read completely). Present: (I am reading a book). Future perfective: (I will read the book). So in present tense, you always use imperfective. Common pairs: / (to do), / (to write), / (to say), / (to buy). In African business contexts, you might say: (I buy goods every day) - imperfective, repeated action. (Tomorrow I will buy goods) - perfective, future. Notice the different verb. To form the perfective future, conjugate like present: , , etc. But for now, focus on present tense imperfective. Practice identifying pairs: /, /, /. Use imperfective in present: (I am preparing lunch). (I am watching news). Then imagine future perfective: (I will prepare lunch). (I will watch news). This distinction is crucial. Start with 10 common verb pairs. Write sentences in present imperfective. For example: (I work) - imperfective. (I will do) - perfective future. Don't mix them up. Practice with flashcards. Listen to how native speakers use aspects in context. Soon, you'll grasp the logic. Spend 15 minutes daily on aspect pairs.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Present Tense Verbs & Simple Sentences for African Agribusiness

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working toolkit for Russian-language communication in agribusiness settings. Whether you are negotiating a grain supply contract in Rostov, coordinating a shipment of cocoa from Lagos to St. Petersburg, or hosting a Russian technical delegation at a processing plant in Nairobi, the present tense is the backbone of everyday professional Russian. Russian has no separate continuous tense: can mean "I read," "I am reading," or "I do read." Context and time markers do the rest of the work. For business purposes, this is an advantage one form covers most routine communication.

This module focuses on the two productive verb conjugations, negation and question formation, an introduction to aspect, and the sentence patterns you need to describe daily operations, schedules, and ongoing transactions. All examples below are framed around African agribusiness export and import workflows: cocoa, coffee, tea, cashew, sesame, cut flowers, and horticulture moving between African producers and Russian-speaking buyers, logistics firms, and certification bodies.

Negation is simple: place immediately before the verb. . "We are not dispatching coffee today." Russian uses double negation in some structures, but for business statements, a single is the rule.

SAFETY / COMMERCIAL WARNING

Do not rely on present-tense forms alone for legally binding commitments. Russian contracts distinguish (perfective) and (imperfective) aspect, and the present tense of a perfective verb does not exist it becomes future meaning. A phrase such as ("we dispatch / are dispatching") describes routine or ongoing action, not a guaranteed one-time delivery. For payment, delivery dates, and liability, insist on written contract language reviewed by a qualified legal translator. Misunderstanding aspect in a dispute can cost a shipment or a payment.

3. Verb Aspects: Introduction for Business Use

Every Russian verb belongs to an aspect pair. The imperfective aspect describes process, repetition, or general fact. The perfective aspect describes a completed, single result. In the present tense, only imperfective verbs have forms perfective verbs in present-tense form actually express future meaning.

Example pair: (imperfective, "to dispatch / be dispatching") vs. (perfective, "to dispatch once, complete"). . "We dispatch coffee every week." (routine) vs. . "We will dispatch the coffee tomorrow." (completed future action).

For African exporters dealing with Russian buyers, mastering this distinction prevents costly ambiguity. "We ship regularly" and "we will ship this consignment" are different commitments, and Russian marks the difference in the verb itself.

5. Talking About Daily Routines

Daily routine vocabulary is the fastest route to operational fluency. Time expressions such as (every day), (in the morning), (after lunch), and (in the evening) combine naturally with present-tense verbs. A Russian-speaking counterpart asking about your schedule will use these forms, and you can respond with the same patterns.

For example, a Kenyan flower exporter might say: . . . . "In the morning we harvest roses. Then we check quality. After lunch we send the cargo to the airport. In the evening we speak with the buyer."

This routine language is directly transferable to scheduling calls, WhatsApp coordination with Russian logistics partners, and daily reporting to buyers.
