1. Introduction and Why It Matters

In broiler farming, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to simple arithmetic. If you sell 50 birds a day, you need to know exactly how many birds to stock, how much feed to buy, and when to start the next batch. Without formulas, you are guessing. Guessing leads to empty pens on some days and overcrowded pens on others. Both cost you money.

Consider this: A broiler eats about 3.5 kg of feed to reach 2 kg live weight. If you plan to sell 50 birds per day, and each bird takes 6 weeks to reach market weight, you need to calculate your weekly and daily requirements. For example, if you sell 50 birds daily, that is 350 birds per week. Over 6 weeks, you need 2,100 birds in the pipeline. But you cannot put all 2,100 birds in one batch because they would all be ready at the same time. You need batches.

The core formulas help you manage batch production. You will learn to calculate the number of batches, the stocking density, feed conversion ratio (FCR), and the number of chicks to order each week. These formulas are not complicated. They use basic multiplication and division. But they must be applied consistently.

Why does this matter in Africa? Because feed is the biggest cost, often 70% of total expenses. If you overstock, you waste feed and money. If you understock, you miss sales. Also, many African markets have fluctuating prices for day-old chicks and feed. By using formulas, you can adjust quickly. For instance, if feed price rises, you can recalculate your selling price or reduce batch size.

This module will give you the tools to run a smooth daily sales operation. You will learn to use a simple calculator, a notebook, and a weighing scale. No computer needed. By the end, you will be able to plan your batches so that you have a steady supply of birds every day, without overstocking or running out. That is the key to consistent income.

2. Tools and Materials

To apply the core formulas, you need a few basic tools. You do not need expensive equipment. Most of these you can find in your local market or agro-vet shop. First, a simple calculator. A basic solar-powered calculator costs about 2,000 to 5,000 shillings in East Africa, or 1,000 to 3,000 naira in Nigeria. It must have a division and multiplication function. Your phone calculator can also work, but a separate calculator is better because it will not run out of battery.

Second, a notebook and pen. You must record every calculation. Write down the date, number of birds, feed consumed, and deaths. This record helps you track your FCR and adjust formulas. Use a hardcover notebook so it does not get damaged. Third, a weighing scale. You need to weigh feed and birds. A hanging scale that can weigh up to 50 kg is ideal. It costs around 15,000 to 30,000 shillings. You can also use a bathroom scale for small batches, but a hanging scale is more accurate for feed bags.

Fourth, a feed scoop or container of known volume. For example, a 2-litre plastic scoop. If you know that 1 scoop of feed weighs 1.5 kg, you can measure feed without weighing every time. Fifth, a calendar or wall chart. You need to mark your batch schedule: when chicks arrive, when they move to different pens, and when they will be sold. A simple wall calendar works.

Sixth, a record book for sales. This is separate from the production notebook. Record daily sales: number of birds sold, weight, price, and customer. This helps you calculate average daily sales and adjust production. Finally, a marker and masking tape to label feed bags and pens. In Africa, heat and humidity can spoil feed quickly, so label dates clearly. Also, keep a small first aid kit for yourself and your workers, but that is for safety, not formulas.

All these tools are affordable and available in most African towns. If you cannot afford a weighing scale immediately, borrow one from a fellow farmer or use a known-weight object to calibrate a homemade scale. The key is to start. Even a pencil and paper can get you far. But the more accurate your tools, the better your formulas will work.

3. Step by Step Method

Let us go through the step-by-step method to calculate your batch production. We will use a real example: You want to sell 50 broilers every day. You plan to sell birds at 2 kg live weight. Your broilers reach 2 kg in 6 weeks (42 days). Your expected mortality is 5%. You want to know how many chicks to order each week, how many batches to run, and how much feed to buy.

Step 1: Calculate weekly sales. 50 birds/day 7 days = 350 birds per week. Step 2: Calculate total birds needed per batch cycle. Since you sell over 6 weeks, you need 6 weeks 350 birds = 2,100 birds to sell over the cycle. But you also need to account for mortality. Add 5%: 2,100 1.05 = 2,205 birds. So you need to start with 2,205 chicks per cycle.

Step 3: Determine number of batches. You cannot raise all 2,205 birds at once because they would all be ready at week 6. You need to split into weekly batches. So you have 6 batches, each started one week apart. Each batch will have 2,205 6 = 367.5, round to 368 chicks. So order 368 chicks every week.

Step 4: Calculate feed needs. A broiler eats about 3.5 kg feed to reach 2 kg. Total feed for one batch of 368 birds: 368 3.5 = 1,288 kg. Over 6 weeks, you have 6 batches at different stages. Total feed per week varies. But you can calculate average weekly feed: total feed for all 2,205 birds = 2,205 3.5 = 7,717.5 kg. Over 6 weeks, that is about 1,286 kg per week. So buy about 1,300 kg of feed per week. Adjust as birds grow.

Step 5: Calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR = total feed consumed total weight gained. If a bird eats 3.5 kg and gains 2 kg, FCR = 3.5 2 = 1.75. This is good. If FCR is above 2.0, you are wasting feed. Check for feed spillage or disease.

Step 6: Plan housing. Each bird needs about 0.1 square meters at market age. For 368 birds, you need 36.8 square meters per batch. With 6 batches, you need 6 pens of that size, or a rotating system. In Africa, use deep litter system with wood shavings. Ensure good ventilation.

Step 7: Set selling price. If feed costs 50 shillings per kg, feed cost per bird = 3.5 50 = 175 shillings. Add chick cost (say 100 shillings), medication (20 shillings), labour (30 shillings), and other costs (25 shillings). Total cost = 350 shillings per bird. To make 20% profit, sell at 420 shillings per bird. Adjust based on market price.

Follow these steps every cycle. Keep records and adjust numbers as you gain experience.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even with formulas, farmers make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not accounting for mortality. Many farmers calculate chicks needed based only on sales, forgetting that some chicks will die. If you need 2,100 birds and mortality is 5%, you must start with 2,205 chicks. Fix: Always add 5-10% to your chick order. In Africa, mortality can be higher due to heat stress or disease. Start with 10% if you are new.

Mistake 2: Overstocking. Putting too many birds in one pen leads to stress, disease, and poor growth. For example, if you put 500 birds in a pen meant for 368, they will not reach 2 kg in 6 weeks. Fix: Follow the stocking density formula: 0.1 square meters per bird at market age. For 368 birds, provide at least 36.8 square meters. If space is limited, reduce batch size.

Mistake 3: Inaccurate feed measurement. Using a scoop without knowing its weight leads to under- or over-feeding. Fix: Weigh one scoop of feed on a scale. If 1 scoop = 1.5 kg, then 10 scoops = 15 kg. Write this on the feed bag. Also, weigh feed bags when you buy them to ensure you are not cheated.

Mistake 4: Ignoring FCR. If your FCR is above 2.0, you are losing money. Common causes: feed spillage, poor quality feed, or disease. Fix: Check feeders for holes, use high-quality feed, and vaccinate on schedule. Also, weigh birds weekly to track growth.

Mistake 5: Not adjusting for seasonal changes. In Africa, the hot dry season reduces feed intake, so birds grow slower. In the rainy season, wet litter causes disease. Fix: Adjust your batch schedule. In hot months, start fewer chicks or provide cooling. In wet months, ensure proper drainage and use dry litter.

Mistake 6: Selling all birds at once. If you sell all 2,100 birds in one week, you will have no birds for the next 5 weeks. Fix: Stick to weekly batches. Sell only the batch that is ready. This ensures daily sales.

Mistake 7: Poor record keeping. Without records, you cannot calculate FCR or profit. Fix: Use a notebook. Record daily feed, deaths, and sales. Review weekly.

By avoiding these mistakes, you will run a profitable operation. Remember, formulas are only as good as the data you put in. Keep accurate records and adjust as needed.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in broiler production is not just about avoiding injury. It is also about biosecurity, feed safety, and financial safety. First, personal safety. When handling feed, wear a dust mask and gloves. Feed dust can cause respiratory problems. When using chemicals for disinfection, wear protective clothing and keep them away from children. In Africa, many farmers use formalin for fumigation. Formalin is dangerous. Use it only in a well-ventilated area, wear a mask, and never mix it with other chemicals.

Second, biosecurity. Disease can wipe out your entire batch. Follow these practices: Keep visitors away from your pens. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Change clothes and boots before entering. Vaccinate chicks against Newcastle disease, Gumboro, and fowl pox. In Africa, Newcastle is common. Buy vaccines from reputable agro-vet shops and follow the cold chain. If you have multiple batches, handle the youngest birds first, then older ones. This prevents spreading disease from older to younger birds.

Third, feed safety. Store feed in a dry, cool place. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. In humid areas, feed can grow mould. Mould produces aflatoxin, which is poisonous. Never feed mouldy feed. Buy feed from trusted suppliers and use within 4 weeks. If you mix your own feed, use aflatoxin binder. Also, protect feed from rodents and birds.

Fourth, financial safety. Keep separate records for production and sales. Do not mix business money with personal money. Use a simple cash book. If you sell 50 birds a day, record each sale. At the end of the week, calculate your profit. If you are making a loss, adjust your formulas. Also, have a contingency fund for disease outbreaks or feed price hikes. In Africa, prices can change quickly. A fund of at least 10% of your monthly costs is wise.

Fifth, best practice for daily sales. Sell birds at a consistent time each day. This builds customer trust. Weigh birds in front of the customer if possible. Use a clean scale. Provide a receipt or record the sale. Keep the pen clean and remove dead birds immediately. Dead birds attract disease and predators. Bury or burn them far from the pen.

Finally, take care of yourself. Farming is hard work. Rest, drink clean water, and eat well. A healthy farmer makes better decisions. By following these safety and best practices, you protect your birds, your money, and your health.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to practice. This exercise will help you apply the formulas to your own situation. Grab your calculator, notebook, and pen. We will work through a scenario. You want to sell 30 broilers per day. Your birds reach 2 kg in 7 weeks (49 days). Mortality is 8%. Feed conversion ratio is 1.8. Feed costs 45 shillings per kg. Chicks cost 120 shillings each. Other costs (medication, labour, etc.) are 50 shillings per bird. You want a 25% profit margin.

Step 1: Calculate weekly sales. 30 birds/day 7 = 210 birds per week. Step 2: Total birds needed for 7 weeks: 210 7 = 1,470 birds. Add 8% mortality: 1,470 1.08 = 1,587.6, round to 1,588 chicks. Step 3: Number of batches: 7 batches (one per week). Chicks per batch: 1,588 7 = 226.8, round to 227 chicks per week.

Step 4: Feed per bird: FCR = 1.8, so feed consumed = 1.8 2 kg = 3.6 kg per bird. Total feed for all birds: 1,588 3.6 = 5,716.8 kg. Over 7 weeks, average weekly feed = 5,716.8 7 = 816.7 kg. So buy about 817 kg per week.

Step 5: Cost per bird. Feed cost: 3.6 kg 45 = 162 shillings. Chick cost: 120 shillings. Other costs: 50 shillings. Total cost = 162 + 120 + 50 = 332 shillings. Step 6: Selling price for 25% profit: 332 1.25 = 415 shillings per bird.

Step 7: Housing. Each bird needs 0.1 sq m. For 227 birds, need 22.7 sq m per batch. With 7 batches, you need 7 pens of that size, or a rotating system.

Now, do this for your own farm. Write down your target daily sales, growth period, mortality rate, FCR, and costs. Calculate the number of chicks to order each week, feed needed, and selling price. Check your math. Ask a fellow farmer to verify. Then, implement it for one cycle. Keep records. After the cycle, compare your actual results with your calculations. Adjust your formulas for the next cycle. This practice will make you a better planner and a more profitable farmer.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Daily Broiler Sales The Core Formulas

This practical reference consolidates the formulas, tables, and checks used across the module Daily Broiler Sales: Batch Production System. It is written for small and medium broiler enterprises in Africa that sell birds daily while running a batch production cycle. The aim is simple: know your numbers before you sell, so every sale protects your margin and your next batch.

In a daily sales model, you are not selling one flock at the end of a cycle. You are selling birds every day while new chicks are growing. That means your cost base is mixed: some birds are finishing, some are halfway, and some are just starting. Without core formulas, it is easy to sell at a price that looks profitable but actually eats into the capital needed for feed, vaccines, and the next batch.

Core Formulas You Must Know

The following formulas are the backbone of daily broiler sales. They are simple enough to calculate on a phone or in a notebook, but they must be applied consistently.

1. Cost per bird (CPB)
CPB = (Chick cost + Feed cost + Vaccine/medication cost + Labour + Utilities + Mortality loss) Number of birds placed

2. Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR)
FCR = Total feed consumed (kg) Total live weight gained (kg)

3. Daily sales price floor (PF)
PF = (Cost per bird + Daily overhead per bird + Target margin per bird) Average market weight (kg)

4. Replacement reserve (RR)
RR = Daily sales revenue 0.15 to 0.25 (set aside for next batch)

5. Mortality-adjusted cost
Adjusted CPB = CPB (1 Mortality rate)

These formulas work together. If your FCR rises, your cost per bird rises, and your price floor must rise or your margin disappears. If you do not set aside a replacement reserve, you will struggle to buy the next batch of chicks even if sales are strong.

SAFETY WARNING Do Not Sell Below Your Price Floor

Selling below your calculated price floor to move stock fast is one of the most common causes of broiler business failure in Africa. It destroys your replacement reserve, forces you to buy cheaper feed, and increases mortality. If market prices are below your floor, reduce batch size, improve FCR, or negotiate bulk feed prices do not sell at a loss. Always calculate your price floor before the first sale of the day.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Calculate cost per bird before selling. Include chick, feed, vaccines, labour, utilities, and mortality loss.
  • Know your FCR. Weigh feed and birds weekly. FCR above 2.2 needs urgent review.
  • Set your daily price floor. Never sell below it, even if buyers push for discounts.
  • Set aside 1525% of daily sales for the next batch. Treat this as a non-negotiable cost, not profit.
  • Track mortality daily. Remove dead birds immediately and record the number.
  • Weigh a sample of birds weekly. Use a hanging scale or digital kitchen scale.
  • Record every sale. Note date, number of birds, weight, price per kg, and buyer.
  • Review your numbers weekly. Adjust feed, price, or batch size based on actual data.
