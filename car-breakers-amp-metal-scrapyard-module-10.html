1. Identifying your target customers: mechanics, DIYers, and scrap dealers

Your scrapyard has three main customer types: mechanics, DIYers, and scrap dealers. Each wants different things. Mechanics buy parts to fix customers' cars quickly. They need reliable parts, fair prices, and fast service. A mechanic might buy 10 alternators a month if you treat him well. DIYers are car owners who fix their own cars. They come on weekends, ask many questions, and want cheap parts. They might buy one part every few months, but they tell friends. Scrap dealers buy metal by weight. They don't care about part quality; they want kilos. They come with trucks and pay cash on the spot.

To find mechanics, visit local garages. Introduce yourself, give them your phone number, and offer a 10% discount on their first purchase. Ask what parts they need most. Keep a notebook listing each mechanic's name, garage, and common requests. For DIYers, put up posters at fuel stations, car washes, and hardware stores. Use simple language: "Used car parts cheap." Include your WhatsApp number. For scrap dealers, contact local construction sites and welding workshops. They often need scrap metal and can connect you to dealers.

Price differently for each group. Mechanics get a trade discount (e.g., 15% off marked price). DIYers pay full price but you can offer a small freebie like a bolt or nut. Scrap dealers pay per kilogram: in many African countries, scrap steel sells for $0.20-$0.40 per kg, aluminium for $0.80-$1.20 per kg. Weigh accurately and pay promptly to build trust.

Track your customers in a simple exercise book. Write date, name, type, part sold, and price. After one month, see which group brings the most money. Focus your marketing on that group. For example, if mechanics bring 70% of income, visit five new garages each week. If DIYers are strong, post more on Facebook Marketplace. Always ask for a phone number and send a thank-you message after a sale. That personal touch turns a one-time buyer into a regular.

2. Creating simple signage and business cards for your scrapyard

Good signage brings walk-in customers. Your sign must be visible from the road. Use a large board (at least 1 metre by 0.5 metre) painted white with bold black or red letters. Write your business name, what you sell (e.g., "USED CAR PARTS"), and your phone number. Add a simple arrow pointing to your entrance. If you can afford it, paint your phone number on the roof of your yard or on a wall facing the road. In many African cities, hand-painted signs by local artists cost $10-$30. That is a one-time cost that works 24 hours a day.

For business cards, keep it simple. You can print 100 cards for about $5 at a local print shop. Include: your name, business name, phone number (WhatsApp enabled), and a short list of what you sell: "Engines, gearboxes, alternators, batteries, scrap metal." Add your location: "Behind Total Petrol Station, Kibera Road." If you have no fixed address, write "Call for directions." Do not use fancy fonts; use clear, large text. Laminate a few cards to keep them clean in your pocket.

Distribute cards to mechanics, taxi drivers, and at auto parts shops. When a customer buys a part, staple your card to the receipt or hand it to them. Ask them to save your number on WhatsApp. You can also make a simple banner from tarpaulin for $15-$25. Hang it at the entrance and at a nearby market. Change the banner every 6 months if it fades.

If you cannot read or write well, ask a family member or a local sign writer to help. Many sign writers in African towns can design and paint a sign for you. Show them a sample from another business. Always include a mobile number because that is how customers will contact you. A sign without a phone number is useless. Finally, keep your sign clean and repair it if it tears. A dirty, broken sign tells customers you do not care about quality.

3. Using WhatsApp and Facebook Marketplace to sell parts

WhatsApp is the most powerful sales tool for a scrapyard in Africa. Most customers have it. Start by saving all your customers' numbers in your phone with their name and what they bought. Create a WhatsApp Business account (free) to set up a catalogue. Take clear photos of parts with good light. Use a plain background like a white cloth or a clean wall. For each part, write a short description: "Toyota Corolla 2005 alternator, tested, $30." Include your location and phone number. Post these photos to your WhatsApp Status every morning. Status updates disappear after 24 hours, so post daily.

Join local WhatsApp groups for mechanics, taxi drivers, and car owners. Do not spam. Instead, offer value: "I have a Nissan NP200 gearbox for $150. Call me if you need one." Respond quickly to messages. If someone asks for a part you don't have, say "I will check and get back to you." Then actually check and reply within 2 hours. Quick replies build trust.

Facebook Marketplace is free and reaches many people. Create a Facebook account using your real name. Go to Marketplace, click "Sell," and list each part as a separate item. Use the same clear photos. Set the price slightly higher than your final price so you can negotiate. In the description, write: "Used car parts. Cash only. Pick up at my yard. Call or WhatsApp 07XX XXX XXX." Share your listings to local buy-and-sell groups. In Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Ghana, these groups have thousands of members.

Be safe. Meet buyers at your yard during daylight. Do not accept mobile money payments before the buyer sees the part. If you must deliver, ask for a deposit via M-Pesa or similar. After a sale, ask the buyer to leave a positive review on your Facebook page. Positive reviews make other buyers trust you. Finally, keep your phone charged and have a small power bank. You do not want to miss a sale because your phone died.

4. Building trust with repeat customers through fair pricing

Repeat customers are the backbone of a scrapyard. To keep them, you must be fair and consistent. Fair pricing means you charge the same price for the same part to similar customers. Do not charge a mechanic $50 for an alternator today and $70 tomorrow. Write down your prices in a notebook. For common parts, set a fixed price: e.g., alternator $30, starter $35, gearbox $150, battery $20. For rare parts, check online or ask other yards, then add 20% profit.

Offer a small discount for repeat buyers. For example, after a customer's third purchase, give 10% off. Tell them: "You are a good customer, so I give you a discount." This makes them feel valued. Also, offer a guarantee. If a part fails within 7 days, allow the customer to return it for a replacement or refund. Write this on your receipt. Many scrapyards cheat customers, so your honesty will stand out. A mechanic who trusts you will bring all his business to you.

Use transparent weighing for scrap metal. Let the customer see the scale. If you use a mechanical scale, zero it before weighing. If you use a digital scale, show the reading. Pay immediately in cash or mobile money. Do not delay payment. If you promise to pay later, you break trust. For parts, show the customer the part working if possible. For example, test an alternator with a multimeter or spin a starter. If you cannot test it, say so and lower the price.

Keep a customer record book. Write the date, customer name, part sold, price, and any complaint. Before selling to a repeat customer, check the book. If they had a problem last time, ask about it and make it right. Send a WhatsApp message after a big sale: "Thank you for your business. Tell your friends." Small gestures build loyalty. Finally, never argue over small amounts. If a customer says they were short by $2, let it go. That $2 buys you a customer for life.

5. Handling customer complaints and returns

Complaints are normal. How you handle them decides if the customer returns. When a customer complains, listen first. Do not interrupt. Let them finish. Then say: "I hear you. Let me check." Ask for the receipt or WhatsApp message as proof of purchase. If they have no proof, still try to help if you remember the sale. Never call the customer a liar. That ends the relationship.

If a part is faulty, offer a replacement or refund. For example, if a starter you sold for $35 does not work, test it yourself. If it fails, give another starter or return the money. If you have no replacement, refund in cash or mobile money. Do not offer credit unless the customer agrees. For scrap metal, if a customer says the weight was wrong, re-weigh in front of them. If your scale was wrong, pay the difference immediately and apologise.

Set clear return rules and write them on your receipt: "Returns within 7 days with receipt. Part must be in same condition." This protects you from abuse. If a customer returns a part they damaged, politely explain that you cannot accept it. Offer a small discount on a new part instead. Stay calm. If they become aggressive, ask them to leave and come back when calm. Do not fight. Your safety is first.

After resolving a complaint, write it in your complaint book. Note the date, customer, problem, and solution. Look for patterns. If many customers complain about the same part, stop buying that type. If a supplier sells you bad parts, find a new supplier. Finally, follow up with the customer after a few days. Send a WhatsApp message: "Hello, is the replacement working well?" This shows you care. A customer whose complaint was solved well often becomes your most loyal customer. They will tell others: "That scrapyard is honest."

6. Networking with local garages and auto parts shops

Networking means building relationships with other businesses. Local garages and auto parts shops can send you customers and buy from you. Start by visiting every garage within 5 kilometres of your yard. Introduce yourself to the owner or head mechanic. Bring a small gift: a cold soda or a packet of biscuits. Say: "I run a scrapyard nearby. I have used parts. If you need something, call me. I give you good price." Leave your business card or write your number on their wall.

Offer a referral fee. If a garage sends a customer who buys a part, give the garage 10% of the sale. For example, if you sell a gearbox for $150, give the garage $15. Tell them: "When you send me a customer, I pay you." This motivates them to recommend you. Keep a record of referrals in a notebook. Pay promptly, even if it is a small amount. Word spreads fast among mechanics.

Partner with auto parts shops that sell new parts. They often have customers who want cheaper used parts. Ask the shop owner: "If a customer cannot afford new, send them to me. I will send my customers to you for new parts." This is a two-way relationship. Also, work with scrap metal dealers. If you have too much scrap, sell to them. If they need a specific part, they can buy from you. Attend local auto shows or market days where mechanics gather. Hand out flyers and talk to people.

Join or form a small association of scrapyard owners in your area. Meet once a month to share prices and avoid undercutting each other. Together you can buy in bulk or negotiate with transporters. Finally, keep your promises. If you tell a garage you will have a part by Tuesday, have it by Tuesday. If you cannot, call them before they call you. Reliability makes you the go-to scrapyard in your area. After six months, you will have a network that brings you steady business without spending money on advertising.

<h2>Practical Reference: Marketing &amp; Customer Relations for Car Breakers &amp; Metal Scrapyards</h2>

<p>In the African auto-recycling trade, your yard is only as profitable as the customers who walk through the gate or message your phone. A scrapyard that sells a used alternator for KES 3,500 to a walk-in mechanic, then posts the next one on Facebook Marketplace for KES 4,200 to a DIYer in another town, is running a smarter business than one that simply waits for buyers. This module gives you the practical tools: identifying who your real customers are, low-cost marketing that works on a phone, fair pricing that brings people back, and the networking habits that turn a scrapyard into a reliable parts hub.</p>

<h3>1. Identifying Your Target Customers</h3>

<p>Your customers fall into three main groups, and each buys differently. <strong>Mechanics</strong> (fundis) buy frequently, need parts fast, and care about fit and function more than appearance. They will pay a fair price for a guaranteed working part and will return weekly if you treat them well. <strong>DIYers</strong> (home mechanics, boda-boda owners, taxi drivers) buy occasionally, often need advice, and are price-sensitive but willing to pay for convenience. <strong>Scrap dealers and metal buyers</strong> buy in bulk by weight copper, aluminium, batteries, catalytic converters and are driven almost entirely by price per kilogram and speed of transaction.</p>

<h3>2. Simple Signage and Business Cards</h3>

<p>Many scrapyards lose customers simply because nobody knows they exist. You do not need a billboard. You need three things: a visible roadside sign, a business card or flyer, and a phone number that is always answered.</p>

<p><strong>Signage:</strong> Use a painted metal or wooden board at the gate. Include: business name, what you sell ("Used Auto Parts &amp; Scrap Metal"), phone number, and opening hours. Paint it in high-contrast colours (white on blue, yellow on black). If you are near a main road, add a directional arrow. In many African cities, a simple hand-painted sign with a WhatsApp number is more effective than a printed banner because it feels local and trustworthy.</p>

<p><strong>Business cards:</strong> Print 200 cards for a few dollars at any local print shop. Include your name, yard name, phone/WhatsApp number, and a one-line list of what you stock. Give cards to every mechanic, tow-truck driver, and garage you meet. A card that says "Engines Gearboxes Batteries Scrap Metal" tells the customer instantly whether to call you.</p>

<h3>3. Using WhatsApp and Facebook Marketplace to Sell Parts</h3>

<p>In Africa, WhatsApp is the single most powerful sales tool for a scrapyard. Here is a practical system:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>WhatsApp Status:</strong> Post 35 photos daily of newly arrived parts. Include the car model, year, and price. Example: "Toyota Vitz 2008 alternator KES 2,800, tested."</li>
<li><strong>WhatsApp Business:</strong> Use the free WhatsApp Business app to set up a catalogue, auto-replies, and a business profile with your location.</li>
<li><strong>Facebook Marketplace:</strong> List individual parts with clear photos, price, and your location. Facebook Marketplace reaches buyers outside your immediate area. Use local buy-and-sell groups too.</li>
<li><strong>Broadcast lists:</strong> Build a list of mechanics and send a weekly "new arrivals" message. Do not spam once a week is enough.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Building Trust Through Fair Pricing</h3>

<p>Trust is your most valuable asset. A mechanic who trusts you will not negotiate for 30 minutes he will pay your price and come back next week. Fair pricing means three things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Consistent pricing:</strong> Do not charge one price today and a higher price tomorrow for the same part. Keep a price list on the wall.</li>
<li><strong>Honest grading:</strong> If a part is untested, say so. If it is refurbished, say so. A "tested working" label means you stand behind it.</li>
<li><strong>Fair scrap rates:</strong> Weigh accurately and show the customer the scale reading. Word spreads fast if you cheat on weight.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border: 2px solid #c62828; background: #fdecea; padding: 12px; margin: 16px 0; border-radius: 6px;">
<strong style="color: #c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never sell or install used brake pads, brake discs, airbags, seatbelts, or fuel-system components unless they are certified and tested to manufacturer standards. These are safety-critical parts. Selling faulty safety parts can cause fatal accidents and exposes you to legal liability. Always label such parts clearly as "NOT FOR RESALE SCRAP ONLY" if they are worn or damaged.
</div>

<h3>5. Handling Customer Complaints and Returns</h3>

<p>Complaints are not a threat they are a chance to prove you are trustworthy. Follow this simple process:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Listen without arguing.</strong> Let the customer explain the problem fully.</li>
<li><strong>Verify the part.</strong> Check your records (date of sale, part number, customer name).</li>
<li><strong>Offer a fair solution.</strong> If the part failed within your guarantee period (714 days is standard), offer a replacement or refund. If it was sold "as-is," explain politely but consider a partial credit to keep the relationship.</li>
<li><strong>Record the complaint.</strong> Keep a notebook or phone note of every complaint. Patterns will show you which parts to stop buying.</li>
<li><strong>Follow up.</strong> A WhatsApp message the next day asking "Is the replacement working?" turns a complaint into loyalty.</li>
</ol>

<h3>6. Networking with Local Garages and Auto Parts Shops</h3>

<p>Your best customers are other businesses. Build a network by:</p>
<ul>
<li>Visiting 5 garages per week with your business card and a list of what you stock.</li>
<li>Offering a small commission or discount to mechanics who refer customers to you.</li>
<li>Partnering with tow-truck drivers they bring you scrap vehicles and buy parts.</li>
<li>Attending local mechanic association meetings or informal fundi gatherings.</li>
<li>Keeping a "wanted list" if a garage needs a rare part, you call them when it arrives.</li>
</ul>
