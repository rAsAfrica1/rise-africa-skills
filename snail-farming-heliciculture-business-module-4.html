1. Nutritional Requirements of Snails

Snails need a diet rich in protein, calcium, carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals to grow quickly and reproduce. Protein is critical for meat development; aim for 16â22% crude protein in their feed. Young snails (growers) need more protein (20â22%) than adults (16â18%). Calcium is the most important mineralâsnails use it to build and repair their shells. A lack of calcium leads to thin, cracked shells and slow growth. Carbohydrates from fruits and vegetables provide energy. Vitamins like A, D, and E support shell formation and immune health. Fiber aids digestion.

In Africa, you can meet these needs using locally available feed: pawpaw leaves, sweet potato leaves, cabbage, carrot, cucumber, and fruits like mango, banana, and watermelon. These provide protein, vitamins, and some calcium. However, fresh vegetables alone may not supply enough calcium. Supplement with ground oyster shell, limestone powder, or crushed eggshells. A simple ratio: for every 10 kg of fresh feed, add 1â2 kg of calcium powder. Also provide a protein boost with soybean meal, groundnut cake, or fishmeal (5â10% of total feed).

Water is often overlooked but essential. Snails need a moist environment to produce mucus and digest food. Keep the soil substrate dampâsqueeze a handful; it should hold together but not drip. Mist the pen daily with clean water. Without enough moisture, snails become inactive and stop eating.

A balanced diet prevents stunted growth and high mortality. Observe your snails: healthy snails have smooth, shiny shells and are active at night. If shells are dull or broken, increase calcium. If growth is slow, check protein levels. Adjust feed based on their stage and condition. Always provide feed in shallow trays or on clean flat stones to avoid contamination. Remove uneaten feed after 24 hours to prevent mould. With proper nutrition, your snails will reach market size in 8â12 months, yielding better profits.

2. Types of Feed: Fresh Vegetables, Fruits, and Commercial Diets

Snails are herbivores and thrive on a variety of fresh plant materials. The best feeds are those that are locally available, cheap, and nutritious. Fresh vegetables: cabbage, lettuce, spinach, kale, sweet potato leaves, pawpaw leaves, cocoyam leaves, and cucumber. These are rich in vitamins and water. Fruits: pawpaw, mango, banana, watermelon, pineapple, and tomato. Fruits are high in sugar and water, which snails love, but they should not be the only feedâtoo much sugar can cause health problems. Always wash vegetables and fruits thoroughly to remove pesticides and dirt. Chop them into small pieces to make eating easier.

Commercial diets: In many African countries, you can buy formulated snail feed from agricultural supply stores. These are usually pellets or mash with balanced protein (18â22%) and added calcium. They are convenient but more expensive. If you cannot afford commercial feed, you can make your own mix: 40% ground maize, 20% soybean meal, 20% wheat bran, 10% fishmeal, and 10% ground oyster shell. Mix well and offer as a moist mash. This homemade diet can cost less than half the price of commercial feed.

A good feeding strategy combines fresh and dry feeds. Offer fresh vegetables and fruits in the evening (snails are nocturnal) and a small amount of dry mash or pellets in the morning. This ensures they get both moisture and concentrated nutrients. A common mistake is feeding only one typeâe.g., only cabbageâwhich leads to nutrient deficiencies. Rotate feeds daily. For example: Monday: pawpaw leaves + cucumber; Tuesday: cabbage + banana; Wednesday: commercial pellets + watermelon rind; and so on. This variety mimics their natural diet and keeps them healthy.

Store feed properly: keep fresh produce in a cool, shaded place and use within 2 days. Dry feeds should be kept in airtight containers to avoid moisture and pests. Never feed mouldy or rotten foodâit can kill your snails. Also avoid salty or spicy kitchen waste. With careful selection, you can feed your snails well using mostly farm or market waste, reducing costs significantly.

3. Creating a Balanced Feeding Schedule

A feeding schedule ensures your snails get the right nutrients at the right time without waste. Snails are nocturnal, so the main feeding should be in the evening (around 5â7 PM). This is when they are most active. A second light feeding can be given in the early morning if you notice they are still hungry. Feed only what they can consume in 12â24 hours. Overfeeding leads to uneaten food that attracts pests, mould, and bacteria. Underfeeding causes slow growth and cannibalism.

A simple weekly schedule for a medium-sized pen (e.g., 1m x 1m with 100 growers):
- Monday: 500g chopped pawpaw leaves + 200g cucumber slices + 50g calcium powder.
- Tuesday: 500g cabbage leaves + 200g banana pieces + 50g commercial pellets.
- Wednesday: 500g sweet potato leaves + 200g watermelon rind + 50g soybean meal.
- Thursday: 500g lettuce + 200g mango slices + 50g calcium powder.
- Friday: 500g cocoyam leaves + 200g tomato + 50g fishmeal.
- Saturday: 500g spinach + 200g pawpaw fruit + 50g ground oyster shell.
- Sunday: 500g mixed vegetables + 200g fruit scraps + 50g homemade mash.

Adjust quantities based on snail size and number. Hatchlings (0â2 months) eat very littleâabout 1â2g of feed per snail per day. Growers (2â6 months) need 5â10g. Adults (6+ months) need 10â20g. Always provide a shallow dish of clean water for drinking, but change it daily to prevent drowning of small snails.

Monitor consumption: if feed remains after 24 hours, reduce the amount. If snails are actively searching for food, increase slightly. Keep a notebook to record feed types, amounts, and snail response. This helps you fine-tune. Also, ensure the pen is dark and moist during feeding times. Avoid disturbing them. A consistent schedule reduces stress and promotes steady growth. Remember, a well-fed snail is a healthy snail that will reproduce and fetch good market prices.

4. Supplementing Calcium for Shell Health

Calcium is the single most important nutrient for snails. Their shells are made of calcium carbonate, and a deficiency causes thin, brittle shells, slow growth, and high mortality. In the wild, snails get calcium from soil, bones, and shells of other animals. In captivity, you must provide it directly. A mature snail needs about 1â2% of its body weight in calcium daily. For a 100g snail, that's 1â2g of calcium. But since they also get some from feed, supplement with an additional 0.5â1g per snail per day.

Common calcium sources in Africa: ground oyster shell (available at feed stores or poultry suppliers), limestone powder (from agricultural lime), crushed eggshells (dry them, then grind to powder), and wood ash (from clean, untreated wood). You can also use cuttlebone (from pet shops) but it's expensive. The cheapest is eggshellsâcollect from your kitchen, sun-dry for 2 days, then crush with a mortar and pestle or grind in a blender. Oyster shell is also cheap if bought in bulk (e.g., 50kg bag for a few dollars).

How to supplement: Mix calcium powder into the feed at 5â10% of the total feed weight. For example, if you feed 1kg of vegetables, add 50â100g of calcium powder. Alternatively, offer calcium in a separate shallow dishâsnails will eat it as needed. This is called 'free-choice' feeding and prevents over-supplementation. Place the dish near their hiding spots. Replace weekly. You can also dust chopped vegetables with calcium powder before feeding.

Signs of calcium deficiency: soft or cracked shells, holes in shells, snails that hide all day, and slow growth. If you see these, increase calcium immediately. Also, ensure the soil substrate has some calciumâmix in crushed limestone at 1 cup per square meter. Avoid using sand or acidic soil. A simple test: if your soil is red or clayey, it may lack calcium. Add powdered limestone. With proper calcium, snails develop strong, shiny shells and reach market size faster. Healthy shells also mean fewer injuries and less stress during handling.

5. Hydration and Moisture Management

Snails are 70â80% water and need a moist environment to survive. Without adequate moisture, they produce less mucus, move slowly, and can die within days. Hydration comes from two sources: the moisture in their feed and the humidity of their pen. In Africa, where temperatures can be high, maintaining moisture is critical. A relative humidity of 70â90% is ideal. You can measure this with a cheap hygrometer (available at hardware stores). If humidity drops below 60%, snails become inactive and may seal themselves in their shells (aestivation).

To maintain moisture: mist the pen daily with clean water using a hand sprayer. The best time is early morning and late evening. Avoid spraying at midday when it's hotâwater evaporates quickly and can cause temperature shock. The soil substrate should be damp but not waterlogged. Test by squeezing a handful: it should form a ball that holds together without dripping. If water drips, it's too wet; if it crumbles, it's too dry. A good substrate mix is 70% loamy soil, 20% peat moss or coco coir, and 10% sand. This holds moisture well.

Provide a shallow water dish (1â2 cm deep) for drinking. Use a clean lid or saucer. Change the water daily to prevent bacterial growth. Small snails can drown, so add a few small stones or pieces of charcoal to the dishâthis gives them a safe place to stand. You can also place fresh, wet vegetables like cucumber or watermelon in the pen; these provide extra water. But don't rely on them alone.

Monitor your snails: if they are active at night, have shiny shells, and are eating well, moisture is good. If they hide deep in the soil or seal themselves, increase misting. In dry seasons, you may need to mist 2â3 times a day. In rainy seasons, reduce to once a day and ensure good drainage to avoid flooding. Cover the pen with a breathable mesh or shade cloth to retain humidity while allowing air circulation. Proper hydration leads to faster growth, better reproduction, and healthier snails. It's a simple, low-cost practice that makes a huge difference.

6. Common Feeding Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Many snail farmers fail because of simple feeding mistakes. Here are the most common and how to fix them.

1. Overfeeding: Giving too much food leads to waste, mould, and pests. Uneaten feed rots and produces ammonia, which is toxic to snails. Solution: Feed only what snails can finish in 12â24 hours. Start with small amounts and adjust based on consumption. Remove leftovers every morning.

2. Underfeeding: This causes stunted growth, cannibalism (snails eating each other's shells for calcium), and death. Solution: Weigh your snails monthly. If they are not gaining weight, increase feed by 10â20%. Provide a consistent schedule.

3. Lack of calcium: As covered, this is critical. Many farmers feed only vegetables. Solution: Always add calcium powder (eggshells, oyster shell) at 5â10% of feed. Provide free-choice calcium.

4. Feeding poisonous plants: Some plants are toxic to snails, such as cassava leaves (raw), tomato leaves, potato leaves, and anything sprayed with pesticides. Solution: Stick to safe feeds: pawpaw leaves, sweet potato leaves, cabbage, lettuce, spinach, and fruits. Wash all produce thoroughly.

5. Not providing water: Snails need drinking water and humidity. Solution: Provide a shallow water dish and mist daily. Keep substrate damp.

6. Feeding at the wrong time: Snails are nocturnal. Feeding during the day means food dries out and snails may not eat. Solution: Feed in the evening.

7. Sudden diet changes: Snails are sensitive. Changing feed abruptly can cause digestive stress. Solution: Introduce new feeds gradually over 3â4 days, mixing with old feed.

8. Ignoring hygiene: Dirty feeding trays and spoiled food spread disease. Solution: Use clean trays or flat stones. Wash them weekly with clean water. Remove uneaten food daily.

9. Using salty or spicy kitchen waste: Salt is lethal to snails. Solution: Never feed leftovers with salt, pepper, or oil.

10. Not recording: Without records, you can't tell what works. Solution: Keep a simple notebook: date, feed type, amount, and observations. Review weekly.

By avoiding these mistakes, you'll save money, reduce mortality, and grow healthy snails. Remember, snails are low-maintenance but sensitive to poor management. Consistent, clean, and balanced feeding is the key to success.

Practical Reference: Snail Nutrition and Feeding for African Heliciculture Businesses

In snail farming, feed is the single largest recurring cost after stock and housing. For African heliciculture enterprises â whether you are raising Achatina achatina in Ghana, Achatina fulica in Kenya, or Archachatina marginata in Nigeria â the margin between profit and loss is decided at the feeding trough. A well-managed feeding programme can cut feed costs by 30â40%, shorten the grow-out cycle by two to three months, and lift shell quality to export grade. This practical reference condenses the core lessons of this module into actionable guidance for commercial snail farms.

1. Nutritional Requirements of Snails

Snails are monogastric herbivores with a simple digestive tract. Unlike poultry or pigs, they do not require high-energy concentrates. Their diet must supply protein (for meat and egg development), calcium (for shell growth and repair), carbohydrates (for energy), fibre (for gut motility), and trace minerals. Protein requirements peak during the juvenile phase (16â20% crude protein) and drop to 12â14% at grow-out. Calcium is the most critical mineral: a laying adult snail needs 5â8% calcium in its diet, and a deficiency shows up as thin, cracked, or pitted shells within two weeks.

Water is equally important. Snails are 70â80% water and lose moisture rapidly in dry conditions. A farm that gets hydration wrong will see stunted growth, poor shell formation, and high mortality regardless of how good the feed is.

2. Types of Feed: Fresh Vegetables, Fruits, and Commercial Diets

African snail farmers have three feed categories to work with. The cheapest and most available are fresh vegetables and fruits. The most consistent are formulated commercial or on-farm mixed diets. The most practical commercial strategy is a hybrid: use fresh produce as the base and supplement with a formulated mash for protein and calcium.

3. Creating a Balanced Feeding Schedule

Snails are nocturnal and most active between 6 pm and 6 am. Feed them in the late afternoon or early evening so the feed does not dry out or ferment under daytime heat. A commercial farm should feed once daily, with a light top-up in the morning during peak growth or breeding periods. Juveniles need feeding twice daily because their intake per body weight is higher.

Feed quantity should be calculated as a percentage of body weight. A practical rule: 5â8% of body weight per day for juveniles, 3â5% for adults. Overfeeding wastes money and creates mould; underfeeding slows growth and triggers shell cannibalism (snails will rasp each other's shells for calcium if deficient).

4. Supplementing Calcium for Shell Health

Calcium is the make-or-break nutrient in snail farming. Without it, shells become thin, translucent, and easily broken â a direct rejection factor for export and premium local markets. Provide a continuous supply of calcium in a separate shallow dish or sprinkled over feed. Good African sources include crushed oyster shell, limestone flour, dried and ground eggshell, bone meal, and wood ash (use ash sparingly; excess raises pH).

A practical ratio: for every 1 kg of feed, add 50â80 g of calcium supplement for growing snails, and up to 100 g for breeding adults. Keep the calcium dish dry and refresh weekly. Snails will self-regulate their calcium intake if it is always available.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Pesticide and Herbicide Contamination

Never feed snails vegetables, fruits, or leaves collected from farms or gardens that have been sprayed with pesticides, herbicides, or fungicides within the past 21 days. Snails absorb chemicals directly through their skin and gut, and contaminated feed can wipe out an entire batch within 48 hours. Symptoms include excessive slime production, withdrawal into the shell, and rapid death. If you buy produce from markets, wash it thoroughly in clean water and soak for 30 minutes before feeding. Where possible, grow your own snail feed on a dedicated plot with no chemical inputs â this also reduces feed cost and gives you traceability for export buyers.

5. Hydration and Moisture Management

Snails do not drink from bowls; they absorb moisture through their skin and from their food. The rearing pen must maintain 70â85% relative humidity. In dry African climates â the Sahel, northern Nigeria, parts of Kenya and Ethiopia â this requires deliberate intervention. Practical methods include misting the pen once or twice daily with clean water, using damp (not soaked) soil or coco peat as substrate, placing shallow water dishes, and covering pens with shade netting or moistened sacks.

Overwatering is as dangerous as drying. Waterlogged substrate breeds bacteria, fungi, and mites. The substrate should be damp to the touch but never dripping. For commercial farms, a simple hygrometer (USD 5â15) is a worthwhile investment; guessing humidity is expensive.

6. Common Feeding Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Most snail farm failures trace back to a handful of avoidable feeding errors. The most common are: feeding spoiled or mouldy produce (causes gut disease), offering only one type of feed (causes nutrient deficiency), leaving feed in the pen for more than 24 hours (attracts ants and pests), forgetting calcium (causes shell damage and cannibalism), using chlorinated tap water for misting (chlorine harms snails â let water stand for 24 hours before use), and feeding during the heat of the day (feed dries out and is ignored).

On the business side, the biggest mistake is failing to track feed cost per kilogram of snail produced. A farm that does not know this number cannot price profitably. Keep a simple feed log: date, feed type, quantity, cost, and observed consumption. Review weekly.
