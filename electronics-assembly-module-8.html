1. Rework Tools and Equipment

To fix electronics, you need the right tools. In many African workshops, a basic rework station costs between $50 and $150. Start with a temperature-controlled soldering iron (adjustable 200450C), a hot air rework station (100500C), and a desoldering pump (manual or electric). You also need fine tweezers, a flux pen, solder wick (12mm), isopropyl alcohol (99%), and a brass sponge for cleaning tips. A magnifying lamp or a smartphone with a macro lens helps for small parts.

For power, ensure your iron matches your local voltage (220240V in most African countries). Use a surge protector; power fluctuations can damage heating elements. If you often work on through-hole boards, a desoldering gun (like the Hakko FR-301) saves time, but a $5 manual pump works for occasional jobs. For surface-mount (SMD) work, hot air is essential. Practice on scrap boards before touching customer devices.

Keep a multimeter (budget $10$20) to check continuity and voltages. A cheap USB microscope ($20$40) lets you inspect solder joints on your phone. Store tips tinned with solder to prevent oxidation. In humid coastal areas, keep silica gel packets in your tool case to avoid rust.

Safety first: work in a ventilated area, wear safety glasses, and never touch the hot air nozzle. Use a fume extractor or a small fan blowing away from you. Solder contains lead in some regions; wash hands after handling. Label your tools and keep them cleandirty tips transfer heat poorly and damage pads.

Finally, a good workbench light and an anti-static mat (or a clean cotton cloth) reduce static damage. If you cannot afford a hot air station, a paint stripper heat gun with a nozzle can work for removing large ICs, but control is harder. Start with quality basics and upgrade as you earn.

2. Desoldering Techniques

Desoldering is removing solder to free a component or clean a pad. The goal is to heat the joint quickly and remove solder without lifting pads or burning the board. For through-hole parts, use a desoldering pump. Heat the joint with your iron (set to 350C for leaded solder, 380C for lead-free) for 12 seconds, then quickly place the pump nozzle over the joint and release. Repeat if needed. Add fresh solder firstit helps heat transfer.

For surface-mount parts, use hot air. Set the temperature to 300350C and airflow to low (23 on a 18 scale). Apply flux to the joint, then move the nozzle in small circles around the component. Once solder melts, lift the part with tweezers. Never force it. For small two-pin parts like resistors, you can use two irons or a specialized tweezers iron.

Solder wick (desoldering braid) is best for cleaning pads. Place the wick over the pad, press with your iron, and the solder will flow into the wick. Add flux to the wick for better results. Use a fresh section of wick each time. For large ground planes, you may need a higher wattage iron (60W+) or preheat the board with a hot air station at 100C.

If a pad is stubborn, add more solder (yes, add) to increase thermal mass, then remove it all at once. In Africa, where humidity can cause oxidation, clean the joint with isopropyl alcohol before desoldering. Avoid overheatingif the pad turns dark or lifts, stop. Practice on old circuit boards from broken TVs or radios.

After desoldering, clean the area with alcohol and a brush. Inspect for leftover solder balls that can cause shorts. A desoldering pump with a silicone tip (like the Engineer SS-02) is more effective than cheap plastic ones. Always tin your iron tip before and after use.

3. Component Removal and Replacement

Removing a component without damaging the board is a skill. For through-hole resistors or capacitors, heat one leg at a time while gently pulling from the other side. Use a small flat screwdriver or tweezers to lift the leg as solder melts. For ICs with many pins, use hot air. Apply flux, heat the whole area evenly, and lift the chip with a vacuum pen or tweezers. Never pry it off.

For SMD two-terminal parts (resistors, capacitors), use two soldering irons or a hot air station. Heat both ends simultaneously and lift with tweezers. For multi-pin ICs (SOIC, QFN), hot air is best. Direct the nozzle around the chip, not on top, to avoid blowing it away. Once solder melts, the chip will floatlift it straight up. Clean pads with wick and alcohol.

Replacement: First, clean the pads. Apply fresh solder to one pad, then place the new component with tweezers. Solder one pin to hold it, then solder the rest. For SMD, use fine solder (0.5mm) and flux. For through-hole, insert the leads, bend them slightly, solder, and trim. Ensure no solder bridges between pins. Use a magnifier to check.

In Africa, components may be scarce. Salvage from dead boards: test with a multimeter before reuse. Store components in labeled bags or film canisters. For replacements, match voltage, capacitance, and tolerance. A 25V capacitor can replace a 16V one, but never lower. For resistors, 1/4W is common; for power, use 1W or higher.

After soldering, verify with a multimeter: check continuity, resistance, and voltage. If the board does not work, recheck orientation (diodes, electrolytic capacitors, ICs). A common mistake is reversed polarity. Take photos before removal to remember orientation.

4. Pad and Track Repair

Pads and tracks can lift or break during rework. A lifted pad means the copper has separated from the board. To repair, first clean the area with alcohol. If the pad is still attached but lifted, you can glue it back with a tiny drop of superglue, then solder a thin wire from the pad to the track. If the pad is gone, you must create a new connection.

For a missing pad, find the track that connected to it. Scrape the green solder mask off the track near the break with a fiberglass pen or a sharp knife. Apply flux and tin the exposed copper. Then solder a thin insulated wire (30 AWG wire-wrap wire) from the track to the component lead. Use tweezers to route the wire neatly. Secure it with UV-curable solder mask or nail polish.

For broken tracks, use a jumper wire. If the track is on the surface, you can bridge the gap with a small piece of copper foil or a wire. For inner-layer breaks, it's often not repairable. In that case, run a wire on the surface. Always keep wires short and flat to avoid shorts.

Tools: fiberglass pen ($3), 30 AWG wire ($5), UV solder mask ($10). In Africa, you can use a toothbrush and acetone to clean. Avoid using too much heatit causes more damage. Practice on scrap boards: deliberately lift a pad and repair it.

After repair, test continuity with a multimeter. Check that the repair is mechanically strongtug gently on the wire. If the board is for a customer, document the repair with a photo. Pad repair is common in phone and laptop repair, where boards are dense. Patience and a steady hand are key.

5. Jumper Wires and Modifications

Jumper wires are used to bypass broken tracks or add modifications. Use 30 AWG wire-wrap wire for signal lines and 24 AWG for power. Cut the wire to length, strip 1mm of insulation, and tin both ends. Solder one end to the source point and the other to the destination. Keep wires as short as possible and route them away from hot components.

For modifications, like adding an LED or changing a resistor value, plan first. Draw a simple schematic. Identify where to cut tracks (if needed) and where to solder. Use a sharp knife to cut a track, then verify with a multimeter that it's open. Add the new component and jumper wires. Secure wires with UV solder mask or hot glue.

In Africa, modifications are common in solar projects, car electronics, and phone repairs. For example, you might bypass a faulty charging IC by running a wire from the USB port to the battery. Always check polarity and voltage. Use a multimeter to confirm connections before powering up.

When soldering jumpers, use flux and a fine tip. Avoid excessive heat. If the wire is stranded, twist and tin it first. For high-current paths, use thicker wire and reinforce with solder. Label wires with a marker if multiple.

After modification, test thoroughly. Check for shorts between adjacent wires. If the device works, secure the wires with tape or glue. Document the modification with a photo and note. Remember, jumpers are a temporary fixadvise the customer if it's not permanent. Practice on old boards by adding an LED with a resistor.

6. Rework Documentation and Verification

Documenting your rework is essential for quality and traceability. After every repair, record: date, board model, fault, components replaced, and any jumpers or pad repairs. Use a notebook or a smartphone app. Take before and after photos. This helps if the device fails again and builds trust with customers.

Verification: After rework, visually inspect with a magnifier. Check for solder bridges, cold joints, and stray solder balls. Use a multimeter to test continuity, resistance, and voltage. Power up with a current-limited supply if possible. For simple devices, check that it functions as expected.

In Africa, where customer trust is crucial, show the customer the repair. Explain what you did. Provide a warrantyeven 7 days. Keep a copy of the documentation. If you repair many devices, create a simple form: serial number, fault, action, parts used, technician name.

For complex repairs, test under load. For example, if you replaced a power transistor, run the device for 10 minutes and check temperature. If it gets too hot, recheck the solder joints. Use a thermal camera or your finger (carefully) to detect hot spots.

Finally, clean the board with isopropyl alcohol to remove flux. Flux residue can cause corrosion over time, especially in humid climates. Store repaired boards in anti-static bags. If you cannot test fully, note that in your documentation. Always follow up with the customer after a few days. Good documentation turns a one-time repair into a repeat customer.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Rework and Repair in African Electronics Assembly

In African electronics assembly and repair enterprises from Nairobi's River Road component markets to Lagos's Computer Village and Cape Town's informal electronics clusters rework and repair capability determines whether a business survives on thin margins or thrives on repeat contracts. A technician who can reliably replace a BGA on a control board, repair a lifted pad on a power supply, or document a modification for a client's warranty file is worth three who can only assemble. This practical reference consolidates the tools, techniques, data, and verification habits that make rework profitable rather than wasteful.

Rework is not the opposite of quality it is the recovery mechanism that keeps quality affordable. In markets where imported boards arrive with intermittent faults, where humidity and dust accelerate corrosion, and where replacement parts may take weeks to source, the ability to rework locally is a competitive advantage. This lesson gives you the numbers, the sources, and the checklists to build that advantage.

Tools and Equipment: What You Actually Need

The minimum viable rework bench in an African context includes: a temperature-controlled soldering station (6080 W, with fine and chisel tips), a hot air rework station (700900 W, adjustable 100450 C), a desoldering pump or vacuum desoldering station, flux (no-clean and water-soluble), solder wick, isopropyl alcohol (IPA 99%), ESD-safe tweezers, a magnifier or stereo microscope (1020), and a fume extractor. For pad and track repair, add copper foil tape, epoxy (two-part, high-temperature), and jumper wire (30 AWG and 26 AWG). For verification, a multimeter with continuity and diode test, and ideally an oscilloscope or at least a logic probe.

Budget reality: a competent entry-level rework setup in Nairobi or Accra costs roughly USD 250600, while a production-grade station with microscope and fume extraction runs USD 1,5004,000. The return on investment comes from jobs that would otherwise be scrapped a single repaired inverter board or ECU can justify the entire toolset.

Desoldering Techniques: Data-Driven Choices

Desoldering removes solder from a joint to free a component. The three main methods are wick (braid), pump (manual or vacuum), and hot air. Choice depends on pad size, thermal mass, and whether the component will be reused. For through-hole parts on single-sided boards common in African power supplies, a manual pump plus wick is fastest. For multi-layer boards with ground planes, hot air or a vacuum desoldering station is essential because the heat sink effect of inner copper layers defeats a 40 W iron.

Source: IPC-7711/7721 and manufacturer application notes (Weller, Hakko, JBC).

Practical rule: preheat the board to 80120 C with a hot plate or preheater before desoldering multi-layer joints. This reduces required tip temperature, cuts dwell time, and dramatically lowers pad-lift risk a major issue in humid African workshops where boards absorb moisture.

Component Removal and Replacement

For SMD removal, apply flux, heat both ends alternately with two irons or use hot air, and lift with tweezers once solder is molten. For BGA, use a dedicated rework station with bottom-side preheat and top-side hot air; align with a split-optics or camera system. Replacement follows the reverse: clean pads, apply fresh solder or paste, position, and reflow. For through-hole, ensure holes are clear before insertion; use a solder sucker and wick combination.

In African markets, counterfeit or recycled components are common. Always verify component markings, test with a multimeter, and where possible use a curve tracer or known-good board for comparison. A reworked board that fails in the client's hands costs more than the component saved.

Pad and Track Repair

Lifted pads and broken tracks are the most common rework defects. Repair methods: (1) for a lifted pad still attached, reflow with flux and secure with epoxy; (2) for a missing pad, use a copper foil eyelet or a short jumper wire to the nearest via; (3) for a broken track, scrape the solder mask, tin the exposed copper, and bridge with 30 AWG wire, then secure with epoxy. Cure epoxy at 120150 C for 3060 minutes depending on product.

SAFETY WARNING Lead and Flux Fumes

Solder containing lead (SnPb) and rosin flux fumes are hazardous. In poorly ventilated workshops common in dense African urban markets, cumulative exposure causes respiratory and neurological damage. Always use a fume extractor or work outdoors with cross-ventilation. Wash hands after handling leaded solder never eat or smoke at the bench. Store leaded solder and flux separately from food. If you feel dizzy, leave the area immediately. Use lead-free solder where the client's specification allows.

Jumper Wires and Modifications

Jumper wires restore continuity or implement design changes. Use 30 AWG for signal, 26 AWG for power. Route jumpers on the component side where possible, secure with epoxy or adhesive, and keep them short to minimize inductance. Document every modification with a photo and a note in the job file. For client-owned equipment, get written approval before modifying this protects you legally and commercially.

Rework Documentation and Verification

Documentation is the difference between a repair shop and a rework business. For each job, record: board ID, fault description, rework performed, materials used, technician, date, and verification result. Verification includes visual inspection (IPC-A-610 criteria), continuity and resistance checks, functional test, and where applicable, thermal or load test. Keep records for at least the warranty period typically 312 months in African B2B contracts.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Preheat multi-layer boards to 80120 C before desoldering to prevent pad lift.
  • Use flux on every joint never rely on solder alone for heat transfer.
  • Match tip size to pad: too small means longer dwell and more damage.
  • Verify component authenticity before installation; test with multimeter.
  • For pad/track repair, always epoxy-secure the repair and check continuity < 0.5 .
  • Use fume extraction and wash hands after leaded solder work.
  • Photograph every modification and file it with the job record.
  • Functionally test the board under load before returning to client.
