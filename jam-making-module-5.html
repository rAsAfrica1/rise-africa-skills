JAM MAKING · MODULE 5 OF 12

Sterilisation and Filling

The step that decides shelf life. A perfect jam in an unsterile jar is mouldy in two weeks. A good jam in a sterile jar lasts a year on a shelf.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain what sterilisation achieves and what it does not
  • Sterilise jars by any of three proven methods
  • Prepare lids and seals correctly for a tight vacuum
  • Fill jars without introducing contamination or air bubbles
  • Understand headspace and why it determines seal quality
  • Check seals, store jars, and know the shelf life you can promise
Lesson 5.1

Why sterilisation matters

Jam is preserved by sugar, acid, and heat. Sugar binds the water in the fruit so spoilage organisms cannot use it. Acid slows their growth. Heat kills them. But none of that matters if the jar the jam goes into is carrying mould spores.

A jar straight from a shop shelf is not sterile. It may have been sitting in a warehouse for months, handled by many people, and used as a container for something else before that. Even a new jar from a factory has microscopic dust and contamination on the inside surface.

What sterilisation does and does not do

DoesDoes not
Kills mould spores, yeast, and most bacteria on the jar surfaceSterilise the jam itself. The jam sterilises itself with sugar, acid and heat.
Creates a vacuum-tight seal when the hot jam coolsKeep the jam fresh once the jar is opened
Extends shelf life from days to 6–24 monthsMake up for bad jam. If the jam was not cooked to set, a sterile jar will not save it.
Prevents the most common cause of jam going mouldyReplace good hygiene during filling

What grows on jam and why

The most common spoilage in jam is mould — a fuzzy patch on the surface, sometimes blue-green, sometimes white. It comes from airborne spores that landed on the jam surface. Sterile jars and tight seals prevent it. An unsterile jar or a loose lid lets it in.

Less common but more serious is fermentation — a yeasty or alcoholic smell — caused by yeasts that survive in the jam. This happens with under-cooked jam, too little sugar, or contamination during filling. If you ever smell alcohol on opening a jar, discard the whole jar. Do not eat it.

Lesson 5.2

Three methods for sterilising jars

All three work. Choose based on your equipment and your batch size.

Demonstration of the three methods — boiling, oven, and dishwasher.

Method 1 — Boiling (most reliable)

  1. Wash jars and lids in hot soapy water. Rinse well.
  2. Fill a large pot with water and bring to a boil.
  3. Lower jars into the boiling water. Make sure they are fully submerged and no air is trapped inside.
  4. Boil for 10 minutes. At altitude above 1,000m, add 1 minute per 300m.
  5. Remove with a jar lifter. Stand upside down on a clean tea towel to drain. Do not touch the inside of the jar with your hands.
  6. Use within 30 minutes, while still hot.

Lids: do not boil metal lids with rubber seals for more than 5 minutes — it can damage the seal. Pour boiling water over them in a bowl and let them sit while you sterilise the jars, then drain.

Method 2 — Oven (good for large batches)

  1. Wash jars and lids. Rinse well. Shake out excess water.
  2. Place jars upright on a baking tray, not touching each other.
  3. Place the tray in a cold oven and turn it on to 140°C (280°F).
  4. Once the oven reaches temperature, sterilise for 20 minutes.
  5. Turn off. Leave jars in the oven until you are ready to fill — they stay sterile and hot.

Do not use the oven for lids with rubber seals

Metal lids with rubber gaskets can be damaged by oven heat. If using the oven method, sterilise the jars in the oven and the lids in boiling water. Or use all-metal lids without a soft seal, which can go in the oven.

Method 3 — Dishwasher (least reliable but easiest)

  1. Wash jars on the hottest cycle your dishwasher has.
  2. Use the "heated dry" or "sanitise" cycle if your dishwasher has one.
  3. Leave jars in the dishwasher with the door closed until you are ready to fill.

This works for many home kitchens but does not reliably kill all spores in all dishwashers. If you make jam for sale, use boiling or oven method. Dishwasher is fine for family consumption.

Boiling is the standard for commercial jam production. Oven works well for larger batches. Dishwasher is acceptable only for family consumption.

Lesson 5.3

Preparing lids and seals

The lid is the other half of the seal. A sterile jar with a bad lid is a jar that will go mouldy.

Types of jar closure

What you must never do

  • Never reuse commercial jars for resale. The seal is compromised from the first use and cannot be relied on for a second. Fine for home storage, not for selling.
  • Never buy cheap unbranded lids. A lid that does not seal properly causes more lost jam than any other single cause. Buy from a supplier who sells to food businesses, not from a general market.
  • Never use a lid with visible rust on the inside. The rust will spread and the seal will fail.

Preparing lids before filling

  1. Wash lids in hot soapy water. Rinse thoroughly.
  2. For metal lids with rubber seals: pour boiling water over them in a bowl. Leave 2 minutes. Drain. Do not boil for more than 5 minutes or the seal degrades.
  3. Keep lids in hot water until the moment you need them. A hot lid softens the gasket and produces a better vacuum as it cools.
Lesson 5.4

Filling technique

Filling is where most contamination is introduced. A clean fill is fast, hot, and does not touch the rim.

Before you start

  • Jars sterilised, drained, and still hot (or warm from the oven)
  • Lids in hot water, ready to lift out
  • A wide-mouthed funnel in a clean jug
  • A clean cloth for wiping rims
  • A jar lifter for moving hot jars
  • Jars on a clean, stable surface — not the edge of the counter, not on top of a stove

The filling process, step by step

  1. Lift a hot jar with the jar lifter. Hold it steady on a work surface. Do not put your hand inside — it is hot.
  2. Insert the funnel. Push it down so it sits firmly in the neck.
  3. Ladle or pour the jam through the funnel. Fill to 5mm (about 1/4 inch) from the top of the jar rim. That gap is the headspace (Lesson 5.5).
  4. Remove the funnel and wipe the rim. Use a clean cloth dampened with hot water. Any jam on the rim will prevent the lid from sealing.
  5. Place the lid on the jar. Screw it down firmly by hand — snug, not brutally tight. You are not trying to compress the gasket to death.
  6. Stand the jar to one side to cool. Do not move it while it is hot. Moving a hot jar can break the vacuum seal as it forms.
  7. Repeat with each jar.

Do not double dip

Never put a spoon that has been in your mouth back into the jam pot. Never use the same cloth to wipe multiple jar rims if the cloth becomes dirty. Never leave the funnel sitting in the jam pot for minutes at a time between jars.

Every one of these is a contamination route. Jam sold to the public carries a legal responsibility. Be strict about it.

Speed matters

Jam cools fast. From pot to sealed jar, the entire filling process should take less than 20 minutes for a home batch. If you are slower than that, the jam drops below 80°C and the vacuum forming is weaker. Warm the jars before filling, keep the pot on very low heat while filling (just enough to keep it hot, not boiling), and work at a steady pace.

Lesson 5.5

Headspace and air bubbles

Headspace is the empty gap between the top of the jam and the underside of the lid. It looks unimportant. It is one of the most critical details in jam making.

Why headspace matters

  1. Too little headspace (jam touching the lid): The jam gets under the lid gasket, prevents a good seal, and mould grows at the top of the jar. Also, the jam may overflow during the water-bath canning stage (Module 7) if you use one.
  2. Too much headspace (5cm or more): Extra air in the jar means oxygen exposure, which causes discolouration over months and gives mould spores more room. The vacuum also forms less reliably.
  3. The right headspace (5–6mm): Enough room for the jam to expand as it heats, then contract as it cools, creating a strong vacuum without touching the lid.

How to measure headspace

Most experienced jam makers do this by eye. When the jam level is roughly the same as the level of the jar's threads (the spiral ridges that the lid screws onto), you have the right headspace.

For a beginner: use a ruler or a marked stick. Insert it against the inside of the jar. Measure from the top rim down to the jam surface. Aim for 5–6mm.

Aim for 5–6mm of headspace. Enough for a strong vacuum, not enough for spoilage.

Air bubbles

Stirring fast introduces air bubbles into the jam. If you seal a jar full of bubbles, they can:

  • Show through the glass as a foamy, unprofessional texture
  • Rise to the surface over weeks, creating a layer of air under the lid
  • Create micro-channels that let mould spores move down into the jam

To minimise bubbles: stir slowly. Ladle gently. If a jar does have visible bubbles, tap it on the work surface a few times — most bubbles rise to the top. Do not stir after filling.

Lesson 5.6

Checking seals and storing jars

Once the jars are filled, sealed, and cooling, you have one more job: checking that the seal actually worked.

How to check a seal

  1. Listen for the "pop." A few minutes to hours after filling, each jar should make a small "pop" sound as the lid presses inward. This is the vacuum forming. Not every jar pops audibly, so do not rely on this alone.
  2. Press the centre of the lid. A sealed lid is concave — the centre is pulled down flat or slightly inward. An unsealed lid pops up and down when pressed. Test 24 hours after filling.
  3. Look at the lid. A properly sealed lid sits flat with a slight dip in the centre. An unsealed lid bulges upward or sits flat.

What to do if a jar has not sealed

If a jar lid pops up and down when pressed after 24 hours, the seal has failed.

  1. Do not store it. The jam inside is fine right now, but it will go mouldy within a week or two.
  2. Refrigerate and use within 2–3 weeks. Consider it an opened jar.
  3. Or re-process: empty the jam back into a pot, heat it back to set temperature, sterilise a new jar, and refill. Use a fresh lid.

Cooling and storing

  • Cool at room temperature for 12–24 hours. Do not put hot jars in a fridge — the thermal shock can crack them, and rapid cooling weakens the vacuum.
  • Do not stack jars while they are still warm. The lids are soft and stacking can break the seal.
  • Label after 24 hours once you have confirmed all seals. Include: product name, fruit variety, date made, batch number, and best-before date.
  • Store in a cool, dark, dry place. A cupboard, a pantry, or a cellar. Not above a stove, not next to a boiler, not in direct sunlight.
  • Do not move jars unnecessarily. Once stored, they should sit still.

Shelf life — what you can promise

A correctly made and sealed jar of jam has a shelf life of:

  • 12–24 months in a cool dark store, unopened
  • 4–6 weeks in the fridge once opened

These are general figures. Your local food safety authority may have specific rules about what "best-before" date you can put on a jar sold to the public. Ask them before you print labels.

The signs a stored jar has gone bad

  • Mould on the surface — fuzzy patch of any colour. Discard the whole jar. Do not scrape off the mould and eat the rest — mould produces invisible toxins that spread through the jam.
  • Fermentation smell — a yeasty, alcoholic, or vinegary smell. Discard the whole jar.
  • Lid bulging upward — gas is being produced inside. Discard the whole jar.
  • Fizzy or foamy texture when opened — signs of fermentation. Discard.

In all cases, discard the jar. Do not open, smell, taste, or try to rescue it. A jar of jam costs $2. A case of food poisoning costs a customer relationship and possibly a legal problem.

Practical assignment — Module 5

Make and seal a batch of jam using the full sterilisation and filling process.

  1. Sterilise 6 jars using the boiling method. Time the boiling exactly. Photograph the jars draining upside down on the tea towel.
  2. Make a jam batch (any fruit, 1kg is fine). Cook to set (Module 4).
  3. Fill all 6 jars using the funnel and jar lifter. Aim for exactly 5mm headspace. Photograph one jar right after filling, from the side, showing the headspace.
  4. Wipe rims, seal, and cool. Note the time you sealed the jars.
  5. 24 hours later: press each lid centre. Record which jars sealed (concave, no click) and which did not (pops up and down).
  6. Label each jar with product name, fruit variety, date made, batch number, best-before (12 months ahead), and your business name.
  7. Store in a cool dark place. Check one jar per month for the next three months to confirm shelf stability.
  8. Open one jar after 1 week and taste it. Note whether the flavour has developed (usually it does — jam tastes better after a week or two).

Points 3, 5 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 3 tests whether you can hit headspace consistently. Point 5 tests whether your seals actually work. Point 6 is the labelling that keeps you legal and gives customers confidence.

Module 5 knowledge check

1. What is the primary purpose of sterilising jars?

  1. To make them shiny
  2. To kill mould spores, yeast, and bacteria so the jam's shelf life can be months instead of days
  3. To make the jam sweeter
  4. To prevent the jam from sticking to the glass
Show answer

b. A jar carries mould spores, yeasts and bacteria from the factory, storage, or the last use. Sterilising kills them. The hot jam then creates a vacuum seal that prevents new contamination, giving a shelf life of 12–24 months instead of 1–2 weeks.

2. How long should jars be boiled to sterilise them?

  1. 1 minute
  2. 10 minutes
  3. 30 minutes
  4. Until the water turns colour
Show answer

b. Ten minutes of full submersion in boiling water is the standard. At altitude above 1,000m, add 1 minute per additional 300m. Longer than this is unnecessary; shorter risks surviving spores.

3. What is headspace, and how much should a jar of jam have?

  1. The label area — fill all the way to the top
  2. The gap between the jam and the lid — 5 to 6mm
  3. The space below the jar's threads — 3cm
  4. The gap on the shelf between jars — 2cm
Show answer

b. Headspace is 5–6mm of empty space at the top of the jar. Too little and the jam touches the lid (prevents seal, encourages mould). Too much and there is extra air in the jar (slower vacuum, discolouration over time).

4. How do you check that a jar has sealed properly?

  1. Shake the jar
  2. Press the centre of the lid — it should be concave (pulled down) and not pop up and down
  3. Look for bubbles in the jam
  4. Turn the jar upside down
Show answer

b. A sealed lid is concave — the centre is pulled slightly down by the vacuum. Test 24 hours after filling. An unsealed lid pops up and down. If a jar has not sealed, refrigerate and use within 2–3 weeks, or re-process with a new lid.

5. What should you do if you find mould on top of a jar of jam?

  1. Scrape it off and eat the rest
  2. Discard the whole jar — mould produces invisible toxins that spread through the jam
  3. Re-boil the jam
  4. Add more sugar
Show answer

b. Mould produces mycotoxins that spread invisibly through the whole jar, not just where you can see the mould. You cannot remove them by scraping or re-boiling. Discard the entire jar — a $2 loss is better than a food safety incident.
