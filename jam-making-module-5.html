1. Making Clear Fruit Jellies: Apple and Grape

To make a crystal-clear apple or grape jelly, start with firm, slightly underripe fruit. Underripe apples and grapes have more natural pectin, which helps the jelly set. Squeezing will make the jelly cloudy. You should get about 1 litre of juice. Measure the juice back into a clean pot. For every 1 litre of juice, add 800 g of white sugar and 2 tablespoons of lemon juice (bottled or fresh). The lemon juice adds acid needed for pectin to set.

2. Citrus Marmalade: Orange and Lemon

Marmalade is a citrus jelly with shreds of peel. For a classic orange and lemon marmalade, use 1 kg of oranges (any variety, but Seville oranges are best if available) and 2 lemons. 3. Herb-Infused Jellies: Mint and Rosemary

Herb jellies are versatileâserve with roast lamb, cheese, or as a glaze. For mint jelly, use 1 kg of tart green apples (or 1 litre of apple juice) and a large bunch of fresh mint (about 50 g). 4. Wine and Champagne Jellies

Wine and champagne jellies are elegant and make great gifts. You need a base of fruit pectinâeither homemade apple jelly or commercial liquid pectin. For a simple wine jelly, use 750 ml of red or white wine (any dry variety, like Cabernet or Chenin Blanc) and 500 g of sugar. For champagne jelly, use 750 ml of sparkling wine (or non-alcoholic sparkling cider) and 500 g sugar. You also need 2 tablespoons lemon juice and 1 pouch (85 ml) of liquid pectin, or 1 litre of homemade apple jelly base.

If using homemade apple jelly base: melt 1 litre of apple jelly over low heat. Add the wine, sugar, and lemon juice. Stir until sugar dissolves. 5. Pepper Jelly: Sweet and Spicy

Pepper jelly is a sweet-hot condiment that pairs beautifully with cream cheese, crackers, or grilled meats. 6. Troubleshooting Jelly Clarity and Set

Even experienced cooks face cloudy jelly or failed set. Hereâs how to fix common problems. For cloudy jelly: the main cause is squeezing the jelly bag or using overripe fruit. Troubleshooting Jelly Clarity and Set

Cloudy jelly usually comes from pulp, not pectin. Strain through a double layer of muslin or a clean cotton cloth â do not squeeze or press the bag. Allow it to drip for 2â4 hours, or overnight. Adding a teaspoon of egg white per 2 L and re-boiling then skimming also clarifies. Avoid over-stirring once sugar is in.

Syrupy jelly means you stopped too early. Return to a rolling boil and cook to 104â105 Â°C, or until a spoonful on a cold plate wrinkles when pushed. If pectin was the issue, a second boil with added pectin will rescue it â but label it as a "spread" rather than a jelly.

Rubbery or tough jelly means too much pectin or over-evaporation. Next batch, reduce added pectin by 20% or add 50 ml water per litre before the final boil.

Crystals in the jar come from undissolved sugar. Stir until the sugar is fully dissolved before the boil begins, and never add sugar to a boiling pot.

Mould on top means headspace was too large, the jar was not sterile, or the Brix was below 65. Discard the jar; do not scrape and re-sell.
