Agriculture · 6-Month Program

Groundnut Farming

The underground legume — pegging agronomy, gypsum and dense early stands, the rosette defence, the shell-test harvest, and the drying-and-sorting discipline that beats aflatoxin and builds the peanut butter business.

Course Overview

Groundnut is food, oil, peanut butter, livestock feed and free nitrogen from one plant — and the strangest grower in this college: it flowers in the air and fruits in the soil, on pegs that must never be disturbed. This course teaches the crop around its two decisive battles. In the field: rosette disease, Africa's own groundnut epidemic, defeated almost entirely by resistant varieties planted early and dense. From flowering to the store: aflatoxin, the invisible toxin that follows drought stress, pod damage, slow drying and poor sorting — beaten by a chain of ordinary disciplines this course drills one by one, because the same chain is also the road to premium buyers and safe peanut butter. Around these battles sit the craft lessons: seed kept in its shell, gypsum at flowering, the hands-off pegging law, the darkened-shell maturity test, windrow curing, gleaning, hay stacking and the honest budgets from one hectare to a processing startup.

What You Will Learn

  • Store in-shell for the price rise; build the budgets, the peanut butter startup and premium channels

Month 1: The Crop, Types & the Aflatoxin Sentence
Start

Lesson 1.1 — The crop that fruits underground

Groundnut (peanut) is Africa's great oilseed-legume: food, cooking oil, peanut butter, snack, livestock feed and soil improver in one plant. Its strangest feature drives everything in this course: groundnut flowers above ground but fruits below it. After a flower is fertilised, it sends down a peg — a stalk that drills into the soil — and the pod develops underground at the peg's tip. Loose soil, calcium in the podding zone, and undisturbed pegs are therefore not details; they ARE groundnut agronomy.

  • Season: 90–140 days depending on type — a rains-season crop for semi-arid and savanna zones on light sandy-loam soils.
  • A legume: fixes its own nitrogen and leaves fertility behind — the premier rotation partner for maize and cereals (a story you know from the Beans course).
  • Yields: African average ~800–1,000 kg/ha in pods; good management with improved seed reaches 2,000–3,500 kg/ha.

Ask extension for released varieties for your zone, and read two columns before any other: rosette resistance and days to maturity. Module 4 explains why rosette resistance can be the whole harvest, and Lesson 5.1 explains why maturity days matter more in groundnut than almost any crop.

Lesson 1.3 — The aflatoxin sentence, stated on day one

One word will follow this whole course: aflatoxin — the invisible toxin made by Aspergillus moulds in stressed, damaged or badly dried pods. It causes liver cancer, stunts children, and closes export markets. Everything you will learn — end-of-season stress management, gentle lifting, fast proper drying, sorting, dry storage — is also, simultaneously, aflatoxin control. Groundnut done right is safe food and premium exports; done carelessly it is quietly dangerous. This course teaches it right.

Month 2: Seed, Land & the Dense Early Stand
Locked

Lesson 2.1 — Seed: handle like eggs, shell at the last minute

Groundnut seed is a living embryo in a thin skin, and cracked seed does not grow. The rules:

  • Store seed in the shell; hand-shell only 1–2 weeks before planting. Shelled seed loses viability fast and cracks in bags; the pod is nature's seed packet.
  • Shell gently by hand for seed (machines for food/sale stock, not seed); select bold, uniform, uncracked kernels with intact skins; discard shrivelled, mouldy or discoloured ones — that sorting is also your first aflatoxin cull.
  • Source improved, rosette-resistant varieties from certified/QDS suppliers or your own best plot; refresh every few seasons. Test germination the Bean-course way: 50 seeds, damp cloth, count at 5–7 days; below 75–80%, adjust rate or source better seed.
  • Where recommended locally, treat seed with a fungicide seed dressing against soil rots; where groundnut is new to a field, rhizobium inoculant (groundnut-type) helps nodulation.

Lesson 2.2 — Land: loose, level, and limed where sour

Plough/dig to a fine loose tilth 15–20 cm deep — pegs and lifting both demand it. On crusting or wetter soils, low ridges or raised beds help; on light sands, flat planting is fine. Groundnut hates acid soils' aluminium and needs calcium where pods form: where soils are sour or pops (empty pods) are known locally, apply lime or gypsum — gypsum at flowering is the classic podding-zone calcium fix (Lesson 3.2).

Lesson 2.3 — Density: the quiet yield (and rosette) decision

TypeSpacingPopulation
Spanish/bunchRows 45–60 cm, plants 10–15 cm; 1 seed/station, 3–5 cm deep~120,000–200,000/ha
Virginia/runnerRows 60–75 cm, plants 15–20 cm~80,000–110,000/ha

Plant DENSE and plant EARLY, at the first good rains. This is not only yield arithmetic: a closed canopy established early is the classic cultural defence against rosette disease, because the aphids that carry it land preferentially on sparse, late, gappy stands (Module 4 completes this story). Gap-fill within two weeks. Seed rate: commonly 60–100+ kg of good kernels per hectare — budget for it; thin stands are the most common groundnut mistake in Africa.

Month 3: The Pegging Law, Feeding & Water
Locked

Lesson 3.1 — The pegging law: weed early, then hands off the soil

Groundnut weeding has a hard deadline written by the plant itself:

  • Weeks 2–6: weed thoroughly — two clean hoeings/hand-weedings before flowering ends. The crop is small, weeds are murder on it, and the soil may still be worked freely.
  • From peak flowering/pegging (~6–7 weeks): STOP deep soil work near rows. Every hoe stroke now tears pegs and buries none — each broken peg is a pod deleted. Hand-pull big escapes only, stepping carefully.
  • Light earthing-up at final weeding (just before pegging peaks, especially for bunch types on ridges) banks loose soil where pegs will land — the last soil work of the season.

Lesson 3.3 — Water rhythm

Critical moisture windows: establishment, and above all flowering–pegging–pod fill (roughly weeks 5–11) — drought there cuts yield hardest and, at season's end, flips the aflatoxin switch (Module 4). Early planting into the rains is the main water strategy; on ridges, tied ridging harvests storms in drier zones. Waterlogging even briefly yellows and stunts the crop — drainage matters on heavier land.

Lesson 3.4 — Groundnut in the farm system

Rotate cereals → groundnut → cereals: the cereal that follows commonly gains the equivalent of a meaningful fertiliser dose from fixed N and residues. Never follow groundnut with groundnut — soil diseases and leaf spots build fast. Intercropping with widely spaced cereals is traditional; keep the cereal sparse or the shade cost exceeds the benefit. And plan the haulm harvest: groundnut hay (the vines after lifting) is premium livestock feed that sells or feeds your own animals — in many regions worth 20–30% as much as the pods themselves.

Month 4: Rosette, Leaf Spots & Field Aflatoxin
Locked

Lesson 4.1 — Rosette: the epidemic disease

Groundnut rosette is Africa's signature groundnut disease — a virus complex transmitted by aphids, found nowhere else in the world. Infected plants become stunted bunched 'rosettes' with mottled curled leaves (chlorotic yellow or green form) and yield nothing. In epidemic years unprotected late-planted fields lose 50–100%. The defence stack — you already hold most of it:

  1. Rosette-resistant released varieties — widely available across the continent; the first line and the reason Module 1 sent you to that column.
  2. Early planting + dense stands — the closed canopy the aphid hates (Module 2's promise kept).
  3. Rogue infected plants early, while they are few — each rosette is an aphid virus-fountain.
  4. Insecticide against aphids is a narrow supplement in seed plots and epidemic outbreaks, not a habit — resistance + agronomy do the real work.

Lesson 4.2 — Leaf spots and rust: the slow thieves

Early and late leaf spot (brown/black spotted, yellowing, defoliating leaves from the bottom up) and groundnut rust are everywhere groundnut grows; together they quietly take 20–50% of yield and most of the haulm value. Management: resistant/tolerant varieties, rotation and destruction of volunteer plants and old crop debris (the spores' home), and — where the crop justifies it — 2–3 fungicide sprays (e.g. chlorothalonil/mancozeb-type or triazole per local recommendation) starting when the first spots appear around flowering. A defoliated crop also drops more pods at lifting: leaf health IS harvest efficiency.

Lesson 4.3 — The aflatoxin chain, part one: the field

Aflatoxin control begins BEFORE harvest. Aspergillus lives in soil and invades pods through three doors: end-of-season drought stress (the biggest), pod damage (termites, millipedes, pod borers, cracked shells), and over-mature pods left in the ground. Field rules: match variety length to your rain window so the crop finishes before deep drought; keep podding-zone soil covered and soft; control soil pests where serious; and harvest ON TIME — Module 5's maturity test, not the calendar, gives the date. A stressed, late, termite-chewed crop is an aflatoxin crop before it ever reaches the drying yard.

Month 5: Shell-Test Harvest, Drying & Sorting
Locked

Lesson 5.1 — The maturity test: open pods, read shells

Groundnut hides its harvest date underground; the calendar only estimates it. From the expected window onward, lift 3–5 sample plants weekly and open pods:

  • Ready: ~70%+ of pods with the inside shell wall darkened (brown-black veining/blotching), kernels plump, filling the shell, skins coloured for the variety.
  • Too early: white inner shells, watery pale kernels — light pods, poor oil, poor grades.
  • Too late: pegs rotting, pods detaching in the ground (lost at lifting), Spanish types sprouting if rains linger, and aflatoxin risk climbing daily.

Lesson 5.2 — Lifting and the windrow cure

  1. Lift with hoe or fork entering BESIDE the row, loosening under the pod zone, pulling plants gently so pods come attached; hand-glean the row for detached pods — in good ground gleaning recovers 5–10% of the crop.
  2. Shake soil off. Never leave lifted plants flat on wet soil.
  3. Windrow/stook to dry, pods UP: invert small bundles so pods face sun and air with foliage beneath, or build small A-frame stooks/racks (the 'Mandela cock' style stack in Southern Africa) — pods off the ground, air moving through. Field-dry 3–7 days by weather.
  4. Then strip/pluck pods from haulms; stack haulms as hay (Module 3's second harvest); finish pod drying on mats, tarpaulins or raised racks — never directly on bare soil — turning daily.
The drying target is not negotiable: pods to ~10% moisture, kernels ~7–8%, in days not weeks. The rattle test: dry pods rattle when shaken and kernels snap, not bend. Slow drying in heaps, on damp soil, or under rain showers is the second and biggest aflatoxin door. Fast, off-ground, well-turned drying is the single most protective act in the groundnut year.

Lesson 5.3 — Sorting: the aflatoxin checkpoint you control

Before storage or sale, hand-sort and discard: mouldy, discoloured, shrivelled, insect-holed and broken-shell pods (and later, at any shelling, the same classes of kernels). Research across Africa shows sorting alone removes the large majority of aflatoxin from a farm lot, because the toxin concentrates in exactly the damaged fraction. Sorted rejects go to disposal — not into home food, and not quietly into livestock feed (aflatoxin passes into milk and harms animals too).

Lesson 5.4 — Storage

  • Store in the shell — the pod protects kernels from insects, damage and moisture swings. Shell only for sale, processing or seed.
  • Dry pods in clean woven sacks on pallets/racks in a dry, ventilated, rodent-proofed store; hermetic bags (your Beans-course technology) work excellently for shelled kernels and seed stock.
  • Inspect monthly; any musty smell or visible mould means re-dry and re-sort immediately.
  • Well-stored in-shell groundnut holds quality 6–10 months — carrying you from glut prices to the lean-season and festive peaks.

Lesson 6.3 — The peanut butter startup

The most accessible value-add in this course: roast (drum or pan, even and light — burnt is bitter, under-roasted is flat), blanch/rub skins off, mill to butter (hand or small motor mill; two passes for smoothness), a pinch of salt, into clean jars/tubs. Start-up gear from a few hundred dollars; sell to shops, schools and households. Two absolute rules: only sorted clean kernels ever enter the roaster (aflatoxin concentrates in butter made from rejects — the toxin does not roast away), and taste-test every batch. Your Module 5 discipline is literally the brand.

Lesson 6.4 — Records, seed saving and the growth path

Per plot per season: variety and seed source; planting date and stand count at 3 weeks; weeding/gypsum/spray dates; lifting date with the shell-test evidence; pod and haulm weights; drying days to rattle; sorting reject percentage; storage checks; sales by date, form, price. Report card: stand density, days-to-dry, and reject percentage — the three numbers that predict both yield and safety. Save seed from your best plot in-shell, sorted, hermetically if shelled. Growth path: year one — one well-run hectare plus hay income; year two — storage timing plus shelling/grading; year three — peanut butter or oil-and-cake processing, and the documented-quality premium channels.

📚 Glossary

Peg (gynophore)
The stalk a fertilised groundnut flower drives into the soil; the pod forms at its underground tip.
Pegging law
This course's rule: weed clean before flowering ends, then no deep soil work near rows — broken pegs are deleted pods.
Pops
Empty pods from calcium deficiency in the podding zone — prevented by gypsum or lime on sour soils.
Gypsum
Calcium sulphate dusted over rows at early flowering, feeding calcium directly to where pods form.
Spanish/bunch types
Erect, shorter-season groundnuts with clustered pods and little seed dormancy.
Virginia/runner types
Spreading, longer-season, higher-potential types with fresh-seed dormancy.
Rosette
Africa's aphid-borne groundnut virus complex; stunted bunched plants yielding nothing — beaten by resistant varieties planted early and dense.
Leaf spots & rust
The defoliating fungal 'slow thieves'; managed by resistance, sanitation, rotation and timed sprays.
Aflatoxin
The invisible Aspergillus toxin following stress, damage, slow drying and damp storage; controlled by the field-to-store discipline chain.
Shell test
Opening sample pods to read maturity: ~70%+ darkened inner shell walls means lift.
Windrow / stook
Field-drying arrangements with pods up and off the soil, air moving through.
Rattle test
Dry enough to store: pods rattle when shaken, kernels snap rather than bend (~10% / 7–8% moisture).
Gleaning
Hand-recovering pods detached at lifting — commonly 5–10% of the crop.
Haulms / groundnut hay
The protein-rich vines after pod stripping — premium fodder, the second harvest.
Sorting
Removing mouldy, damaged and shrivelled pods/kernels — the checkpoint removing most aflatoxin from a lot.
Press cake
The protein-rich residue after oil pressing, sold as livestock feed.
