Groundnut Farming

A complete 6-month program — pegging-zone management, rosette-resistant varieties, the weeding timetable that must end before flowering, Mandela-cock drying, ruthless aflatoxin sorting, and the value ladder up to peanut butter.

⏱️ 6 Months (season + processing)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? A groundnut crop runs 90–150 days from planting to lifting, then drying, shelling, grading and value addition turn it into real money. Six months covers the full season plus the post-harvest work where groundnut profits actually live — peanut butter, roasted nuts and graded seed sell for several times the price of raw unshelled nuts. This course also takes seriously the one thing most groundnut training skips: aflatoxin, the invisible toxin that decides whether your crop can be sold to any formal buyer at all.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the field and at the drying racks.

⚠️ Read first: aflatoxin is the crop's central issue

  • Aflatoxin is an invisible, odourless toxin produced by Aspergillus moulds in groundnuts that are drought-stressed, damaged, lifted late, or dried and stored badly. It causes liver damage and cancer, stunts children, and is the reason formal buyers reject African groundnut consignments.
  • It cannot be cooked, roasted or washed out. The only control is prevention: timely harvest, fast drying to 8–10% moisture, careful shelling, ruthless sorting of shrivelled/discoloured/damaged kernels, and dry ventilated storage. Every one of those steps is taught in Months 5–6.
  • Never sell or eat mouldy, shrivelled or bitter-tasting nuts — and never feed them to livestock (the toxin passes into milk).
MONTH 1 — Groundnut Science, Varieties & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why groundnuts pay

  • Triple value: a food (protein and oil rich), a cash crop (peanut butter, oil, roasted snacks, confectionery), and a soil builder — as a legume it fixes nitrogen and leaves the field better for next season's maize.
  • Yield gap: smallholder averages often sit at 0.5–1 t/ha unshelled, while good management with quality seed reaches 2.5–4 t/ha. Same rainfall, same soil.
  • Value addition multiplies everything: raw unshelled nuts are the cheapest form you can sell; shelled graded kernels earn more; roasted and peanut butter earn several times more (Module 6).
  • Women's enterprise note: groundnut processing is one of the most accessible small-business paths in Africa — low equipment cost, year-round demand, and a product that needs no cold chain.

Lesson 1.2 — The biology that shapes management

  • Groundnuts flower above ground, then the fertilised flower sends down a "peg" that pushes into the soil where the pod forms. This is unique — and it means the SOIL SURFACE must stay loose and moist for pegs to penetrate.
  • Consequence 1: no ridging or hoeing after flowering starts — you break the pegs.
  • Consequence 2: hard, crusted or waterlogged surface soil dramatically cuts pod numbers regardless of how healthy the plant looks.
  • Nitrogen fixation: with the right rhizobium bacteria in the soil, groundnuts make their own nitrogen — so they need phosphorus, calcium and gypsum-type nutrition, NOT nitrogen fertiliser.

Lesson 1.3 — Types and varieties

TypeHabitMaturityNotes
Spanish/Valencia (bunch)Erect bunch, pods clustered at base90–110 daysEasy harvest, good for short seasons, less dormancy (can sprout if harvest delayed)

  • Choose for: season length, market preference (confectionery buyers want large uniform kernels; oil/butter buyers want high oil content), rosette-virus resistance (a major African disease), and drought tolerance where rains are short.
  • Ask extension for varieties bred for your area — and for aflatoxin-tolerant/early-maturing types where drought stress is common, since drought stress at pod filling is a key aflatoxin trigger.

Lesson 1.4 — Site and rotation

  • Best soil: light, well-drained sandy loam — heavy clay makes pegging and lifting difficult and increases pod loss in the ground. pH 5.5–7.0.
  • Rotate with cereals (maize, sorghum) and avoid following groundnuts with groundnuts — leaf spots, rosette and soil pests build up. A 2–3 year break is the standard.
  • Groundnuts fit beautifully before maize: the nitrogen they leave behind cuts next season's fertiliser bill measurably.

(2) Survey 3 buyers: raw unshelled, shelled kernel and peanut butter prices per kg — write down the multipliers. (3) Check your plot's rotation history and soil texture (light vs heavy).

MONTH 2 — Seed, Land Preparation & Planting ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Seed quality and handling

  • Groundnut seed is fragile: the kernel is a living embryo wrapped in a thin skin. Shell seed by HAND, gently, no more than 1–2 weeks before planting — machine-shelled or long-stored shelled seed germinates poorly.
  • Select only plump, undamaged, uniform kernels with intact skins. Discard shrivelled, cracked, discoloured or mouldy seed — it neither germinates nor is safe.
  • Germination test 10 days before planting: 100 kernels on damp cloth; below 80% germination, increase your seed rate or find better seed.
  • Seed dressing with a registered fungicide protects against soil-borne rots — worth doing where seedling losses have occurred.

Lesson 2.2 — Land preparation

  • Plough and harrow to a fine, loose tilth 20–25 cm deep — the whole crop is harvested from this layer, so its looseness determines both pod set and how many pods you actually recover at lifting.
  • Incorporate compost/manure where available (5–10 t/ha); avoid heavy fresh nitrogen inputs.
  • Apply basal phosphorus (e.g. single superphosphate/compound with high P) at roughly 100–200 kg/ha, and — critically — calcium in the pegging zone: gypsum at 200–400 kg/ha applied at flowering is standard practice for large-seeded types and prevents "pops" (empty shells).
  • Ridges or flat beds both work; ridges help drainage in wetter areas, flat planting is common on light soils.

Lesson 2.3 — Planting

  • Plant with the onset of reliable rains — groundnuts need consistent moisture from flowering through pod fill, so late planting into a shortening season is a direct route to drought stress and aflatoxin.
  • Spacing: bunch types 30–45 cm rows × 10–15 cm in-row (roughly 150,000–300,000 plants/ha); runners wider, 45–60 cm × 15–20 cm.
  • Depth 5–7 cm into moisture; too shallow dries out, too deep exhausts the seedling.
  • Seed rate: roughly 80–120 kg/ha of shelled kernels for bunch types (more for large-seeded runners) — a substantial cost, so seed quality matters even more.

Lesson 2.4 — Inoculation (where useful)

  • Where groundnuts haven't been grown recently, a rhizobium inoculant applied to seed at planting can improve nodulation and yield — cheap and worth trying where available.
  • Check nodules at flowering: dig a plant, and healthy nodules are pink/red inside when cut. White or green nodules mean fixation isn't working.

MONTH 3 — Weeding, Water & the Pegging Window ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Weeding on a strict timetable

  • The rule: keep the crop clean for the first 6 weeks, and finish all weeding BEFORE flowering/pegging begins (around days 30–40).
  • Weed 1 at 2–3 weeks, weed 2 at 4–5 weeks. After pegging starts, only hand-pull large weeds carefully — never hoe or ridge, because you will destroy pegs and pods.
  • Weeds cost groundnut crops 30–60% of yield when the early window is missed — the plant is small and slow-covering for its first month.

Lesson 3.2 — Water and the critical periods

  • Total crop need: roughly 500–700 mm well distributed. Groundnuts tolerate some drought — but not at the wrong moments.
  • The two critical windows: flowering/pegging and pod filling. Drought at flowering cuts pod numbers; drought during pod fill both shrinks kernels AND is the classic aflatoxin trigger.
  • Where you can irrigate, prioritise those windows above all others. Where you can't, choose an early-maturing variety and plant on time — timing is the poor farmer's irrigation.
  • Waterlogging is equally damaging: it rots pods in the ground and encourages disease. Drain properly.

Lesson 3.3 — Nutrition through the season

  • Calcium at pegging (gypsum) is the key in-season nutrient application — especially for large-seeded varieties.
  • Avoid nitrogen top dressing: it produces lush leaves at the expense of pods and can suppress the plant's own fixation.
  • Watch for deficiency signs: yellowing between veins (iron/manganese on alkaline soils), pale overall growth (poor nodulation — check nodules).

Lesson 3.4 — Managing the pegging zone

  • Keep the soil surface loose and free of crusting — a light mulch of crop residue between rows helps moisture and softness without disturbing pegs.
  • If the surface crusts after heavy rain before pegging begins, break it gently early; after pegging, leave it alone.

MONTH 4 — Pests & Diseases ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Groundnut rosette: the African crop-killer

  • Signs: severe stunting with bright yellow or mottled, distorted small leaves; plants in patches that spread outward.
  • Spread by APHIDS. There is no cure once infected — control is prevention: resistant varieties (widely available and highly effective), EARLY planting (escaping peak aphid flights), and dense correct plant populations (thin stands invite aphid landing).
  • Rogue out infected plants early where infection is light.

Lesson 4.2 — Leaf spots and rust

DiseaseSignsManagement
Early leaf spotBrown spots with yellow halos, from lower leaves upwardRotation, residue removal, resistant varieties, protectant fungicide in severe seasons
Late leaf spotDarker spots, heavy defoliation late in seasonSame — defoliation cuts pod fill and weakens pegs so pods stay in the ground at lifting
RustOrange pustules on leaf undersidesResistant varieties, fungicides where economic
  • Why leaf disease matters more than it looks: severe defoliation weakens the plant so that pegs rot and pods DETACH — you then lift plants with the crop still buried, an invisible loss of 20–40%.

Lesson 4.3 — Pests

  • Aphids: rosette carriers — scout early, control where populations build (soap/neem or registered products), and prioritise resistant varieties.
  • Termites: a major African groundnut pest — they tunnel into pods and pegs, causing pod loss and creating the wounds that let aflatoxin fungi in. Manage by timely harvest (don't leave a mature crop in the ground), clean fields, and soil treatments where severe.
  • Millipedes and white grubs: damage developing pods; rotation and clean cultivation help.
  • Birds, rodents and thieves: real at harvest — plan guarding for the lifting and drying period.

Lesson 4.4 — The aflatoxin prevention chain begins here

  • Aflatoxin infection largely starts in the FIELD: drought stress, termite and insect damage to pods, and delayed harvest all open the door.
  • So Month 4's disease/pest work is aflatoxin work: healthy foliage → intact pegs → sound pods → less infection.
  • Record every stress event (dry spells, termite damage) — those blocks should be harvested, dried and sorted with extra care.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Scout weekly for rosette symptoms and aphids; rogue infected plants and record percentages. (2) Assess termite pressure by digging 10 plants and inspecting pods; note affected blocks. (3) Record leaf-spot severity monthly on a simple 1–5 scale.

MODULE 5 — Harvest, Drying & Aflatoxin Control ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Knowing exactly when to lift

  • The definitive test: dig several plants, open a sample of pods and look INSIDE the shell. When 70–80% of pods show dark brown/black veining and colouring on the inner shell wall, and kernels have their variety's proper skin colour, the crop is mature.
  • Leaf yellowing alone is unreliable — disease can yellow a crop early, and a healthy runner can stay green when mature.
  • Timing is everything: lift too early and kernels are shrivelled and low-value; lift too late and pods detach in the soil, sprout (in low-dormancy Spanish types), and — critically — the aflatoxin risk rises sharply.

Lesson 5.2 — Lifting technique

  • Loosen soil along the row with a fork/plough first, then lift plants by hand so pods come with them — pulling from hard dry ground leaves half your crop buried.
  • Lift when soil moisture is moderate: not so dry that pods snap off, not so wet that soil clings and rots them.
  • Shake soil off gently; do not bang pods against tools or the ground (cracked shells invite mould).

Lesson 5.3 — Drying: the decisive aflatoxin step

  • The target: dry pods to about 8–10% moisture as fast as reasonably possible. Slow drying and rewetting are what produce aflatoxin.
  • The Mandela cock (windrow/stack) method — the recommended African practice: build a small stack around a central pole with pods facing INWARD and foliage outward. Air circulates, pods are shaded from direct sun and protected from rain and dew, and drying is even over 2–3 weeks.
  • Never dry pods directly on bare soil or on tarmac in blazing sun: ground contact re-infects and re-wets them; extreme heat cracks shells.
  • Never leave lifted plants in the field through rain; re-wetting after partial drying is the highest-risk condition of all.
  • The moisture check: properly dried kernels rattle in the shell, snap crisply when bitten, and the shell breaks cleanly rather than bending.

Lesson 5.4 — Stripping, shelling and sorting

  • Strip pods from plants only when fully dry; store unshelled where possible — shells protect kernels and unshelled nuts keep far better.
  • Shell gently (hand or a well-adjusted hand-sheller) close to sale — broken kernels are both lower-value and far more prone to mould.
  • SORT ruthlessly: remove every shrivelled, discoloured, mouldy, insect-damaged, cracked and undersized kernel. This hand-sorting step alone can cut aflatoxin levels dramatically and is the single highest-value hour of work in the whole crop.
  • The rejects are not "for home use" — mouldy nuts are unsafe for your family and for livestock too. Dispose of them.

Lesson 5.5 — Storage

  • Store dry pods/kernels in clean woven or hermetic bags, on pallets, off the floor, away from walls, in a dry ventilated store. Never in sealed plastic while even slightly moist.
  • Keep out rodents and insects; inspect monthly for any musty smell (the first sign of trouble) and re-dry if necessary.
  • Groundnuts stored unshelled at proper moisture keep well for many months — enabling the price-curve selling of Module 6.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Module 5): (1) Do the inner-shell maturity test on 20 pods weekly until the 70–80% point, then lift. (2) Build a Mandela cock stack and dry to the rattle/snap test; record the days taken. (3) Hand-sort a 10 kg sample and weigh the reject pile — that percentage is your aflatoxin-risk indicator.

MODULE 6 — Value Addition, Marketing & Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — The value ladder

FormWork requiredRelative value
Unshelled nuts (raw, at harvest)NoneBaseline — the lowest price

Roasted/salted snack packsRoasting, packingSubstantially higher per kg

  • The lesson: selling raw at harvest is selling at the bottom of the ladder in the cheapest month of the year. Every step up the ladder is available to a smallholder with modest equipment.

Lesson 6.2 — Peanut butter: the flagship product

  • Process: sort ruthlessly (aflatoxin!) → roast evenly (a controlled roast is what creates flavour) → cool → de-skin (rub and winnow) → grind (hand mill or motorised grinder) → optionally add salt/sugar/oil → fill clean dry jars → label and seal.
  • Hygiene and shelf life: absolutely dry equipment and jars; water contamination causes spoilage. Store cool and out of sunlight. Natural separation of oil is normal — say so on the label.
  • Compliance: peanut butter is a food product — check your country's requirements for food handling, labelling and any registration, and note that aflatoxin limits apply to processed products too.
  • Economics: a hand or small motorised grinder is inexpensive relative to the margin uplift, and the product sells year-round to households, tuckshops, schools and supermarkets.

Lesson 6.3 — Marketing

  • Channels: farm-gate traders (lowest), markets, aggregators/exporters (they test for aflatoxin and pay for quality — hence the sorting discipline), processors, and your own branded retail products.
  • Selling on the curve: groundnut prices are lowest at harvest. Properly dried and stored unshelled nuts hold well and typically fetch considerably more months later — sell in tranches.
  • Group marketing matters here: aggregators buy volumes, and a group of trained farmers with consistent, well-sorted, properly dried nuts is exactly what formal buyers cannot find easily.

📖 Glossary

  • Peg — the stalk from a fertilised flower that pushes into the soil to form a pod.
  • Pegging zone — the surface soil layer where pods form; kept loose and calcium-supplied.
  • Pops — empty shells caused by calcium deficiency.
  • Rosette — aphid-spread virus disease causing stunting and yellow distorted leaves.
  • Bunch vs runner — erect short-season types vs spreading longer-season types.
  • Mandela cock — the pods-inward drying stack.
  • Aflatoxin — invisible toxin from Aspergillus moulds; prevented, never removed.
  • Dormancy — the delay before a kernel will sprout; low in Spanish types.
  • Shelling — removing kernels from pods.
  • Rhizobium — the bacteria that fix nitrogen in legume nodules.
