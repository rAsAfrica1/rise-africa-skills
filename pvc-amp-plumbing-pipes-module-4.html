1. When to Use Threaded Fittings vs. Solvent-Weld

In PVC plumbing, you have two main ways to join pipes: solvent-weld (glue) and threaded fittings. Solvent-weld is permanent: you apply PVC primer and cement, push the pipe into the fitting, and it fuses into one piece. Threaded fittings screw together and can be taken apart later. So when do you use each?

Use solvent-weld for most of your piping runs, especially underground or inside walls. It is cheaper, faster, and less likely to leak because there are no threads to seal. For example, a 20mm PVC pipe carrying water from a tank to a tap should be solvent-welded. You need primer, cement, and a clean rag. Cut the pipe square, deburr it, apply primer then cement, and twist a quarter turn as you push. Hold for 30 seconds. That joint will last decades.

Use threaded fittings when you need to connect to metal pipes, pumps, filters, or valves that have threaded ports. Also use them where you might need to disassemble for cleaning or repair, like a drain line cleanout. For instance, connecting a PVC pipe to a galvanized steel pump outlet requires a threaded adapter. In Africa, many water systems mix PVC and galvanized pipes, so threaded transitions are common.

Never use threaded PVC fittings underground without sealing them well, because soil movement can loosen them. Also, threaded PVC is weaker than solvent-weld because the threads cut into the pipe wall. So use threaded fittings only at transition points and keep solvent-weld for the long runs. Remember: solvent-weld for permanent, threaded for removable or metal connections.

2. Using Teflon Tape or Pipe Dope on Threads

When you screw a threaded PVC fitting into another threaded fitting, water will leak unless you seal the threads. The two common sealants are Teflon tape (PTFE tape) and pipe dope (thread sealant paste). Both work, but you must apply them correctly.

Teflon tape is cheap and clean. Buy a roll of white tape (for water lines) about 12mm wide. Wrap it around the male threads only, in the direction the fitting screws on (clockwise when looking at the end). Start one thread back from the end so tape doesn't hang over and get into the waterway. Wrap 2 to 3 layers, pulling tight so it conforms to the threads. If you wrap counter-clockwise, the tape will bunch up when you screw the fitting on. After wrapping, screw the fitting hand-tight, then tighten with a wrench about one full turn. Do not overtightenâPVC threads can crack.

Pipe dope is a paste that you brush onto the male threads. It fills gaps better than tape and is preferred for larger fittings (25mm and above) or where threads are rough. Use a small brush or your finger (wear gloves). Apply a thin, even coat, again leaving the first thread bare. Pipe dope comes in small tins; in many African markets you can find brands like RectorSeal or local equivalents. It doesn't dry out like tape can, and it lubricates the threads so you can tighten more easily.

Never use both tape and dope togetherâit can cause the joint to crack. For plastic-to-plastic threaded joints, tape is usually enough. For plastic-to-metal, pipe dope is better because metal threads are sharper. After assembly, test with water pressure. If you see a drip, tighten another quarter turn. If it still leaks, disassemble, clean the threads, and reapply sealant. Always leak-test before burying pipes.

3. Installing Compression Fittings on PVC Pipes

Compression fittings are common in Africa for connecting PVC pipes to smaller tubing or to metal pipes. They consist of a nut, a brass or plastic ferrule (olive), and a body. When you tighten the nut, the ferrule compresses onto the pipe to make a seal. These fittings are reusable and don't require glue or tape.

To install a compression fitting on PVC, first cut the pipe square with a hacksaw or pipe cutter. Remove burrs with a knife or sandpaperârough edges can cut the ferrule and cause leaks. Slide the nut onto the pipe, then the ferrule. Push the pipe into the fitting body until it bottoms out. Hand-tighten the nut, then use two wrenches: one to hold the body, one to turn the nut. Tighten about 1 to 1.5 turns past hand-tight. Do not overtightenâyou can crush the PVC pipe or split the nut. For 15mm and 22mm fittings, that's usually enough.

Compression fittings work best on rigid PVC (uPVC) or CPVC. They are not recommended for flexible PVC or thin-wall pipes because the ferrule may not grip. If you're using them outdoors, protect from UV with paint or tape, as plastic nuts can become brittle. In many African hardware stores, you'll find compression fittings in sizes like 15mm, 22mm, and 28mm. They are more expensive than solvent-weld but great for quick repairs or connecting to copper or galvanized pipes.

After assembly, turn on the water and check for leaks. If it drips, tighten the nut another quarter turn. If it still leaks, disassemble and check that the ferrule is not cracked and the pipe is not scored. Sometimes wrapping 2-3 turns of Teflon tape on the pipe before sliding the ferrule helps. Remember: compression fittings are mechanical, so they can vibrate loose. Check them periodically, especially on pumps.

4. Connecting PVC to Metal Pipes Without Leaks

Connecting PVC to metal pipes is a common job in African plumbing, especially when transitioning from galvanized steel to PVC. The challenge is that PVC and metal expand at different rates, and threads can leak if not sealed properly. The best method is to use a threaded transition fitting: a PVC male adapter or a metal female adapter.

First, identify the metal pipe thread type. Most galvanized pipes use BSP (British Standard Pipe) threads, which are common in Africa. PVC threaded fittings also come in BSP sizes. So a 20mm PVC male adapter will screw into a 20mm galvanized female socket. Clean both threads with a wire brush to remove rust or dirt. Apply 3-4 wraps of Teflon tape to the metal male threads (or pipe dope if you prefer). Screw the PVC fitting into the metal by hand, then tighten with a wrenchâbut be gentle. PVC threads are softer than metal; overtightening will crack the PVC.

If you are connecting PVC to copper, you can use a compression fitting or a dielectric union to prevent galvanic corrosion. Galvanic corrosion happens when two different metals touch in water; it eats away the metal. A dielectric union has a plastic insulator. For PVC-to-copper, use a brass compression adapter with a plastic ferrule. Tighten snugly, not brutally.

Another method is to use a rubber coupling with hose clamps (often called a Fernco coupling). Slide the rubber sleeve over both pipe ends, then tighten the stainless steel clamps. This works for underground repairs and is forgiving of slight misalignment. However, it's not for high pressure. For pressurized lines, always use threaded or compression fittings. After assembly, pressurize the system and check for leaks with a dry rag. If you see moisture, tighten a quarter turn. Never use solvent-weld directly on metalâit won't bond. Always use a threaded or compression transition.

5. Common Mistakes with Threaded Joints and How to Avoid Them

Threaded joints can be tricky, and many beginners make mistakes that lead to leaks or broken fittings. Here are the most common errors and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Overtightening. PVC threads are soft. If you crank too hard, the fitting cracks or the threads strip. Always hand-tighten first, then use a wrench for only 1 to 1.5 turns. If you feel sudden resistance, stop. Use two wrenches: one to hold the pipe, one to turn the fitting. Avoid pipe wrenches with sharp teeth; use adjustable wrenches or strap wrenches.

Mistake 2: Not using sealant. Some people think threaded PVC will seal by itself. It won't. Always use Teflon tape or pipe dope. Wrap tape clockwise, 2-3 layers. For pipe dope, apply a thin coat. Never use both.

Mistake 3: Cross-threading. If you start the fitting crooked, the threads will jam. Always start by hand, turning counter-clockwise until you feel the threads click, then turn clockwise. If it feels tight immediately, back off and try again.

Mistake 4: Using damaged threads. Dirt, rust, or burrs will cause leaks. Clean threads with a wire brush and inspect for nicks. If threads are badly damaged, replace the fitting.

Mistake 5: Mixing thread types. BSP and NPT threads look similar but are not compatible. BSP is common in Africa; NPT is American. They will leak if forced together. Check the label or ask your supplier.

Mistake 6: Not supporting the pipe. Threaded joints are weak points. If the pipe hangs, the joint can crack. Use pipe clamps every 1 meter to support the pipe.

Mistake 7: Forgetting to leak-test. Always test with water pressure before burying or closing walls. If you see a drip, tighten a quarter turn. If it persists, disassemble, clean, and reapply sealant. By avoiding these mistakes, your threaded joints will last for years.

6. Practice: Making a Transition from PVC to Galvanized Pipe

In this practice lesson, you will make a transition from a PVC pipe to a galvanized steel pipe. This is a common job in African water systems, where you might connect a PVC line from a tank to a galvanized pump or tap. You will need: a PVC pipe (20mm), a galvanized pipe (20mm), a PVC male threaded adapter (20mm), Teflon tape, a hacksaw, a deburring tool or knife, two adjustable wrenches, and a wire brush.

Step 1: Prepare the PVC pipe. Cut the PVC pipe square with a hacksaw. Deburr the inside and outside edges with a knife or sandpaper. Clean the end with a rag. Apply PVC primer and cement to the outside of the pipe and the inside of the PVC male adapter. Push the adapter onto the pipe with a quarter-turn twist. Hold for 30 seconds. Let it cure for at least 15 minutes (or as directed on the cement can).

Step 2: Prepare the galvanized pipe. Clean the male threads on the galvanized pipe with a wire brush. Remove any rust or dirt. Wrap Teflon tape clockwise around the threads, starting one thread back from the end. Use 3-4 wraps, pulling tight.

Step 3: Assemble the joint. Screw the PVC male adapter into the galvanized female fitting by hand until snug. Then use one wrench to hold the galvanized pipe and another to turn the PVC adapter. Tighten about 1 to 1.5 turns past hand-tight. Do not overtightenâPVC can crack. If you feel resistance, stop.

Step 4: Leak-test. Turn on the water slowly. Check for leaks at the joint. If you see a drip, tighten the PVC adapter another quarter turn. If it still leaks, disassemble, clean the threads, and reapply Teflon tape. For extra safety, you can apply pipe dope on top of the tape (but not bothâchoose one).

Step 5: Support the pipe. Install a pipe clamp within 30cm of the joint to prevent stress. This transition is now complete. With practice, you can do this in under 10 minutes. Always leak-test before burying or closing up.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Threaded and Compression Fittings for PVC

In African plumbing and irrigation work, the ability to join PVC pipe to metal systems â pumps, galvanized risers, borehole casings, tank inlets â is a daily commercial necessity. Threaded and compression fittings are the bridge between the plastic and metal worlds. Getting these joints right protects your reputation, prevents callbacks, and keeps water flowing in markets, farms, and homes from Lagos to Nairobi to Lusaka.

This practical reference consolidates the key field knowledge from the module: when to use threaded versus solvent-weld, how to seal threads, how to install compression fittings, how to transition PVC to metal without leaks, and the mistakes that cost installers money. Treat it as a job-site companion, not a substitute for manufacturer instructions.

Threaded vs. Solvent-Weld: Choosing the Right Joint

Solvent-weld (chemical fusion) is the default for PVC-to-PVC connections. It is stronger than the pipe wall itself, cheaper, and requires no thread sealant. Threaded PVC fittings should be reserved for two situations: (1) connecting to metal components that cannot be solvent-welded, and (2) joints that must be disassembled for maintenance, such as pump unions or filter housings.

In African market and farm contexts, threaded PVC is often the only practical way to connect to imported pumps, brass valves, galvanized tank nipples, and borehole riser threads. The trade-off is real: threaded PVC loses roughly 50% of the pipe's pressure rating at the joint, and Schedule 80 threaded fittings are strongly preferred over Schedule 40 for anything above 4 bar.

Sealing Threads: Teflon Tape or Pipe Dope

Threaded joints do not seal on the threads themselves â they seal in the gap between the male and female thread flanks. Teflon (PTFE) tape and pipe dope (thread sealant paste) both fill that gap. The choice depends on the fluid, temperature, and whether the joint may need to be re-opened.

Teflon tape is clean, cheap, and universal. Wrap 2â3 turns in the direction of the thread (clockwise when viewed from the pipe end) so tightening does not unwrap it. Leave the first thread bare so tape does not enter the waterway. Pipe dope is better for larger diameters, higher pressures, and metal-to-metal joints; it also lubricates the threads, reducing the risk of cracking a PVC female fitting. For PVC male threads into metal female fittings, tape plus a light dope is a common field practice, but never use dope alone on PVC-to-PVC threaded joints â it can stress the plastic.

Never use a metal pipe wrench to over-tighten a PVC male thread into a metal or PVC female fitting. PVC is brittle under hoop stress; over-torque causes hairline cracks that fail hours or days later, often at 3 a.m. when the pump cycles. Hand-tighten first, then no more than 1â2 turns with a strap wrench. Always use Schedule 80 threaded fittings on pressurized lines above 4 bar. Never mix thread sealants that are not rated for potable water on drinking-water systems. Wear eye protection when cutting or threading PVC â shards fly.

Installing Compression Fittings on PVC

Compression fittings (nut, olive/ferrule, body) are common on small-bore PVC and polyethylene lines â irrigation laterals, filter connections, and laboratory or greenhouse plumbing. On PVC, the critical rule is: always use a pipe insert (stiffener) inside the pipe end. Without the insert, the compression ring crushes the PVC wall and the joint weeps or blows off under pressure.

Procedure: cut square, deburr, slide nut on, place olive, insert stiffener, push pipe fully into body, hand-tighten nut, then Â½ to 1 turn with a wrench. Do not use tape or dope on compression fittings â the seal is mechanical, and sealant can prevent the olive from seating.

Connecting PVC to Metal Without Leaks

The reliable transition is: PVC pipe â solvent-weld â PVC male threaded adapter (Schedule 80) â tape/dope â metal female fitting. Avoid PVC female threaded adapters onto metal male threads where possible; the wedge action of a metal male thread can split a PVC female. If a PVC female must be used, use Schedule 80 and tighten by hand plus a quarter turn only.

For buried transitions, wrap the metal side with petrolatum tape or Denso tape to slow corrosion, and bed the joint in sand. In high-water-table or saline soils common along the West African coast and the Rift Valley, use dielectric unions or plastic-to-metal transition fittings rated for burial.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Cross-threading: Always start threads by hand for the first two turns before any wrench.
  • Too much tape: More than 4 wraps stresses PVC female threads. 2â3 wraps is enough.
  • Wrong tape direction: Wrap clockwise viewed from the pipe end, or the nut unwraps it.
  • No insert on compression: The single most common cause of blow-offs on PVC.
  • Mixing sealants: Tape plus anaerobic sealant on PVC can crack the fitting.
  • Over-torque: The #1 cause of hidden cracks and callbacks.
  • Buried metal unprotected: Corrosion eats the joint within 2â3 seasons.
