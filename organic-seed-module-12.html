  • How do you know when a seed crop is ready to harvest?

🎁 Reveal a strong answer

Physiological maturity is the moment the seed has stored all the food it will ever store. After that moment, the seed only dries - it does not improve. Harvest at physiological maturity or just after. The biggest mistake is harvesting too early, when the seed looks ready but has not yet stored enough food to germinate strongly.

📖 Nyasha and the Rain Week

Nyasha had done everything right. She had prepared the soil. She had composted. She had weeded on time. Her kale plants were strong, and their seed pods were fat and heavy.

Then the rains came early. Not a lot of rain. Just three days of drizzle.

The seed pods began to split. Each pod, if she squeezed it, released its seed onto the ground.

She panicked. She called her aunt, a seed farmer of fifteen years, and asked what to do.

"Harvest now," her aunt said. "Before the pods all split."

"But the seed is not fully brown yet. The stems are still green."

"The seed inside is ready. The stem being green is fine. If you wait for the stem to turn brown, you will lose half your harvest on the ground."

Nyasha harvested that afternoon. She cut the whole plants at the base, tied them in bundles, and hung them upside down in her shed to dry further.

By the end of the week, the pods had dried on the plants. The seed fell into a cloth she had tied under each bundle.

She collected 42 kilograms of kale seed. Her neighbour, who waited two more weeks for the pods to fully brown on the plants, collected only 18.

The difference was one decision made in one afternoon.

💡 The Big Idea

Harvest at physiological maturity, not at the calendar

Every seed crop has a physiological maturity point - the moment when the seed has stored all the food it will ever store. After that, the seed only dries. Before that, the seed is not ready. You need to harvest around this point, sometimes one week early, sometimes one week late, depending on rain, birds, or shattering risk. Read the plant, not the calendar.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

How to tell when seed is ready

Each crop has its own signals. For kale and brassicas: seed pods turn light brown, and a light shake releases the seed. For tomatoes: fruit turns fully red and begins to soften at the bottom. For pepper: fruit turns red, yellow, or orange (variety dependent) and the first wrinkle appears. For amaranth: the seed head turns brown and the seed falls out when you rub it in your palm. For maize: the black layer forms at the base of the kernel and the leaves dry down. For groundnut: the inside of the pod turns dark and the seed coat peels.

The universal test: open three pods or fruits and check the seed inside. If the seed is fully formed, firm, and starting to dry, you are close. If it is still soft or pale, wait. If it is hard and the pod is beginning to split, harvest immediately.

2

The shattering problem

Some crops shatter - the seed pods burst open and drop seed to the ground. Brassicas (kale, cabbage, broccoli), amaranth, and some legumes shatter easily. If you wait too long, you lose a large part of your crop.

The solution is to harvest at or just before full maturity, then dry the plants further in a controlled place. Cut whole plants, bundle them, and hang them upside down. The seed continues to mature in the bundle. This is the standard technique for brassica seed producers.

For non-shattering crops like tomato and pepper, you can leave fruit on the plant longer. But not too long - rot is the risk instead of shattering.

3

Harvest timing in African climates

In Southern Africa, harvest usually falls in the dry season (May to September). Rain is unlikely, so you can leave crops a few days longer. But cold nights can slow drying.

In East Africa, the two rainy seasons (March-May, October-December) mean harvest must be timed between rains. If you are harvesting near the rains, use the bundle-and-hang method to dry inside.

In West Africa, the dry season (November to March) is the primary harvest window. Dust and heat speed drying but can crack seed coats. Shade drying is preferred.

The universal rule: never let the seed get wet after maturity. One rain event on a ripe seed crop can ruin the whole harvest.

🛠 Hands-On - Test Three Harvest Signals

You need

  • Your seed crop
  • A notebook
  • A small knife
  1. Pick three pods or fruits from different plants.
  2. Open each one and look at the seed inside.
  3. Write down: is the seed firm or soft? Pale or full colour? Dry or moist?
  4. Mark the date in your notebook.
  5. Repeat every 2 days for 10 days.
  6. Notice the day when the seed changes from soft to firm.
  7. That day is your physiological maturity. Harvest within 3 days.
Why it works: This exercise trains your eye to see maturity. In two seasons you will know it instinctively.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Zambia brassica seed harvest

Zambian brassica seed producers harvest when pods turn light brown and begin to split. They cut whole plants, hang them in a dry shed for 10 days, and then thresh. This method recovers 95 percent of mature seed. Leaving the plants in the field loses 30 to 50 percent to shattering.

Kenya tomato seed harvest

Kenyan tomato seed producers pick fruit at full red colour, cut them open, and ferment the seed for 2 days before washing and drying. Fermentation removes the gel coating that prevents germination. Skipping this step drops germination rates by 40 percent.
