1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Mastitis is the most costly disease for small-scale dairy farmers in Africa. It reduces milk yield, lowers milk quality, and can kill cows if not treated. A cow with mastitis may drop from 15 litres per day to 5 litres, and the milk may be unfit for sale. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, studies show that up to 40% of dairy cows have subclinical mastitis at any time. That means you lose money without even seeing sick cows. Mastitis is an infection of the udder, usually caused by bacteria entering through the teat canal. It happens when hygiene is poor, when milking is rough, or when cows lie in dirty, wet bedding. The disease comes in two forms: clinical and subclinical. Clinical mastitis shows clear signs: hot, swollen udder, clumpy or bloody milk, and a sick cow. Subclinical mastitis has no visible signs in the udder or milk, but a lab test (like the California Mastitis Test) will show it. Subclinical cases cause the biggest hidden lossesup to 70% of total mastitis cost. Other health issues in dairy herds include foot rot, milk fever, and retained placenta. Foot rot causes lameness, so cows eat less and produce less milk. Milk fever (hypocalcemia) usually hits high-yielding cows after calving; the cow goes down and cannot rise. Retained placenta (more than 12 hours after calving) can lead to metritis and infertility. All these issues reduce milk sales. By controlling them, you protect your income. Simple steps like clean bedding, post-milking teat dipping, and regular hoof trimming can prevent most problems. This module will give you practical tools to keep your herd healthy and your milk flowing.

2. Tools and Materials

To manage mastitis and other health issues, you need a few basic tools and materials. Most are cheap and available at local agrovet shops or markets. First, for milking hygiene: a clean stainless steel or plastic milking bucket with a lid, a strip cup (a cup with a black mesh screen to detect clumps in milk), and single-use paper towels or clean cotton cloths for drying teats. Never use the same cloth on multiple cows without disinfecting. Second, for teat dipping: a teat dip cup (a small plastic cup that fits over the teat) and a disinfectant solution. Common dips include 0.5% iodine or 4% chlorhexidine. You can also make a dip using 1 part household bleach to 10 parts water, but change it daily. Third, for treating clinical mastitis: antibiotic intramammary tubes (like those containing penicillin or cloxacillin) prescribed by a vet, and a clean syringe for injecting. Always follow the withdrawal period on the labelusually 3 to 5 daysbefore selling milk. Fourth, for foot problems: a hoof knife, hoof trimmers, and a foot bath (a shallow trough) filled with 5% copper sulfate solution or 10% zinc sulfate. Fifth, for milk fever: calcium borogluconate 23% injection (400 ml under the skin or into the vein) and a clean 14-gauge needle. Sixth, for general health: a thermometer (digital is best), a stethoscope, and a notebook to record daily milk yield, cow temperature, and any treatments. Seventh, for subclinical mastitis detection: a California Mastitis Test (CMT) kit, which includes a paddle with four cups and a reagent. You can buy a kit for about $1020. Alternatively, use the strip cup daily. Finally, keep a first-aid box with gloves, antiseptic, and bandages. Store all medicines in a cool, dark place, away from children. Label everything clearly. These tools cost less than the loss from one sick cow, so invest in them early.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to prevent, detect, and treat mastitis and other common dairy health issues. Step 1: Daily observation. Every morning and evening, watch each cow for signs of sickness: off-feed, slow walking, hot udder, or abnormal milk. Use a strip cup to check for clumps, flakes, or blood. Step 2: Pre-milking hygiene. Wash your hands. Use a clean, dry cloth or paper towel to wipe each teat. Discard the first three squirts of milk from each quarter into the strip cup. If you see clumps, that quarter has mastitis. Step 3: Milking order. Milk healthy cows first, then cows with subclinical mastitis (if known), and clinical mastitis cows last. This prevents spreading bacteria. Step 4: Post-milking teat dip. Immediately after milking, dip each teat in disinfectant solution. Do not wipe off. This kills bacteria before they enter the teat canal. Step 5: Treat clinical mastitis. For a hot, swollen quarter with clumpy milk, milk out the quarter completely every 24 hours. Insert one intramammary antibiotic tube into the teat canal after milking, as directed by your vet. Massage the udder. Record the cow's temperature. If the cow has a fever (above 39.5C), call a vet for injectable antibiotics. Step 6: Manage foot rot. For a lame cow, clean the hoof with water and a brush. Use a hoof knife to trim away dead sole and expose the infection. Apply a bandage with copper sulfate paste. Put the cow in a dry, clean pen. If severe, give injectable antibiotics (e.g., oxytetracycline) for 35 days. Step 7: Treat milk fever. If a cow goes down after calving and cannot rise, give 400 ml calcium borogluconate under the skin. Massage the area. If no improvement in 2 hours, call a vet for intravenous calcium. Step 8: Handle retained placenta. If the placenta is not out 12 hours after calving, do not pull it. Give the cow 50 ml of oxytocin injection and 500 ml of calcium. If still retained after 24 hours, call a vet. Step 9: Record everything. Write down date, cow ID, symptoms, treatment, and milk withdrawal period. Step 10: Review monthly. Count how many cases you had. If more than 2 cases per 10 cows per month, improve bedding and milking routine.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers lose money because of simple mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not using a strip cup. You cannot see clumps in a bucket of milk. Fix: Use a strip cup every milking. It costs $2 and catches mastitis early. Mistake 2: Using a dirty cloth to dry teats. A wet, dirty cloth spreads bacteria from cow to cow. Fix: Use single-use paper towels or a clean, dry cloth per cow. Wash cloths in hot water and disinfectant after each use. Mistake 3: Skipping post-milking teat dip. The teat canal stays open for 30 minutes after milking. Bacteria enter easily. Fix: Dip every teat immediately after milking. Use fresh dip solution daily. Mistake 4: Treating only the visible quarter. Mastitis often affects multiple quarters. Fix: Treat all quarters with intramammary tubes if the cow has clinical signs. Mistake 5: Selling milk during antibiotic withdrawal. This leaves antibiotic residues in milk, which is illegal and harmful. Fix: Read the label. Withdrawal is usually 35 days. Discard milk from treated cows. Mistake 6: Ignoring subclinical mastitis. It causes 70% of losses. Fix: Test with CMT every month. Cows with a CMT score of 2 or 3 need treatment. Mistake 7: Rough milking. Squeezing too hard or pulling teats damages tissue. Fix: Use full-hand milking, not thumb-and-finger. Keep a steady rhythm. Mistake 8: Dirty bedding. Wet, manure-filled bedding breeds bacteria. Fix: Add fresh dry bedding daily. Use sawdust, straw, or sand. Clean out wet spots. Mistake 9: Not trimming hooves. Overgrown hooves cause foot rot and lameness. Fix: Trim hooves twice a year. Use a sharp hoof knife. Soak feet in copper sulfate solution every 2 weeks. Mistake 10: Delaying treatment for milk fever. A down cow can die in 24 hours. Fix: Keep calcium borogluconate on hand. Treat immediately. Mistake 11: No records. You forget which cow was treated and when. Fix: Keep a notebook. Write date, cow ID, drug, and withdrawal date. Mistake 12: Using the same needle for all injections. This spreads diseases like anaplasmosis. Fix: Use a new needle for each cow, or sterilize between cows. Fix these mistakes and your herd will be healthier and your milk sales higher.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is important for you, your workers, and your cows. When handling medicines, always wear gloves. Some antibiotics can cause allergic reactions. Never eat, drink, or smoke while treating animals. Wash your hands with soap after handling drugs. Store all medicines in a locked box, away from children and food. Follow the dosage on the label. Giving too little antibiotic can lead to resistant bacteria. Giving too much can harm the cow. Use a new needle for each injection. For intramammary tubes, clean the teat end with an alcohol swab before inserting the tube. Do not use the same tube for more than one cow. For teat dipping, use a fresh solution every day. Iodine dips can stain, so wear old clothes. Keep the dip cup clean. When treating foot rot, use a hoof knife carefullycut away from your body. Wear steel-toed boots when working with cows. For milk fever, calcium borogluconate must be given slowly if injected into the vein. If you are not trained, give it under the skin instead. Watch for signs of shock: rapid breathing, muscle tremors. If they occur, stop and call a vet. Best practice for herd health includes: 1) Vaccinate against common diseases like anthrax and blackleg. 2) Deworm every 34 months. 3) Keep a closed herdavoid bringing in new cows without testing for mastitis and other diseases. 4) Quarantine new animals for 30 days. 5) Provide clean water and balanced feed. 6) Maintain a clean, dry resting area. 7) Milk at the same times every day. 8) Cull cows with chronic mastitis that do not respond to treatment. 9) Train your workers on hygiene and handling. 10) Work with a vet for regular check-ups. Remember: prevention is cheaper than treatment. A healthy cow gives more milk and more profit. Also, respect animal welfarehandle cows gently. Stress reduces milk let-down and increases disease. Finally, keep a first-aid kit for humans as well: bandages, antiseptic, and gloves. If you get injured, clean the wound and seek medical help. Safety and best practice protect your investment and your family.

6. Practical Exercise

This exercise will help you apply what you learned. Do it over one week with your herd. You will need: a strip cup, a CMT kit (or strip cup only), teat dip, a notebook, and a pen. Day 1: Gather your tools. Wash the strip cup and CMT paddle with hot water. Prepare fresh teat dip solution. Walk through your herd and record each cow's ID, milk yield, and any visible udder swelling. Day 2: During morning milking, use the strip cup on every cow. For each quarter, discard three squirts into the strip cup. Look for clumps, flakes, or watery milk. Record any abnormal quarter. Also check for hot or swollen quarters by feeling. Day 3: If you have a CMT kit, test each quarter. Squirt a small amount of milk into each cup of the paddle. Add an equal amount of CMT reagent. Swirl for 10 seconds. Read the score: 0 (no reaction), 1 (slight slime), 2 (gel forms), 3 (thick gel). Record scores. If no kit, use the strip cup results. Day 4: For any cow with clinical mastitis (clumpy milk, hot udder), treat as follows: milk out the quarter completely, insert an intramammary antibiotic tube, and massage. Record the cow's ID, date, and drug. Mark the withdrawal date on your calendar. For cows with CMT score 2 or 3 but no visible signs, consult your vet for treatment. Day 5: Check all cows for lameness. Lift each hoof and look for swelling, redness, or foul smell. For any lame cow, clean the hoof, trim dead tissue, and apply a copper sulfate bandage. Record. Day 6: Review your records. Count how many cows had mastitis or lameness. Calculate the percentage: (number of cases / total cows) x 100. If above 10%, identify the main cause (dirty bedding, rough milking, etc.). Day 7: Make a plan. Write three actions to improve: e.g., add fresh bedding daily, use teat dip after every milking, trim hooves next month. Share your plan with your workers. Repeat this exercise every month. Over time, you will see fewer cases and higher milk sales. Submit your records to your trainer for feedback. This practical work is the core of herd health management.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Mastitis Control in the African Dairy Herd

This practical reference is designed for the working dairy farmer, extension officer, or agribusiness manager who needs to make fast, informed decisions about udder health. In sub-Saharan Africa, mastitis remains one of the most costly production diseases in smallholder and medium-scale dairy systems. A single clinical case can reduce daily milk yield by 1030% for the affected quarter, and subclinical infections can silently cut herd productivity by 515% without any visible signs. For a business selling milk daily, that loss is a direct hit to cash flow.

Mastitis is an inflammation of the mammary gland, usually caused by bacterial infection. The economic impact goes beyond lost milk: discarded milk during treatment withdrawal, veterinary costs, culling of chronic cases, and reduced cow longevity all erode profitability. In African contexts where cooling chains are weak and milk is often sold raw or informally, the risk of zoonotic and spoilage bacteria entering the food chain is also significant. This reference brings together practical tools, data, and checklists to help you protect both herd health and your daily milk income.

Why This Matters for Your Daily Milk Business

Mastitis is not just a veterinary problem; it is a business problem. In East Africa, studies estimate that mastitis costs the dairy sector between USD 30 and 90 per cow per year in lost production and treatment. For a farmer with 10 lactating cows, that is USD 300900 annually often more than the profit margin on those animals. The disease also reduces milk quality, which matters increasingly as processors and cooperatives enforce somatic cell count (SCC) thresholds and reject high-SCC milk.

In African informal markets, where milk is often sold raw and unrefrigerated, mastitis-causing bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus agalactiae can cause human illness. E. coli and other environmental pathogens can lead to rapid spoilage. Controlling mastitis therefore protects both your income and your customers' health.

SAFETY WARNING: Antibiotic Residues and Milk Withdrawal

Never sell or consume milk from a cow treated with antibiotics until the full withdrawal period stated on the product label has passed. In many African markets, milk is sold raw and informally, and antibiotic residues can cause allergic reactions, disrupt gut flora, and drive antimicrobial resistance. Always record treatment dates, drug name, dose, and withdrawal period. If in doubt, discard the milk. Residue testing at cooperative or processor level can lead to rejection of entire batches and loss of your market access. Use only veterinary-approved drugs and follow dosage instructions exactly.

Step-by-Step Practical Method: Daily Udder Health Routine

1. Pre-milking check: Before attaching the cluster or hand-milking, inspect each quarter. Look for swelling, heat, redness, or pain. Strip the first two to three streams of milk from each teat onto a strip cup or dark surface. Any clots, flakes, or watery milk indicate clinical mastitis. Isolate the cow and milk her last to avoid spreading bacteria.

2. Clean and dry teats: Wash hands and use a clean, single-use cloth or paper towel per cow to clean teats. In African smallholder settings, a clean bucket of chlorinated water (0.5% chlorine) and individual drying towels reduce environmental contamination. Avoid using the same cloth across multiple cows this is a major route of S. aureus transmission.

3. Foremilk and CMT: For herds with a history of subclinical mastitis, run a CMT on each quarter at least once monthly. Mix equal parts milk and CMT reagent, swirl, and read the gel formation. A score of 2 or 3 indicates high SCC and likely infection. Record results per cow and quarter.

4. Milking hygiene: Use gloves if available. For hand-milking, use a full-hand technique and avoid pulling on teats. For machine milking, ensure correct vacuum and pulsation settings, and avoid over-milking. Over-milking causes teat-end damage and opens the canal to bacteria.

5. Post-milking teat dip: Immediately after milking, dip each teat in an approved post-milking disinfectant (e.g., 0.5% iodine or 0.25% chlorhexidine). This is the single most effective routine practice to prevent new infections. In dry seasons when teats crack, use a teat salve to maintain skin integrity.

6. Treat clinical cases promptly: Consult a veterinarian for antibiotic selection. Intramammary tubes are common, but systemic treatment may be needed for severe cases. Record all treatments and respect withdrawal periods. For chronic or repeated cases, cull the cow to protect the herd.

7. Monitor and record: Keep a simple herd health log: cow ID, date, quarter affected, CMT score, treatment, and withdrawal end date. This data helps you identify problem cows, evaluate treatment success, and make culling decisions. It also supports milk quality audits if you sell to a processor.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Milking a mastitis cow with the same cloth or hands as healthy cows. Fix: Use separate equipment and milk mastitis cows last. Disinfect hands and equipment between cows.

Mistake 2: Ignoring subclinical mastitis because the milk looks normal. Fix: Implement monthly CMT screening. Subclinical cases cause the greatest hidden losses.

Mistake 3: Using antibiotics without veterinary advice or stopping treatment early. Fix: Follow prescribed dosage and duration. Incomplete treatment leads to chronic infection and resistance.

Mistake 4: Selling milk during withdrawal period. Fix: Keep a written record of withdrawal dates. Discard milk safely never feed it to calves, as residues can affect them too.

Mistake 5: Poor milking machine maintenance. Fix: Check vacuum levels, replace liners every 2,500 milkings or every 6 months, and service the machine annually.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check every quarter before milking look, feel, and strip milk onto a strip cup.
  • Use clean, individual towels and disinfected hands for each cow.
  • Run CMT monthly on all lactating cows to catch subclinical cases.
  • Dip teats immediately after milking with approved disinfectant.
  • Milk mastitis cows last and use separate equipment.
  • Record all treatments, dates, and withdrawal periods never sell milk before withdrawal ends.
  • Maintain milking machines and replace liners on schedule.
  • Cull chronic repeat offenders to protect herd productivity and cash flow.
