Lessons

1. Role of builders in detergents

Builders are the workhorses of a detergent formula. They soften water by tying up calcium and magnesium ions that would otherwise stop soap and surfactants from cleaning. In many African cities, water hardness can be 200-500 mg/L as CaCO3. Without builders, you waste surfactant and get poor cleaning. Builders also provide alkalinity to break down acidic soils like grease and oils, and they help keep dirt suspended so it rinses away.

Common builders include sodium carbonate (washing soda), sodium silicate, zeolites, and citrates. In a typical powder detergent for hand washing, you might use 20-30% sodium carbonate and 5-10% sodium silicate. For machine washing, zeolites or polycarboxylates are better because they don't leave deposits. Builders also affect pH. Most laundry detergents work best at pH 9-10.5. You can test pH with cheap pH strips from a pharmacy or agricultural shop.

In Africa, builders are often the cheapest way to boost cleaning power. For example, in Nigeria, a common formulation uses 25% sodium carbonate, 10% sodium silicate, and 5% zeolite. This mix costs less than adding more surfactant. However, too much builder can damage fabrics or cause skin irritation. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling powders.

To test builder effectiveness, mix a small batch with and without builder, wash soiled cloth swatches (e.g., with palm oil or clay), and compare. You can also measure water hardness with a simple titration kit. If you use borehole water, builders are essential. For soft water (rainwater), you can reduce builder amount by half. Remember, builders are not a replacement for surfactants; they work together. A good rule: if your detergent doesn't lather well in local water, increase builder or add a chelating agent like citrate.

2. Inorganic builders: zeolites, carbonates, silicates

Inorganic builders are minerals that soften water and provide alkalinity. The three most common are zeolites, sodium carbonate, and sodium silicate. Zeolites are hydrated aluminosilicates, like Zeolite A (Na12Al12Si12O4827H2O). They exchange sodium ions for calcium and magnesium, softening water without creating scum. In Africa, zeolites are imported but affordable. Use 15-25% in powder detergents. They work best at 60C but also function at lower temps. Zeolites are insoluble, so they need a dispersant like polycarboxylate to prevent deposits on clothes.

Sodium carbonate (washing soda, Na2CO3) is the cheapest builder. It precipitates calcium as CaCO3, but that precipitate can redeposit on fabric. Use 20-40% in hand-wash powders. It also raises pH to 10-11, which helps remove grease. In East Africa, soda ash is mined in Kenya (Lake Magadi) and is very cheap. Sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) is a corrosion inhibitor and builder. It forms a protective layer on metal parts of washing machines and helps keep dirt suspended. Use 5-10% in powders. It also adds alkalinity and can be bought as a liquid (water glass) or powder.

When mixing, add silicates last because they can gel. Wear a dust masksilicate dust is harmful. For a simple hand-wash powder: 30% sodium carbonate, 10% sodium silicate, 15% zeolite, 15% surfactant (e.g., LABSA), 20% sodium sulfate, and 10% other additives. Test by washing oily cloths in hard water. If white spots remain, reduce carbonate and increase zeolite. If cloth feels stiff, reduce silicate. In rural areas, you can make a builder mix of 70% soda ash and 30% silicate for boosting soap. Store in airtight bagscarbonates absorb moisture.

3. Organic builders: citrates, polycarboxylates

Organic builders are biodegradable and work well in hard water without leaving precipitates. The two main types are citrates and polycarboxylates. Sodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7) is made from citric acid, which can be fermented from cassava or maize starch. It chelates calcium and magnesium, softening water. Use 5-15% in liquid or powder detergents. Citrate is especially good for eco-friendly products. In Africa, you can buy sodium citrate or make it by reacting citric acid with soda ash. It works best at pH 8-9, so combine with a little carbonate.

Polycarboxylates are synthetic polymers like polyacrylic acid (PAA) or acrylic-maleic copolymers. They are excellent at dispersing zeolites and preventing soil redeposition. Use 2-5% in powders with zeolites. They are not fully biodegradable but are used at low levels. In liquid detergents, use 1-3% sodium polyacrylate. These builders are more expensive than carbonates, so use them where needed: for machine washing, for anti-redeposition, or for hard water above 300 mg/L.

A practical African formulation for a liquid detergent: 10% LABSA, 5% sodium citrate, 2% polycarboxylate, 3% sodium carbonate, 2% sodium silicate, and water to 100%. Adjust pH to 9 with citric acid. For a powder: 20% zeolite, 5% citrate, 3% polycarboxylate, 25% carbonate, 10% silicate. Test by washing in hard water and checking for greyness. If clothes grey, increase polycarboxylate. If water doesn't soften, increase citrate. Citrates are also food-safe, so they are good for cleaning food surfaces. Store polycarboxylates in dry placethey can absorb moisture.

4. Bleaching agents and activators

Bleaching agents remove stains and whiten fabrics by oxidation. The most common is sodium percarbonate (2Na2CO33H2O2), a solid form of hydrogen peroxide. It releases oxygen in water, especially above 60C. In Africa, many washes are done at lower temperatures, so you need activators. Tetraacetylethylenediamine (TAED) is the main activator. It reacts with percarbonate to form peracetic acid, which works at 40C. Use 1-3% TAED with 10-20% percarbonate in powder detergents.

Another bleach is sodium hypochlorite (liquid bleach), but it damages fabrics and is not compatible with enzymes or fragrances. Use it only for white cotton and disinfecting. For color-safe bleaching, use percarbonate with TAED. You can buy percarbonate from chemical suppliers; it's often sold as 'oxygen bleach'. In Kenya or Nigeria, check with soap-making suppliers. TAED is imported but affordable.

Formulation tip: mix percarbonate and TAED dry, and keep away from moisture. Add 0.5% of a chelating agent like EDTA to stabilize. For a bleach booster: 70% sodium percarbonate, 20% TAED, 5% sodium carbonate, 5% citrate. Use 10-20 g per wash. Test by soaking stained cloth (tea, tomato, grass) in warm water with 5 g/L booster for 30 minutes. Rinse and compare. If stains remain, increase percarbonate or temperature. For low-temp (30C), use more TAED. Note: percarbonate loses activity over timestore in sealed bags away from heat. Never mix with chlorine bleach. For hand washing, wear gloves; percarbonate can irritate skin.

5. Enzymes: proteases, amylases, lipases

Enzymes are proteins that break down specific stains. Proteases break down proteins (blood, egg, grass), amylases break down starches (rice, pasta, chocolate), and lipases break down fats (oil, butter, gravy). They work at low temperatures (30-50C) and are used at 0.5-2% in detergents. In Africa, enzymes are imported as liquid or granulated powders. Store them cool and dry; heat above 60C destroys them.

For a powder detergent, use granulated enzymes coated for stability. Add them last in the mixing process, after the powder has cooled. For liquid detergents, use liquid enzymes and avoid high pH (above 10) and high surfactant levels that can denature them. A typical liquid enzyme mix: 0.5% protease, 0.5% amylase, 0.2% lipase. You can buy these from suppliers like Novozymes or local distributors. They are expensive, so use only if your market values stain removal.

Practical test: soak stained cloths in a solution of 2 g/L detergent with and without enzymes at 40C for 30 minutes. Rinse and compare. You'll see better removal of protein and starch stains with enzymes. For oily stains, lipase helps but needs more time. In hard water, enzymes work less well, so use a builder. Also, never mix enzymes with chlorine bleachit destroys them. For a budget enzyme booster: 50% protease, 30% amylase, 20% lipase, mix and use 1-2 g per wash. Keep in a cool, dark place. In West Africa, some entrepreneurs make enzyme-rich detergents using local papain from pawpaw, but it's less stable. Stick to commercial enzymes for consistency.

6. Optical brighteners, fragrances, and dyes

Optical brighteners (OBAs) are dyes that absorb UV light and re-emit blue light, making fabrics look whiter and brighter. They don't clean but improve appearance. Common OBAs are stilbene derivatives, like Tinopal. Use 0.1-0.5% in powder or liquid detergents. In Africa, OBAs are imported and can be expensive. For a cheap alternative, some use small amounts of blue dye (e.g., ultramarine blue) to mask yellowing, but OBAs are more effective. Add OBAs to the detergent base after surfactants, and mix well. For liquids, dissolve OBA in a small amount of water first.

Fragrances make detergent pleasant and signal cleanliness. Use 0.5-2% of a fragrance oil. In Africa, you can buy local essential oils like lemongrass, eucalyptus, or citrus, but they may be less stable. Synthetic fragrances are cheaper and stronger. Add fragrance last, at low temperature, to prevent evaporation. For powders, spray fragrance onto the powder while mixing. For liquids, add with gentle stirring. Test by smelling washed cloth after 24 hours; if too weak, increase.

Dyes are used to color the detergent itself, not the clothes. Use 0.001-0.01% of a dye like Acid Blue 9 or food coloring. Too much dye can stain fabrics. For a green detergent, use a few drops of green dye per liter. Always add dye diluted in water. In a powder, mix dye with a small amount of sodium sulfate to avoid clumps. A simple brightener/fragrance/dye premix: 10% OBA, 20% fragrance, 1% dye, 69% sodium sulfate. Use 1-2% of this premix in your detergent. Store in dark containers. Test on white cloth: wash with your detergent and compare to a control. If cloth looks blue, reduce OBA. If it smells too strong, reduce fragrance. Remember, these additives don't affect cleaning but drive customer satisfaction.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Builders and Additives in Detergent Manufacturing

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Builders and Additives module and turns them into a working tool for African detergent producers. Builders and additives typically account for 2545% of raw material cost in a powdered or liquid laundry detergent, and they determine whether your product performs in hard water, meets regulatory limits, and carries a margin you can defend. Use this page alongside your batch sheets, supplier quotations, and lab test records.

1. What Builders Do and Why They Matter Commercially

Builders perform four jobs: they soften water by binding calcium and magnesium, they supply alkalinity to saponify fats, they help disperse and suspend soil so it does not redeposit, and they buffer pH during the wash. In African markets, water hardness is the single most common cause of customer complaints about "the powder does not foam" or "clothes come out grey." A well-built formulation handles hardness up to 300500 mg/L as CaCO, which covers much of the continent's groundwater. If you sell in a hard-water city, builder level is a competitive weapon, not a cost line to cut.

2. Inorganic Builders: Zeolites, Carbonates, Silicates

Zeolite A (sodium aluminosilicate) is the workhorse of phosphate-free powders. It exchanges sodium for calcium inside its cage structure. It is insoluble, so it needs a co-builder (usually polycarboxylate) to prevent it depositing on fabric. Typical use: 1530% in powder.

Sodium carbonate (soda ash) is cheap, locally available in Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt, and South Africa, and provides alkalinity. It precipitates calcium as CaCO, which is acceptable in powders with good anti-redeposition agents but can cause incrustation on fabric if overused. Typical use: 1025%.

Sodium silicate provides alkalinity, protects washing machine metal parts from corrosion, and acts as a binder in spray-dried powder. It also helps control powder density and flow. Typical use: 28%.

3. Organic Builders: Citrates and Polycarboxylates

Sodium citrate is biodegradable, safe, and effective in liquid detergents where zeolite would settle out. It sequesters calcium but has lower capacity per gram than zeolite, so it is used at 515% in liquids. It is a strong marketing point for "eco" or "green" lines.

Polycarboxylates (e.g., sodium polyacrylate, acrylic-maleic copolymers) are co-builders. They boost zeolite performance, disperse calcium carbonate crystals, and prevent soil redeposition. Typical use: 15%. They are poorly biodegradable, so keep levels low and disclose in your MSDS.

4. Bleaching Agents and Activators

Sodium percarbonate (2NaCO3HO) releases hydrogen peroxide in warm water and is the standard oxygen bleach for colored and white fabrics. It is stable in powder if kept dry and below 40C in storage. TAED (tetraacetylethylenediamine) is the activator: it reacts with peroxide to form peracetic acid, which bleaches effectively at 40C instead of 60C. This matters commercially because most African households wash at ambient temperature; without an activator, your bleach is largely wasted. Typical levels: percarbonate 1020%, TAED 14%.

SAFETY WARNING Bleach and Enzyme Handling

Never mix sodium percarbonate or any peroxide bleach with chlorine-releasing compounds or with concentrated acids: toxic chlorine gas can be released. Store percarbonate away from heat, moisture, and organic matter (it is an oxidizer and can accelerate fire). Enzyme powders are respiratory sensitizers: wear a P2/N95 mask and eye protection when weighing proteases, amylases, or lipases, and never handle them in the same open area as bleach. In many African production sites, dust control and PPE are the weakest link fix ventilation before increasing batch size. Follow the supplier SDS and your national OSHA/health and safety authority requirements.

5. Enzymes: Proteases, Amylases, Lipases

Enzymes let you cut surfactant and builder levels while improving stain removal. Proteases break down protein stains (blood, egg, grass, milk). Amylases attack starch stains (rice, cassava, porridge, potato). Lipases hydrolyze fats and oils (cooking oil, body sebum). Typical total enzyme dose in a powder is 0.31.5% of active enzyme granulate; in liquids, 0.21.0%. Enzymes are inactivated by high pH, high temperature, and free chlorine. In Africa, the biggest enzyme risk is heat: unventilated warehouses in Lagos, Mombasa, or Khartoum can exceed 40C and destroy activity. Store in a cool, dry place and rotate stock on a first-in-first-out basis.

6. Optical Brighteners, Fragrances, and Dyes

Optical brighteners (fluorescent whitening agents, FWAs) absorb UV light and re-emit blue light, making white fabric look brighter. Typical dose: 0.050.3%. They work on cotton and cellulosics; on polyester they are less effective. Fragrances are the strongest driver of repeat purchase in African retail a customer often buys the smell. Typical dose: 0.10.5% for powder, 0.20.8% for liquid. Choose fragrance concentrates that survive spray drying or, better, dose post-drying. Dyes at 0.0010.01% give visual identity; keep them lightfast and check compatibility with bleach.

7. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Test your water first. Measure hardness (mg/L as CaCO) in your target cities before fixing builder levels; hard water needs more zeolite or citrate.
  • Balance builders with co-builders. Zeolite without polycarboxylate deposits on fabric never skip the co-builder.
  • Match bleach to wash temperature. Below 50C, use TAED or another activator, or the percarbonate is mostly wasted.
  • Protect enzymes from heat and moisture. Store below 30C, use FIFO, and verify activity on each delivery.
  • Dose fragrance post-drying. Spray-dried fragrance is largely lost; post-dose for a stronger, cheaper scent claim.
  • Run a 10-wash fabric test. Check whiteness, greying, and brightener buildup on cotton and polyester before launch.
  • Keep SDS and labels current. Polycarboxylates, enzymes, and percarbonate all require correct hazard communication.
  • Calculate cost per wash, not cost per tonne. A cheaper builder that needs a higher dose can cost more per wash.
