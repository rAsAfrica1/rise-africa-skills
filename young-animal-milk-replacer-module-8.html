1. Why Puppies and Kittens Need Milk Replacer: Biology, Risks, and When to Intervene

Puppies and kittens are altricial at birth: their eyes are closed, they cannot thermoregulate, and they depend entirely on the dam for warmth, immunity, and nutrition. Orphaned, rejected, or large-litter neonates die quickly without intervention. Understanding the biology behind milk replacer prevents the two most common African smallholder mistakes: feeding cow milk or diluted condensed milk, and overfeeding. This lesson covers the physiology, the real risks, and the decision rules for when to step in. Neonatal puppies and kittens need roughly 130-220 kcal metabolisable energy per kg body weight per day in the first week, falling to about 110-130 kcal/kg/day by week four as they grow and begin eating solids. Colostrum is critical in the first 12-24 hours because the gut absorbs intact antibodies only during this window; after that, closure of the intestine means antibodies are digested rather than absorbed. If the dam dies or rejects the litter, there is no substitute for colostrum except another lactating bitch or queen, or a commercial colostrum replacer. Cow milk is not a substitute: it has roughly 3.5 percent fat, 3.3 percent protein, and 4.8 percent lactose, while bitch milk has about 8-10 percent fat, 7-8 percent protein, and 3-4 percent lactose, and queen milk is even richer at 10-12 percent fat and 8-9 percent protein. Feeding cow milk causes diarrhoea, bloating, and failure to thrive because the protein and energy density are too low and the lactose load is too high for the neonate gut. Goat milk is closer but still not adequate without supplementation. Commercial puppy and kitten milk replacers are formulated to match species-specific needs. In Africa, availability varies: in Kenya, brands such as Royal Canin Baby Dog and Baby Cat milk, and local equivalents from suppliers in Nairobi and Nakuru, cost roughly KES 2,500-4,500 per 400 g tin. In Nigeria, imported Royal Canin and Beaphar products in Lagos and Abuja run NGN 15,000-30,000 per tin, while South African outlets sell similar products for ZAR 350-600. Denkavit, a Dutch supplier with distribution in East and West Africa, produces milk replacers for multiple species and is a reliable reference for formulation standards. FAO livestock manuals and NAP (National Academies Press) nutrient requirements for dogs and cats provide the baseline figures used by manufacturers. When should you intervene? Intervene if the dam has died, if she has mastitis or agalactia, if the litter is larger than eight puppies or six kittens and the smallest are losing weight, if a neonate fails to gain 5-10 percent of birth weight daily, or if the dam rejects a pup or kitten. Weigh neonates daily on a kitchen scale accurate to 1 g. A healthy puppy gains about 5-10 percent of birth weight per day; a kitten similar. If a 300 g puppy is not gaining 15-30 g daily, supplement. Do not wait for visible weakness. The first 48 hours determine survival. Hypothermia is the silent killer: a chilled neonate cannot digest milk, so warming must precede feeding. Normal rectal temperature for a neonate is 35-37 Celsius in the first week, rising to 37-38 Celsius by week three. If temperature is below 35 Celsius, warm gradually with a hot water bottle wrapped in cloth, a heat lamp at a safe distance, or skin-to-skin contact, then feed. Never feed a cold neonate. Dehydration is the second killer: pinch the skin at the back of the neck; if it stays tented, the neonate is dehydrated and needs oral rehydration solution before milk. A simple homemade ORS is 1 litre clean water, 2.5 g salt, 2.5 g baking soda, and 20 g glucose or sugar, given at 10-15 ml per kg body weight every 2-4 hours. Only after warming and rehydration should milk replacer be introduced. Common mistakes in African smallholder settings include using cow milk or maize porridge, feeding cold milk, feeding too fast with a large bottle, and failing to stimulate urination and defecation. Neonates cannot urinate or defecate on their own for the first two to three weeks; the dam licks the perineum to stimulate this. You must replicate this with a warm damp cotton ball or cloth, gently rubbing the genital and anal area after each feed until the neonate urinates and defecates. Skipping this causes bladder rupture and death. Finally, hygiene: mix milk replacer with clean boiled and cooled water, sterilise bottles and teats by boiling for 5 minutes, and discard unused milk after 1 hour at room temperature. These basics save lives before any advanced feeding schedule matters.

2. Choosing and Mixing Puppy and Kitten Milk Replacer Correctly

The market for milk replacers in Africa is uneven. Some products are excellent, some are repackaged cow milk powder with a puppy label, and some are expired imports sold cheaply. This lesson teaches you how to choose, read labels, and mix correctly. Start with the label. A legitimate puppy or kitten milk replacer states the species, the guaranteed analysis, the mixing ratio, and the manufacturer. Key figures to check: crude protein should be 30-40 percent for puppies and 35-45 percent for kittens on a dry matter basis; crude fat 20-30 percent for puppies and 25-35 percent for kittens; lactose should be low, ideally under 10 percent, because high lactose causes osmotic diarrhoea. Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk has roughly 33 percent protein and 25 percent fat; Baby Cat Milk has about 40 percent protein and 25 percent fat. Denkavit puppy milk replacer follows similar specifications. If a product lists wheat flour, maize starch, or sucrose as a main ingredient, reject it: neonates cannot digest starch well and sucrose causes diarrhoea. If the label says for all animals or livestock milk replacer, it is not suitable for puppies and kittens. Cost matters. In Kenya, a 400 g tin of Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk costs KES 2,500-4,500 and makes about 2.5-3 litres of reconstituted milk, giving roughly KES 850-1,500 per litre. In Nigeria, imported tins cost NGN 15,000-30,000, or NGN 5,000-10,000 per litre. In South Africa, expect ZAR 350-600 per tin, or ZAR 120-200 per litre. Local alternatives exist: some Kenyan and Ugandan suppliers sell generic puppy milk replacer at KES 1,200-2,000 per 500 g, but quality varies. If cost is prohibitive, a home-made emergency formula can be used for a few days: 1 litre whole milk, 3 egg yolks, 1 teaspoon cod liver oil, and 1 teaspoon glucose, plus a pinch of salt. This is not ideal and should not be used long-term, but it is better than cow milk alone. FAO and NAP both caution that home-made formulas risk nutritional imbalance, especially calcium and phosphorus. Mixing is where most mistakes happen. Always follow the manufacturer ratio. A typical ratio is 1 part powder to 4 parts water by volume for puppies, or 1 part powder to 3 parts water for kittens, but check the tin. Weighing is more accurate than scooping: a standard 400 g tin usually makes 2.5-3 litres. If the tin says 100 g powder per 500 ml water, use a kitchen scale. Water must be clean, boiled, and cooled to 45-50 Celsius before mixing, because hot water destroys vitamins and clumps the powder. Stir or whisk until fully dissolved, then cool to feeding temperature. Feeding temperature is critical: 35-38 Celsius. Milk above 40 Celsius burns the mouth and oesophagus; milk below 30 Celsius causes refusal, poor digestion, and hypothermia. Use a thermometer. If you do not have one, test a drop on your inner wrist: it should feel slightly warm, not hot. Never microwave milk because it heats unevenly and creates hot spots. Mix only what you need for 24 hours and refrigerate the rest at 4 Celsius. Discard any milk left at room temperature for more than 1 hour. Reheat gently in a warm water bath, not directly on a flame. Common mistakes include using too little powder to save money, which causes malnutrition and stunted growth; using too much powder, which causes diarrhoea and dehydration; mixing with hot water, which destroys nutrients; and mixing with unboiled water, which introduces E. coli and Salmonella. In many African settings, water quality is the biggest risk. Always boil and cool water. If you cannot boil, use bottled or filtered water. Another mistake is mixing a large batch for the week: reconstituted milk grows bacteria within hours. Mix daily. Finally, do not switch brands abruptly. If you must change, transition over 2-3 days by mixing the old and new formulas. Sudden changes cause diarrhoea. Store powder in a cool, dry place, sealed, away from sunlight; humidity in coastal West Africa can caking powder within weeks, so keep the tin closed and use within the expiry date. If the powder smells rancid or is discoloured, discard it.

3. Feeding Schedules, Volumes, and Techniques for Puppies and Kittens

Feeding a neonate is a skill. Too little and the animal starves; too much and it aspirates or gets diarrhoea. This lesson gives exact volumes, frequencies, and techniques. Start with body weight. Puppies and kittens should receive 10-15 percent of body weight in milk replacer per day in the first week, divided into feeds. For a 300 g puppy, that is 30-45 ml per day. For a 100 g kitten, that is 10-15 ml per day. By week two, increase to 15-20 percent of body weight; by week three, 20-25 percent; by week four, begin introducing solid food and reduce milk to 15-20 percent. These figures are consistent with Royal Canin and Denkavit feeding guides and with NAP nutrient requirements. Frequency: newborn puppies and kittens need feeding every 2-3 hours, including overnight, for the first week. That is 8-12 feeds per day. In week two, every 3-4 hours, or 6-8 feeds. In week three, every 4-5 hours, or 5-6 feeds. In week four, every 6 hours, or 4 feeds, alongside solid food. Sleep deprivation for the caregiver is real; share the schedule among family members. Volume per feed: divide the daily volume by the number of feeds. For a 300 g puppy at 40 ml per day over 10 feeds, that is 4 ml per feed. For a 100 g kitten at 12 ml per day over 10 feeds, that is 1.2 ml per feed. Use a syringe without the needle for small volumes, or a small pet nursing bottle with a soft teat. For kittens, a 1 ml or 3 ml syringe is ideal; for puppies, a 5 ml or 10 ml syringe, or a bottle. Technique: warm the milk to 35-38 Celsius. Hold the puppy or kitten upright, belly down, head slightly elevated, never on its back like a human baby, because that causes aspiration into the lungs. Place the teat or syringe tip in the mouth and let the animal suckle. Do not squirt milk forcefully; let the neonate control the rate. If milk bubbles from the nose, stop immediately, hold the animal head down, and clear the airway. Aspiration pneumonia kills quickly. Feed slowly, burp after each feed by holding the animal upright and gently patting the back, then stimulate urination and defecation with a warm damp cloth. Weigh daily before the first morning feed. If weight is stagnant or falling, increase volume by 10 percent or check for illness. If diarrhoea occurs, reduce volume slightly, dilute the formula by 10-20 percent for one day, and give oral rehydration solution between feeds. If diarrhoea persists beyond 24 hours, see a veterinarian. Common mistakes in Africa include feeding cow milk or porridge, feeding cold milk, feeding too fast, using a large bottle with a fast-flow teat, and skipping night feeds. Another mistake is feeding until the animal refuses: neonates will overeat if milk flows freely, causing bloat and diarrhoea. Measure each feed. Do not free-feed. Hygiene: wash bottles, teats, and syringes in hot soapy water, rinse, and boil for 5 minutes before each use. Prepare fresh milk for each feed or refrigerate for up to 24 hours. Never leave milk at room temperature for more than 1 hour. In hot African climates, bacteria multiply fast. If you must feed outside the home, carry powder and mix on site with boiled cooled water. Finally, weaning: at 3-4 weeks, introduce a shallow dish of milk replacer or puppy/kitten gruel. By 4-5 weeks, offer softened puppy or kitten kibble. By 6-8 weeks, puppies and kittens should be eating solid food and drinking water, with milk replacer reduced to 1-2 feeds per day. Abrupt weaning causes stress and diarrhoea; transition over 2 weeks. If the mother is present but has low milk, let the neonates nurse first, then supplement. This maintains maternal bonding and stimulates milk production.

4. Health Monitoring, Common Problems, and When to Call a Vet

Even with perfect feeding, neonates get sick. Early detection is the difference between life and death. This lesson covers monitoring, common problems, and treatment basics for African smallholders. Daily monitoring: weigh every puppy and kitten at the same time each morning before feeding. Record weights in a notebook. A healthy neonate gains 5-10 percent of birth weight daily. A 300 g puppy should gain 15-30 g per day; a 100 g kitten should gain 5-10 g per day. If weight is flat or falling for 2 days, act. Check temperature: normal is 35-37 Celsius in week one, 36-37 Celsius in week two, 37-38 Celsius by week three. Check hydration: pinch the skin at the back of the neck; it should snap back immediately. Check gums: pink and moist is normal; pale, white, or tacky means anaemia or dehydration. Check stool: normal is soft, yellow-brown, formed. Watery, green, or bloody stool is abnormal. Check urine: normal is pale yellow; dark yellow means dehydration. Check behaviour: a healthy neonate sleeps deeply, wakes to feed eagerly, and cries when hungry or cold. A neonate that is constantly crying, limp, or refusing to feed is sick. Common problems: 1) Diarrhoea. Causes include overfeeding, wrong mixing ratio, cow milk, infection, or stress. Treatment: reduce milk volume by 20-30 percent for 24 hours, give oral rehydration solution at 10-15 ml per kg every 2-4 hours, keep warm. If diarrhoea is bloody or persists beyond 24 hours, see a vet. 2) Constipation. Causes include underfeeding, dehydration, or formula too concentrated. Treatment: give 1-2 ml of warm water or oral rehydration solution, stimulate the perineum, and adjust formula. 3) Bloat. Causes include overfeeding or feeding too fast. Treatment: stop feeding for 2 hours, massage the belly gently, keep warm. If bloat is severe, see a vet. 4) Aspiration pneumonia. Causes include feeding on the back or too fast. Signs: coughing, laboured breathing, milk from the nose. Treatment: hold head down, clear airway, see a vet immediately for antibiotics. 5) Hypothermia. Causes include cold environment, wet bedding, or illness. Treatment: warm gradually with a hot water bottle wrapped in cloth, a heat lamp, or skin-to-skin contact. Do not feed until warm. 6) Hypoglycaemia. Causes include infrequent feeding or illness. Signs: weakness, tremors, coma. Treatment: rub glucose syrup or honey on the gums and see a vet. 7) Fleas and ticks. Neonates can die from anaemia caused by fleas. Treatment: use a flea comb and warm bath with mild soap, and consult a vet for safe products; many flea products are toxic to neonates. 8) Umbilical infection. Signs: red, swollen, or smelly navel. Treatment: clean with dilute iodine or chlorhexidine, see a vet. 9) Fading puppy or kitten syndrome. Signs: poor weight gain, weakness, crying, death within days. Causes are often multifactorial: poor colostrum, infection, hypothermia, or congenital defects. Treatment: supportive care, warmth, fluids, and vet attention. Vaccination and deworming: puppies and kittens should be dewormed at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks with a vet-approved product, and vaccinated starting at 6-8 weeks. In Africa, common diseases include parvovirus in puppies and panleukopenia in kittens, both preventable by vaccination. Keep neonates away from unvaccinated animals. Hygiene: wash bedding daily, disinfect surfaces, and wash hands before handling. In many African homes, neonates share space with poultry, goats, and other animals; isolate them. Cost of vet care varies: in Kenya, a vet visit costs KES 1,500-3,000 plus drugs; in Nigeria, NGN 5,000-15,000; in South Africa, ZAR 400-800. Prevention is cheaper than treatment. Keep records: birth weight, daily weight, feeding volumes, stool consistency, and any treatments. This helps you and your vet identify problems early. Finally, know your limits. If a neonate is not improving after 24 hours of correct feeding and warmth, call a vet. Do not wait until it is too late.

5. Practical Feeding Plans, Costing, and Local Adaptation for African Smallholders

This lesson brings everything together into practical plans, costs, and local adaptations for African smallholders raising puppies and kittens, whether for sale, security, or companionship. Start with a feeding plan for a litter of four puppies from birth to eight weeks. Week 1: feed 10-15 percent of body weight daily, every 2-3 hours, 10 feeds per day. For a 300 g puppy, 4 ml per feed. Mix 100 g powder per 500 ml water. Cost: one 400 g tin makes about 2.5 litres, enough for roughly 5-7 days for four puppies, costing KES 2,500-4,500 in Kenya, NGN 15,000-30,000 in Nigeria, or ZAR 350-600 in South Africa. Week 2: 15-20 percent body weight, every 3-4 hours, 8 feeds. Week 3: 20-25 percent body weight, every 4-5 hours, 6 feeds, introduce gruel. Week 4: 4-5 feeds plus softened kibble. Week 5-8: 2-3 milk feeds plus solid food, wean by week 8. For kittens, the same structure but smaller volumes and richer formula. Costing: a litter of four puppies from birth to eight weeks needs roughly 4-6 tins of milk replacer, costing KES 10,000-27,000, NGN 60,000-180,000, or ZAR 1,400-3,600. This is a significant investment. If selling puppies, price accordingly. If keeping them, consider the cost before breeding. Local adaptation: in Kenya, suppliers in Nairobi, Nakuru, and Mombasa stock Royal Canin, Beaphar, and generic brands. In Nigeria, Lagos and Abuja pet shops stock imported brands; online suppliers deliver. In South Africa, pet shops and veterinary clinics stock a wide range. In Tanzania, Uganda, and Ghana, availability is lower; order in advance or use a vet clinic. If commercial milk replacer is unavailable, use the emergency home formula for no more than 5-7 days: 1 litre whole milk, 3 egg yolks, 1 teaspoon cod liver oil, 1 teaspoon glucose, pinch of salt. Mix well, warm to 35-38 Celsius, feed as above. This formula lacks adequate calcium and phosphorus; add 1 teaspoon of finely ground eggshell powder per litre if feeding longer. FAO and NAP both warn that home formulas are temporary. Another local option is goat milk, which is closer to bitch milk than cow milk: mix 1 litre goat milk, 1 egg yolk, 1 teaspoon glucose, and 1 teaspoon cod liver oil. Goat milk is available in many African communities. However, goat milk is low in folate and vitamin E; supplement if used beyond one week. Common mistakes in Africa: using cow milk or maize porridge, feeding cold milk, overfeeding, skipping night feeds, failing to stimulate urination, using dirty water, and not weighing. Another mistake is buying cheap expired milk replacer; check expiry dates. Another is mixing too little powder to save money, causing malnutrition. Another is feeding puppies and kittens together with adult dogs and cats, which causes stress and disease. Another is not vaccinating or deworming. Another is breeding without a plan for the cost of milk replacer if the mother dies. Record keeping: keep a notebook with birth date, birth weights, daily weights, feeding volumes, costs, and health events. This helps you improve and shows buyers you are professional. Marketing: if selling puppies or kittens, highlight that they were hand-reared with quality milk replacer, vaccinated, and dewormed. This commands a higher price. In Kenya, hand-reared puppies sell for KES 15,000-50,000 depending on breed; in Nigeria, NGN 50,000-200,000; in South Africa, ZAR 2,000-8,000. The milk replacer cost is a fraction of the sale price. Finally, plan for emergencies: keep a stock of milk replacer, oral rehydration salts, a thermometer, syringes, and a hot water bottle. Know your nearest vet and their emergency number. Join a local breeders or smallholders group for advice. Teagasc (Ireland) and university extension services in Africa, such as the University of Nairobi and Ahmadu Bello University, publish practical guides on neonatal care. Use them. With correct feeding, hygiene, and monitoring, you can raise healthy puppies and kittens even without the mother, turning a potential loss into a productive outcome.
