1. Activity Sequencing and Dependencies

Before you can build a schedule, you need to know what tasks must happen and in what order. This is activity sequencing. Start by listing every activity needed to complete your project. For a maize farming project in Kenya, activities might include: clear land, buy seeds, plough, plant, apply fertiliser, weed, harvest, transport to market. Now identify dependencies which tasks must finish before another can start. For example, you cannot plant before ploughing. That is a finish-to-start dependency, the most common type.

There are four dependency types. Finish-to-start (FS): Task B starts after Task A finishes. Start-to-start (SS): Task B can start only after Task A starts. Finish-to-finish (FF): Task B cannot finish until Task A finishes. Start-to-finish (SF): Task B cannot finish until Task A starts (rare). In a road construction project in Nigeria, laying asphalt (B) can start only after grading (A) is complete FS. But you might start compacting (C) only after grading starts SS.

Dependencies also have reasons. Mandatory dependencies are required by law or physics you cannot roof a house before walls are up. Discretionary dependencies are preferred by your team you might choose to paint before installing floors to avoid damage. External dependencies involve outside parties a supplier must deliver cement before you can pour a foundation. Understanding these helps you avoid unrealistic schedules.

Use a simple tool: a precedence diagram. Draw boxes for each activity and arrows for dependencies. For a small poultry project in Ghana, you might have: buy chicks build coop buy feed vaccinate sell. But note: building coop can happen in parallel with buying chicks, as long as both finish before chicks arrive. This parallel work saves time.

Finally, document your sequencing in a table or software like Microsoft Project or free tools like ProjectLibre. For a 3-month project, spending one day on sequencing saves weeks of confusion. Always involve your team the person who does the work knows the real order. In African contexts, consider local factors: rainy seasons, market days, and availability of transport. Sequencing is not just about logic; it is about reality.

2. Estimating Activity Durations

Estimating how long each activity will take is critical. If you guess wrong, your whole schedule fails. Use three techniques: expert judgment, historical data, and three-point estimating. For a solar panel installation in Tanzania, you might ask an experienced installer how long it takes to mount 20 panels. They say 3 days. That is expert judgment. Check past projects: last time, 20 panels took 4 days because of rain. That is historical data. Three-point estimating uses optimistic (O), most likely (M), and pessimistic (P) durations. Formula: (O + 4M + P) / 6. If O=2 days, M=3 days, P=7 days, then (2 + 12 + 7)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5 days. This gives a weighted average.

Always add a buffer for uncertainty. In Africa, power cuts, fuel shortages, and sudden price hikes are common. For a catering project in South Africa, cooking for 100 people might take 4 hours normally, but add 1 hour buffer for gas outage or load shedding. Use a percentage: 10-20% for well-known tasks, 30-50% for new or risky ones. Do not pad every task that hides real risks. Instead, keep a central project buffer.

Productivity rates help. For brick laying, a mason might lay 500 bricks per day. If you need 5,000 bricks, that is 10 days. For farming, one person can weed one acre in 2 days. For 5 acres, 10 person-days. If you have 2 people, 5 days. Always specify units: person-days, machine-hours, etc. In many African projects, labour is available but tools are not. If you have one wheelbarrow for 10 workers, your duration estimate must reflect that bottleneck.

Use software like Microsoft Excel to record estimates. List each activity, its O, M, P, and calculated duration. For a 6-month project, this takes a few hours but pays off. Also, involve the people doing the work they know how long it really takes. A driver knows that a trip from Lagos to Ibadan takes 2 hours without traffic, but 4 hours on a Monday morning. Document assumptions: 'Assuming no fuel scarcity' or 'Assuming one delivery truck available.'

Finally, review estimates with your team. Ask: What could go wrong? How long did it take last time? Adjust. Remember, duration estimates are not promises; they are best guesses. Update them as you learn more. In African contexts, always account for 'African time' not as a stereotype, but as a reality of unpredictable infrastructure. Build flexibility into your schedule.

3. Critical Path Method (CPM)

The Critical Path Method (CPM) finds the longest sequence of dependent tasks that determines the shortest possible project duration. Any delay on the critical path delays the whole project. To use CPM, you need a list of activities, their durations, and dependencies. Let us do a small example: A house renovation in Uganda. Activities: A=remove roof (2 days), B=repair walls (3 days, after A), C=install new roof (4 days, after B), D=paint interior (2 days, after B), E=clean site (1 day, after C and D). Draw a network diagram.

Calculate early start (ES) and early finish (EF) by moving forward. A: ES=0, EF=2. B: ES=2, EF=5. C: ES=5, EF=9. D: ES=5, EF=7. E: ES=max(9,7)=9, EF=10. So project duration = 10 days. Now calculate late start (LS) and late finish (LF) by moving backward. E: LF=10, LS=9. C: LF=9, LS=5. D: LF=9, LS=7. B: LF=min(5,7)=5, LS=2. A: LF=2, LS=0. Float = LS - ES. For A: 0; B: 0; C: 0; D: 2; E: 0. Activities with zero float are on the critical path: A-B-C-E. That path is 2+3+4+1=10 days. D has 2 days float, so it can be delayed up to 2 days without affecting the project.

In real projects, use software like Microsoft Project, Primavera, or free tools like ProjectLibre. For a 50-activity project, manual CPM is error-prone. But understanding the concept helps you manage. For a market stall construction in Senegal, critical path might be: foundation walls roof stall fittings. You can fast-track by overlapping, but that adds risk.

CPM also helps with resource leveling. If two critical activities need the same mason, you may need to delay one, which extends the project. Always identify the critical path early. In African projects, critical paths often involve imported materials or specialised labour. For example, a solar project might have a critical path through inverter delivery from China. A delay there delays everything. Monitor critical activities daily.

Finally, remember that CPM assumes unlimited resources and deterministic durations. In reality, durations vary. But CPM gives a baseline. Update it as work progresses. Use it to communicate with stakeholders: 'If we delay the roof, the whole project slips by 4 days.' That is powerful.

4. Gantt Charts and Milestone Tracking

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that shows activities over time. It is the most common schedule visual. Each activity is a horizontal bar, with length equal to its duration. Dependencies are shown with arrows. Milestones are diamonds that mark key events like 'foundation complete' or 'first harvest'. For a small business project in Rwanda, a Gantt chart might show: market research (2 weeks), buy equipment (1 week), hire staff (2 weeks), train staff (1 week), open shop (milestone).

To create a Gantt chart, list activities on the left, dates along the top. Draw bars. Use free tools: Google Sheets, Excel, or online tools like GanttProject, TeamGantt, or Smartsheet. For a 6-month project, a simple Excel template works. Colour-code: critical path in red, non-critical in blue. Milestones in yellow. This makes it easy to see progress. In African contexts, print a large chart and put it on the wall. Update it weekly with a marker low-tech but effective.

Milestones are zero-duration points. They help track progress. For a farming project, milestones might be: land prepared (week 2), seeds planted (week 3), first weeding (week 6), harvest (week 20), sold at market (week 22). Each milestone has a deliverable. When you hit a milestone, celebrate it boosts team morale. If you miss one, investigate why and adjust.

Gantt charts also show float. If a bar has slack, you can delay it without affecting the project. Use this to manage resources. For example, if your only tractor is needed for two tasks, schedule the critical one first. In many African projects, equipment is shared. A Gantt chart helps you see conflicts. You can also show progress: shade the completed portion of each bar. This gives a quick visual of whether you are ahead or behind.

Update your Gantt chart regularly at least weekly. Compare planned vs actual. If an activity is behind, check if it is on the critical path. If yes, take action: add resources, work overtime, or compress. If not, you may have time. Share the chart with your team and stakeholders. In areas with low literacy, use pictures or colours. A Gantt chart is not just a plan; it is a communication tool. Keep it simple and visible.

5. Schedule Compression Techniques

When your project is running late or you need to finish earlier, you compress the schedule. Two main techniques: crashing and fast tracking. Crashing adds resources to critical activities to shorten their duration. For example, a construction project in Egypt needs to finish 2 weeks early. You hire more masons to work double shifts. This costs more but saves time. Crashing works only on critical activities adding resources to non-critical tasks does not shorten the project. Also, there is a limit: you cannot add infinite people to one task. Nine women cannot make a baby in one month.

Fast tracking overlaps activities that would normally be sequential. Instead of designing then building, you start building before design is fully complete. This can save time but increases risk of rework. For a road project in Ghana, you might start clearing land while final survey is being done. If the survey changes, you may have cleared the wrong area. Use fast tracking only when activities can be overlapped safely. Always assess risk.

Other techniques: adjust leads and lags. A lead means you start the next activity before the previous finishes (e.g., start painting before plastering is fully dry). A lag means you delay the start (e.g., wait 3 days for concrete to cure). You can also reduce scope do less work. For a software project in Nigeria, you might launch with core features and add others later. This is often the cheapest way to meet a deadline.

When compressing, always calculate the cost-time trade-off. Crashing costs money. Fast tracking costs risk. For a small business, maybe you can negotiate a later deadline. But if you must compress, focus on the critical path. Use a crashing table: for each critical activity, list normal duration, crash duration, and crash cost per day. Choose the cheapest activities to crash first. For example, Activity A: normal 5 days, crash 3 days, cost $100/day. Activity B: normal 4 days, crash 2 days, cost $200/day. Crash A first.

In African contexts, compression often involves informal labour. You can hire temporary workers from the community. But ensure quality. Also, consider weather: in rainy season, crashing may not work if you cannot work outdoors. Always update your schedule after compression. Communicate changes to your team. Compression is a tool, not a cure-all. Use it wisely.

6. Monitoring and Updating the Schedule

A schedule is useless if you do not monitor and update it. Monitoring means tracking actual progress against the plan. Updating means revising the schedule based on that progress. For a small retail project in Kenya, you might check daily: Did we receive stock? Did we hire staff? Use a simple tracking sheet: activity, planned start, planned finish, actual start, actual finish, percent complete. At the end of each week, update the schedule. If an activity is behind, note why. If ahead, note why.

Use earned value management (EVM) for more control. EVM compares planned value (PV), earned value (EV), and actual cost (AC). For a 10-day activity with a budget of $1,000, after 5 days you should have earned $500 (PV). If you have completed 40% of work, EV = $400. Schedule performance index (SPI) = EV/PV = 400/500 = 0.8. SPI < 1 means behind schedule. SPI > 1 means ahead. EVM gives early warning. You do not need software; a spreadsheet works.

Update the schedule by revising durations, dependencies, or resources. If a task took longer than planned, adjust remaining tasks. If a key person is sick, reassign. Always involve the team they know what is happening. In African contexts, face-to-face meetings are effective. Hold a 15-minute stand-up every morning. Ask: What did you do yesterday? What will you do today? Any blockers? This keeps everyone aligned.

When updating, watch for changes to the critical path. A non-critical activity may become critical if it delays. For example, if painting (float 2 days) takes 3 days longer, it becomes critical. Recalculate the critical path after each update. Use software to do this quickly. Also, update your Gantt chart and milestones. Communicate changes to stakeholders. If you need more time, ask early. Do not wait until the deadline.

Finally, document lessons learned. At the end of the project, review what went well and what did not. For example, 'We underestimated transport time due to bad roads.' Use this to improve future estimates. In Africa, where infrastructure can be unpredictable, monitoring is not optional it is survival. A schedule that is not updated is just a wish list. Keep it alive.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Schedule Development and Control in African Agribusiness Projects

In African agribusiness, a project schedule is not a paperwork exercise it is the operating rhythm of the business. A maize aggregation scheme in Tanzania, a cold-chain expansion in Kenya, or a cassava processing line in Nigeria all live or die by whether planting windows, input deliveries, off-taker collections, and processing runs happen in the right sequence and at the right time. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the working toolkit: how to sequence activities, estimate durations realistically, find the critical path, track progress with Gantt charts and milestones, compress schedules when the rains or the buyer will not wait, and update the schedule without losing control.

1. Activity Sequencing and Dependencies

Sequencing means deciding what must happen before what. In agribusiness, the strongest dependencies are biological and climatic, not administrative. You cannot harvest before the crop matures; you cannot dry maize before it is shelled; you cannot deliver to an off-taker before grading and moisture testing. Map four dependency types:

  • Mandatory (hard logic): Land preparation planting weeding harvest. Non-negotiable.
  • Discretionary (soft logic): Whether to train farmers before or after input distribution. A business choice.
  • External: Supplier delivery of certified seed, government phytosanitary permits, buyer inspection.
  • Internal: Your own team's handoffs field officer reports to aggregation centre, then to transport.

Use the Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM) with Finish-to-Start (FS) as the default, but recognise Finish-to-Start with lag is common in agriculture: planting must finish, then a lag of 710 days before the first weeding. Where you have float, you can overlap activities (Start-to-Start) for example, begin farmer registration while input procurement is still closing.

2. Estimating Activity Durations

Duration estimates in African agribusiness must account for variability that office-based projects ignore: rainfall onset, road conditions in the rainy season, customs clearance delays, and the reality that smallholder farmers' labour availability competes with their own food crops. Use three-point estimating:

Expected duration = (Optimistic + 4 Most Likely + Pessimistic) 6

For a 20-hectare maize block, land preparation might be: Optimistic 4 days, Most Likely 6 days, Pessimistic 12 days (tractor breakdown, fuel shortage, wet field). Expected = (4 + 24 + 12) 6 = 6.7 days. Build contingency into the schedule, not into optimism.

3. Critical Path Method (CPM)

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent activities through the network. It determines the shortest possible project duration, and any delay on it delays the whole project. In the diagram above, the critical path is B D E F = 12 + 10 + 14 + 7 = 43 days. Activity C (farmer training) has 4 days of float it can slip without delaying delivery, provided it finishes before planting in practice.

For African agribusiness, CPM is most powerful when you overlay the agricultural calendar. If the critical path ends after the rains begin, the schedule is worthless. Work backwards from the biological deadline (planting date, off-taker delivery window) and let CPM show you where the real risk sits.

4. Gantt Charts and Milestone Tracking

A Gantt chart turns the network into a calendar view that field teams, co-operatives, and financiers can read. Milestones are zero-duration checkpoints: "all inputs delivered to aggregation centres", "moisture content verified below 13.5%", "first truck dispatched to off-taker". Milestones are what you report to a bank or an impact investor they want to see evidence, not activity bars.

5. Schedule Compression Techniques

When the planting window or the buyer's delivery date is fixed, you compress. Two techniques:

  • Crashing: Add resources to critical activities. Hire a second tractor for land preparation, or run two drying floors in parallel. Crashing costs money and has diminishing returns the classic "nine women cannot make a baby in one month" limit applies to crop biology.
  • Fast-tracking: Overlap activities that were sequential. Start farmer training while input procurement is still ongoing. Risk: rework if assumptions change. In agribusiness, fast-tracking works best on administrative activities (registration, training, contracts) and worst on biological ones (you cannot fast-track germination).
SAFETY WARNING Post-Harvest and Chemical Handling

Never compress post-harvest drying, aflatoxin testing, or pesticide application activities to save time. Rushing maize drying above 13.5% moisture creates aflatoxin risk that can destroy an entire consignment and expose your business to regulatory rejection and reputational damage. Rushing pesticide application without the full pre-harvest interval (PHI) leaves chemical residues that fail export standards. Schedule compression must never override food safety, worker safety, or phytosanitary compliance. If compression threatens these, escalate to the project sponsor and renegotiate the deadline with the buyer.

6. Monitoring and Updating the Schedule

A schedule is a living document. Update it weekly during field operations and daily during harvest. Use Earned Value Management (EVM) for objective progress:

  • SV (Schedule Variance) = EV PV. Negative SV means behind schedule.
  • SPI (Schedule Performance Index) = EV PV. SPI < 1.0 means behind schedule.

In low-data environments, proxy measures work: hectares planted vs. planned, tonnes aggregated vs. target, trucks dispatched vs. scheduled. Record actual start and finish dates, update remaining durations, and re-run the critical path. If the critical path shifts for example, a delayed input delivery pushes planting the float on other activities changes, and your risk profile changes with it.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Map every activity against the agricultural calendar planting, harvest, and off-taker windows are immovable constraints.
  2. Classify dependencies as mandatory, discretionary, external, or internal before drawing the network.
  3. Use three-point estimates for any activity exposed to weather, transport, or supplier risk.
  4. Identify the critical path and track float on non-critical activities weekly.
  5. Define milestones that a financier or buyer can verify with documents, not opinions.
  6. Compress only administrative activities; never compress food safety, drying, or phytosanitary steps.
  7. Update the schedule weekly in field operations and daily during harvest and delivery.
  8. Report SPI and milestone status to the sponsor with a clear recovery plan when SPI < 1.0.
