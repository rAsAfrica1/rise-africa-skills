Lessons

1. Mounting Front Brake Caliper and Rotor

Start by gathering your tools: 5mm and 6mm Allen keys, 10mm and 12mm spanners, a torque wrench, threadlocker (blue), and a clean rag. Put the motorbike on a stable stand so the front wheel is off the ground. Remove the wheel if your model requires it; otherwise, work with it in place. First, clean the rotor with brake cleaner and a lint-free cloth to remove any oil. Slide the rotor onto the hub, aligning the bolt holes. Apply a drop of threadlocker to each rotor bolt, then tighten them in a star pattern to 1215 Nm. Don't overtightenyou can warp the rotor.

Next, mount the caliper. If the caliper bracket is separate, bolt it to the fork leg first, using the original bolts and threadlocker. Tighten to 2025 Nm. Then slide the caliper over the rotor, making sure the brake pads are seated correctly and the rotor sits centered between them. If the pads are new, you may need to push the pistons back gently with a flat screwdriver before fitting. Bolt the caliper to the bracket or fork, and tighten to 2530 Nm. Spin the wheelit should turn freely with a slight drag from the pads. If it locks or rubs hard, the caliper may not be centered; loosen the mounting bolts, wiggle the caliper, and retighten.

In many African workshops, you'll work with basic hand tools, so always use the correct size to avoid stripping bolts. If you don't have a torque wrench, tighten firmly but not with extreme forcestripped threads are a common problem. After mounting, pump the brake lever a few times to seat the pads. Check for leaks at the caliper banjo bolt. A properly mounted front brake is critical for safety, especially on rough roads. Test by spinning the wheel and applying the brake; it should stop the wheel smoothly. If you hear grinding, check pad alignment. Finally, recheck all bolts after the first ride.

2. Installing Rear Brake Caliper and Rotor

The rear brake setup is similar to the front but often has less space and may include a mechanical parking brake on some EV models. You'll need the same tools: Allen keys, spanners, torque wrench, threadlocker, and brake cleaner. Start by supporting the rear wheel off the ground. Remove the rear wheel if necessaryon many electric motorbikes, you must disconnect the motor cable first. Clean the rear rotor and hub. Mount the rotor onto the hub, applying threadlocker to the bolts. Tighten in a star pattern to 1215 Nm. If the rotor is warped, replace it; a warped rotor causes pulsating brakes.

Now, attach the caliper bracket to the swingarm or axle mount. Use new bolts if the old ones are corroded. Apply threadlocker and tighten to 2025 Nm. Slide the caliper over the rotor, ensuring the pads are correctly positioned. On some models, you need to remove the pads to fit the caliper, then reinsert them. Push the pistons back if they are extended. Bolt the caliper to the bracket and tighten to 2530 Nm. Spin the wheel; it should rotate with a slight drag. If the wheel is hard to turn, the caliper may be misaligned. Loosen the mounting bolts, center the caliper by eye, and retighten.

For EVs with regenerative braking, the rear brake may be used less, but it must still work perfectly as a backup. After installation, pump the brake pedal or lever to build pressure. Check for fluid leaks at the caliper and banjo bolt. If the brake feels spongy, you'll need to bleed the system (covered in a later lesson). In rural areas, dust and mud can quickly contaminate brake pads, so ensure the caliper is not rubbing. Test the brake by riding slowly and applying it; the bike should stop smoothly without pulling to one side. Recheck bolt tightness after your first ride, as vibration can loosen them.

3. Connecting Brake Lines and Bleeding

Brake lines carry hydraulic fluid from the master cylinder to the caliper. If you're installing new lines, use copper washers on both sides of the banjo bolt. Start by connecting the line to the master cylinder first, then to the caliper. Tighten the banjo bolts to 2025 Nmtoo loose and it leaks, too tight and you crush the washer. Use a clean syringe or a small funnel to fill the master cylinder reservoir with DOT 3 or DOT 4 brake fluid. Never mix different types. Keep the reservoir topped up during bleeding to avoid air entering.

Bleeding removes air from the system. You'll need an 8mm spanner, a clear plastic tube, and a container for old fluid. Place the tube over the bleeder nipple on the caliper and submerge the other end in a little brake fluid. Pump the brake lever several times and hold it. Open the bleeder nipple a quarter turnfluid and air bubbles will flow out. Close the nipple before releasing the lever. Repeat until no bubbles appear and the lever feels firm. Check the fluid level in the reservoir often; if it runs dry, you'll have to start over. In hot African climates, brake fluid absorbs moisture quickly, so use fresh fluid from a sealed container.

If you don't have a bleeding kit, you can use a clean bottle and tube. Work in a well-ventilated area; brake fluid damages paint and is toxic. After bleeding, top up the reservoir to the MAX line and replace the diaphragm and cap. Test the brake leverit should feel solid, not spongy. If it's still spongy, there may be air trapped; try tapping the caliper and lines to dislodge bubbles and bleed again. Check all connections for leaks. A proper bleed is essential for safe braking, especially on steep hills. Always dispose of old brake fluid responsiblynever pour it on the ground.

4. Adjusting Brake Lever Free Play

Brake lever free play is the small amount of movement before the brake starts to engage. Too much free play and the brake feels vague; too little and the brake may drag, overheating the pads. For most motorbikes, the recommended free play at the lever end is 1020mm. Check by pulling the lever lightly until you feel resistance; measure the gap between the lever and the handlebar grip. On cable-operated brakes, adjust using the knurled nut at the lever or the adjuster near the caliper. Loosen the lock nut, turn the adjuster out to reduce free play or in to increase it. Tighten the lock nut and recheck.

For hydraulic brakes, free play is not adjustable at the lever; it's set by the master cylinder. If there's excessive play, it may indicate air in the system or a worn master cylinder. Bleed the brakes first. If the lever still has too much play, the master cylinder may need rebuilding. On some EVs, the brake lever also activates a switch for the brake light or regenerative braking. Ensure the switch clicks just as the brake engages. Adjust the switch position if needed.

In African workshops, riders often prefer a slightly firmer lever for better control on rough roads. However, never set free play to zero; the brake will drag and overheat. After adjustment, spin the wheel and apply the brakeit should stop the wheel without rubbing when released. Test ride at low speed to ensure the brake engages smoothly. If the lever feels spongy, bleed again. Regularly check free play as part of your maintenance routine. A well-adjusted brake lever improves safety and reduces fatigue on long rides.

5. Testing Brake Function and Safety

After installing and adjusting your brakes, you must test them thoroughly before riding on public roads. Start with a static test: with the bike on a stand, spin each wheel and apply the brake. The wheel should stop immediately and not rub when released. Check the brake lever and pedal for firmnessif they feel spongy, bleed the system. Inspect all connections for leaks; a drop of fluid under the caliper or master cylinder means a loose banjo bolt or a damaged seal. Tighten or replace as needed.

Next, do a low-speed test in a safe area, like an empty yard or quiet street. Ride at walking pace and apply the front brake gently, then the rear. The bike should slow smoothly without pulling to one side. If it pulls, one caliper may be sticking or the rotor may be warped. Increase speed to about 20 km/h and brake harder. You should feel a progressive bite. If the brake grabs or locks suddenly, the pads may be contaminated with oil or the rotor may be glazed. Clean or replace as necessary. Test the brakes on different surfacestarmac, dirt, gravelto simulate African road conditions.

Check the brake light: it should come on when you apply the brake. For EVs, ensure the regenerative braking engages without interfering with hydraulic braking. After testing, let the brakes cool and recheck bolt tightnessvibration can loosen caliper bolts. Look for signs of overheating, like blueing on the rotor or a burning smell. If you find any issue, fix it before riding further. Remember, brakes are your most important safety system. Never compromise. Carry out these tests after any brake work, and repeat them periodically. A well-tested brake system gives you confidence on any road.

6. Parking Brake Integration (if applicable)

Some electric motorbikes, especially cargo models, come with a mechanical parking brake that locks the rear wheel when parked. This is often a lever or pedal that engages a separate caliper or a locking pin in the rear brake. If your bike has one, you'll need to integrate it with the hydraulic brake system. Start by identifying the parking brake mechanismit could be a cable-operated caliper or a mechanical lock on the main caliper. Install the parking brake caliper or bracket according to the manufacturer's instructions, using threadlocker on all bolts and tightening to specified torque, usually 2025 Nm.

If the parking brake uses a cable, route it along the frame, avoiding sharp bends and hot parts. Adjust the cable so that when engaged, the rear wheel cannot turn. There should be a small amount of free play in the lever when disengaged. Test by engaging the parking brake, then trying to push the bikeit should not move. Disengage and ensure the wheel spins freely. If the parking brake is integrated into the main caliper, it may use a mechanical screw that pushes the pads. Follow the service manual for adjustment. In many African settings, parking brakes are essential on hills; a failed parking brake can cause the bike to roll away.

After installation, test the parking brake on a slope. Engage it, put the bike in gear (if applicable), and try to roll it. It should hold firmly. Check that the brake disengages fully when released; otherwise, the pads will drag and overheat. Lubricate the cable or mechanism with a little grease. Regularly inspect the parking brake for wear. If it's not applicable to your model, skip this lesson, but always use the main brake when parking. For EVs without a parking brake, you can leave the bike in gear (if it has a transmission) or use a strap. Never rely solely on the motor's resistance to hold the bike.

<h2>Practical Reference: Brake System Installation for EV Motorbikes</h2>

<p>Brake system installation on an electric motorbike is not simply a mechanical task it is a commercial risk decision. In African markets, where riders depend on two-wheelers for daily income, a brake failure can destroy a business, trigger liability claims, and damage the reputation of an assembler or workshop. This practical reference gives technicians, assembly-line supervisors, and workshop owners the data, tolerances, and procedures needed to install front and rear calipers, connect hydraulic lines, bleed systems, adjust free play, and verify safety before an EV motorbike leaves the bay.</p>

<p>The module covers six lessons: mounting the front brake caliper and rotor, installing the rear caliper and rotor, connecting brake lines and bleeding, adjusting brake lever free play, testing brake function and safety, and integrating a parking brake where applicable. Use this reference alongside hands-on practice. All torque values, fluid specifications, and test criteria are drawn from manufacturer and industry sources. Where local conditions dust, heat, humidity, poor road surfaces increase risk, the tolerances should be treated as maximums, not targets.</p>

<h2>Brake Fluid and Environmental Data</h2>

<p>Brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, which lowers its boiling point and causes spongy braking. In hot African climates where ambient temperatures regularly exceed 35C and brake components heat up rapidly fluid degradation is faster. Use only the fluid specified by the manufacturer, usually DOT 3, DOT 4, or mineral oil. Never mix DOT and mineral oil systems; the seals will fail.</p>

<h3 style="color: #c92a2a; margin-top: 0;"> SAFETY WARNING Brake Fluid and Bleeding</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0;"><strong>Brake fluid is toxic, corrosive, and damages paint.</strong> Wear nitrile gloves and eye protection. Never blow compressed air into a brake line or reservoir fluid mist can cause serious eye injury. Bleed brakes in a well-ventilated area away from open flames. In African workshops, do not store brake fluid in unlabelled beverage bottles; children and workers have been poisoned by accidental ingestion. Dispose of used fluid at a licensed hazardous waste point never pour it into drains or soil. After any bleeding operation, test the brake at walking speed before road use. If the lever feels spongy or travels more than 70% of its travel, do not release the motorbike to the customer.</p>
</div>

<h2>Step-by-Step Practical Procedure</h2>

<h3>1. Mounting Front Brake Caliper and Rotor</h3>
<p>Clean the hub face and rotor mounting surface with brake cleaner. Fit the rotor with the directional arrow matching wheel rotation. Tighten rotor bolts in a star pattern to 1014 Nm. Slide the caliper over the rotor the pads should have 0.51.0 mm clearance on each side. Align the caliper mount with the fork lugs, apply thread-locking compound to clean bolts, and torque to 2228 Nm. Spin the wheel: it should rotate freely with no pad drag and no rotor runout greater than 0.1 mm.</p>

<h3>2. Installing Rear Brake Caliper and Rotor</h3>
<p>Rear installations often have less clearance because of the hub motor or sprocket. Confirm the rotor is centred in the caliper slot. On many EV motorbikes, the rear caliper is mounted on a floating bracket check that the bracket slides freely on its pins. Torque caliper bolts to the same 2228 Nm range unless the manufacturer specifies otherwise. Verify that the brake line routing does not contact the tyre, swingarm, or suspension spring through full travel.</p>

<h3>3. Connecting Brake Lines and Bleeding</h3>
<p>Route the high-pressure hose with smooth bends no kinks, no rubbing against sharp edges. Use new copper washers on banjo fittings. Fill the reservoir with the correct fluid. Attach a clear bleed tube to the bleeder valve and submerge the other end in a container of fresh fluid. Pump the lever slowly, hold it, open the bleeder, close it, then release. Repeat until no air bubbles appear. Top up the reservoir after every 34 cycles. Never let the reservoir run dry air will enter the master cylinder.</p>

<h3>4. Adjusting Brake Lever Free Play</h3>
<p>Most hydraulic systems have no user-adjustable free play at the lever; the lever should have 25 mm of free movement before pressure builds. If the lever is firm immediately, the master cylinder pushrod may be pre-loaded this can cause brake drag and overheating. Adjust the pushrod or lever reach screw according to the manual. On cable-actuated parking brakes, set free play to 510 mm at the lever.</p>

<h3>5. Testing Brake Function and Safety</h3>
<p>With the motorbike on a stand, spin each wheel and apply the brake the wheel should stop firmly and release without drag. Check for fluid leaks at every fitting. Test ride at low speed in a safe area: brake progressively from 10 km/h, then 20 km/h. The motorbike should stop in a straight line without pulling. Measure lever travel: if it exceeds 70% of total travel, re-bleed. Check brake pad thickness replace if below 1.5 mm.</p>

<h3>6. Parking Brake Integration</h3>
<p>Some EV motorbikes use a mechanical parking brake on the rear wheel or a motor-hold function. If a mechanical lever is fitted, confirm it holds the motorbike on a 10% gradient with the rider seated. If the parking brake is electronic, verify that it disengages automatically when the throttle is applied. Never rely on a parking brake as a service brake.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Rotor mounted with correct direction and torqued in a star pattern (1014 Nm).</li>
<li>Caliper bolts torqued to specification (2228 Nm) with thread-locking compound.</li>
<li>Brake line routed without kinks, contact, or sharp bends; new copper washers used.</li>
<li>System bled until no air bubbles appear; reservoir topped up and capped.</li>
<li>Lever free play 25 mm; no pre-load or brake drag when released.</li>
<li>No fluid leaks at banjo bolts, bleeder, or reservoir after 10 lever pumps.</li>
<li>Road test completed at low speed: straight stopping, no pulling, lever travel under 70%.</li>
<li>Pad thickness above 1.5 mm; parking brake holds on 10% gradient if fitted.</li>
</ul>
