1. Surface Preparation in African Climates

Welcome to Module 9, Lesson 1. This lesson focuses on the single most important factor in any painting or decorating job: surface preparation. In Africa, this step is even more critical because of the extreme conditions we face. From the humid coastal regions of Mombasa and Lagos to the dry, dusty Sahel and the high-altitude chill of Addis Ababa or Johannesburg, your surfaces behave differently. If you skip or rush preparation, your topcoat will fail, and your reputation will suffer. Let us start with the business case. A client calls you because their wall is peeling. You quote a price for painting. If you simply paint over the peeling surface, you will be back in three months doing a free repair. That is lost profit and lost referrals. Proper preparation takes time, but it is the cheapest insurance you can buy. First, assess the substrate. In most African homes and businesses, you will encounter concrete block, rendered cement, mud brick, timber, metal, and sometimes gypsum board. Each needs a different approach. For new cement render, the biggest enemy is alkalinity. Fresh cement is highly alkaline and will burn or blister oil-based paints and even some water-based ones. In hot, dry climates, cement cures quickly on the surface but remains wet inside. A simple test: sprinkle water on the wall. If it darkens and absorbs slowly, it is still curing. Wait at least four weeks for new render, or use an alkali-resisting primer. In coastal areas, salt in the air and in the sand used for building creates efflorescence, a white powdery deposit. You must brush it off, wash with a mild acid solution if severe, and then apply a salt-neutralising primer. Do not just paint over it. Second, remove all loose material. Use a scraper, wire brush, or sandpaper. For large areas, a mechanical sander or grinder with a wire cup is faster. In many African markets, you can hire a grinder for a day. Always wear eye protection and a dust mask. Dust from old paint may contain lead, especially in older buildings from the colonial era. Test kits are available in major cities. If lead is present, you must use wet sanding methods and proper disposal. Third, repair cracks and holes. Hairline cracks can be filled with a flexible filler. Larger cracks need to be cut into a V-shape, cleaned, and filled with a non-shrinking mortar or epoxy filler. In areas with ground movement, like parts of the Rift Valley, use a flexible acrylic sealant. For holes, use a patching plaster. Let it dry completely, then sand smooth. Fourth, address mould and mildew. This is common in humid regions like the Niger Delta, the Congo Basin, and coastal Tanzania. Mould is a health hazard and will grow through new paint. Wash the area with a solution of one part bleach to three parts water. Rinse well and let dry. Then apply a fungicidal wash or a mould-resistant primer. Fifth, prepare timber. Wood swells and shrinks with humidity. Sand with the grain, fill nail holes with wood filler, and prime all knots with a knotting solution to prevent resin bleed. In termite-prone areas, treat the wood with an insecticidal primer before painting. Sixth, prepare metal. Rust is the enemy. Remove rust with a wire brush or rust converter. For galvanised metal, which is common for roofing and gutters, you must use a special etch primer or the paint will flake. For ferrous metal, apply a red oxide or zinc phosphate primer. Now, let us talk about dust. In dry, dusty environments, dust settles on everything. Before you paint, you must damp down the floor and surrounding areas. Use a damp cloth to wipe down the prepared surface just before painting. If you are working in an occupied home, cover furniture with plastic sheets. In many African cities, you can buy cheap plastic sheeting from markets. Always protect yourself. Use gloves, masks, and eye protection. The cost of safety gear is small compared to a hospital bill. Finally, think about the business. A well-prepared surface means you use less paint, because the primer seals and the topcoat covers evenly. You save money on materials. You finish faster because you are not fixing defects. And your client is happy because the finish lasts. In this module, we will practice these steps on sample boards. You will learn to identify substrates, test for moisture and alkalinity, and choose the right primer. Remember, preparation is not a chore. It is the foundation of your craft and your profit. In the next lesson, we will look at choosing the right paints and tools for African conditions.

2. Choosing Paints and Tools for African Markets

Welcome to Lesson 2. Now that your surface is prepared, you need the right paint and the right tools. In Africa, you have a wide range of products, from cheap local emulsions to imported premium brands. Your job is to match the product to the job, the climate, and the client's budget. Let us start with paint types. The most common interior paint is water-based emulsion, often called plastic paint. It is easy to apply, dries fast, and has low odour. In hot climates, it is breathable, which helps prevent mould. However, cheap emulsions have low binder content and will chalk or wash off. Look for paints with at least 30 percent solids by volume. In many African countries, local manufacturers like Crown Paints in Kenya, Plascon in South Africa, and CAP in Nigeria produce good quality emulsions. For exteriors, you need an acrylic or weatherproof paint. These are more expensive but last longer. In coastal areas, choose a paint with fungicides and UV inhibitors. In dusty regions, choose a paint that resists dirt pick-up. For high-traffic areas like corridors and stairwells, use a washable matt or silk emulsion. For kitchens and bathrooms, use a semi-gloss or gloss acrylic, which is moisture-resistant and easy to clean. Oil-based paints, also called enamel, are still popular for wood and metal. They are hard-wearing and give a glossy finish. But they yellow over time, especially in dark areas, and they are slow to dry in cold or humid conditions. In hot, dry climates, they dry quickly but can blister if the surface is damp. For metal, use a direct-to-metal paint or a primer plus topcoat. For wood, use a wood stain or varnish if you want to see the grain, or an enamel if you want a solid colour. Now, tools. In Africa, you often have to work with what is available. But you can still choose wisely. Brushes: buy good quality synthetic bristle brushes for water-based paints and natural bristle for oil-based. Cheap brushes shed bristles and leave brush marks. In many markets, you can find Chinese-made brushes that are decent. Test them by pulling on the bristles. If they come out easily, do not buy. Rollers: for large walls, use a roller with a nap suitable for the surface. A short nap for smooth walls, a medium nap for light texture, and a long nap for rough render. In dusty areas, a roller with a closed-cell foam can be better because it does not trap dust. Sprayers: airless sprayers are fast for large jobs, but they require masking and skill. In many African cities, you can hire a sprayer or a sprayer operator. If you use a sprayer, thin the paint according to the manufacturer's instructions. Thinning too much reduces durability. Other tools: you need a sturdy ladder or scaffolding. In many informal settlements, you will see painters using makeshift bamboo scaffolds. That is dangerous. Invest in a good aluminium ladder or a mobile scaffold. You also need sandpaper, filler knives, caulking guns, and drop sheets. Now, let us talk about paint quantity. A common mistake is to under-order. Calculate the area: measure the perimeter of the room and multiply by the height. Subtract doors and windows. One litre of emulsion covers about 10 to 12 square metres per coat. You need two coats. So for a 4 by 5 metre room with 2.7 metre ceilings, the wall area is about 48 square metres. Two coats means 96 square metres. At 10 square metres per litre, you need about 10 litres. Always buy 10 percent extra for touch-ups. In Africa, paint is often sold in 4-litre, 10-litre, and 20-litre containers. Buying in larger containers is cheaper per litre. But only if you will use it. Do not buy 20 litres if you only need 5. Now, colour. Colour choice is personal, but you can advise. In hot climates, light colours reflect heat and make rooms feel cooler. In small rooms, light colours make the space feel bigger. In dusty areas, mid-tones hide dirt better than white. In coastal areas, blues and greens are popular. In many African cultures, bright colours are used for celebrations and exteriors. But for interiors, neutrals are safer for resale. Always mix all your paint from the same batch to avoid colour variation. If you must use different batches, mix them together in a large container before you start. Now, business. You can increase your profit by buying paint in bulk from a wholesaler. In many cities, there are paint depots where you can get trade discounts. Build relationships with suppliers. Ask for credit terms. But do not over-extend. Also, consider offering a paint supply service. Many clients prefer you to buy the paint so they do not have to. You can add a small markup. But be transparent. Show them the receipts. Finally, keep your tools clean. A clean brush lasts for years. A dirty brush is useless. After each job, wash water-based paint with warm soapy water and oil-based paint with white spirit or kerosene. In the next lesson, we will cover application techniques.

3. Application Techniques for Walls, Wood, and Metal

Welcome to Lesson 3. You have prepared the surface and chosen your paint and tools. Now you must apply the paint correctly. In Africa, the heat, wind, and dust affect how paint behaves. You must adapt your technique. Let us start with walls. For emulsion on interior walls, use a roller for large areas and a brush for edges. Start by cutting in with a brush along the ceiling, corners, and skirting. Then roll the main area. Work in sections of about one square metre. Roll in a W pattern, then fill in without lifting the roller. Do not press too hard. Let the roller do the work. In hot, dry conditions, paint dries fast. Work quickly and do not over-roll. If you over-roll, you will get lap marks. For exterior walls, use a roller with a longer nap to push paint into the texture. If the wall is very rough, you may need to use a brush or a sprayer. In dusty areas, paint early in the morning when there is less wind. If dust blows onto wet paint, it will stick. You can add a small amount of water to emulsion to improve flow, but never more than 10 percent. For oil-based enamel on wood and metal, use a brush. Apply in thin coats. Do not try to cover in one coat. Two thin coats are better than one thick coat. Thick coats will run, sag, and take forever to dry. For wood, brush with the grain. For metal, brush in one direction. Use a tack cloth to remove dust before painting. For spray application, you need a mask and eye protection. Spray in a steady, overlapping pattern. Keep the gun about 20 to 30 centimetres from the surface. Do not spray too close or you will get runs. Do not spray too far or you will get a dry, sandy finish. In many African workshops, spray painting is done outdoors under a shade net. That is fine, but avoid direct sun and wind. Now, special techniques. For textured finishes, you can use a stipple roller, a sponge, or a rag. In some African markets, there is demand for faux finishes that look like marble or stone. You can learn these techniques from online videos or from other painters. But practice on sample boards first. For lime wash, which is traditional in many parts of Africa, mix hydrated lime with water and a little salt. Apply with a brush. It is cheap and breathable. But it is not washable. For distemper, which is used in some rural areas, mix chalk and glue. It is very cheap but not durable. Now, common problems. If the paint is too thick, it will not flow. Add thinner according to the manufacturer's instructions. If the paint is too thin, it will run and not cover. Add more paint. If the temperature is above 35 degrees Celsius, do not paint in direct sun. The paint will dry too fast and blister. If the humidity is above 85 percent, do not paint oil-based paints. They will not cure. If you must paint in high humidity, use a water-based paint with a fungicide. If you see bubbles, you are painting over a damp surface. Stop and fix the moisture problem. If you see brush marks, your brush is too stiff or your paint is too thick. If you see roller marks, you are not overlapping enough. Now, safety. Paint fumes can be harmful. Always ventilate the area. Open windows and doors. Use a fan if possible. Do not smoke while painting. Do not eat or drink in the work area. Wash your hands after painting. If you get paint on your skin, wash with soap and water. If you get it in your eyes, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical help. Now, business. Speed matters. The faster you finish, the more jobs you can do. But speed must not compromise quality. Practice your technique so you become fast and good. Also, keep a clean work area. Clients notice. A clean painter is a professional. Finally, take photos of your work. Use them for your portfolio. In the next lesson, we will cover special effects and decorative finishes.

4. Decorative Finishes and Special Effects

Welcome to Lesson 4. Basic painting pays the bills, but decorative finishes can increase your income. In Africa, there is growing demand for feature walls, textured finishes, and faux effects in homes, hotels, and offices. This lesson will introduce you to several techniques you can offer. First, colour washing. This is a simple technique that adds depth. You need two or three colours of the same base. Apply the base coat and let it dry. Then mix a glaze: one part paint to four parts water or clear glaze. Apply the glaze with a brush or rag in random patches. While it is wet, wipe with a damp cloth to blend. This creates a soft, mottled look. It is popular in bedrooms and lounges. Second, sponging. Use a natural sea sponge or a synthetic sponge. Dip in glaze, squeeze out excess, and dab on the wall. Use a light touch. Build up layers. You can use two or three colours. This gives a textured, organic look. Third, rag rolling. Roll a rag into a cylinder, dip in glaze, and roll on the wall. Or roll a dry rag over wet glaze to remove some paint. This creates a marble-like effect. Fourth, stencilling. Cut a pattern from plastic or buy a stencil. Tape it to the wall. Apply paint with a stipple brush or a sponge. Do not overload. Move the stencil carefully. This is great for borders and feature walls. Fifth, faux marble. This is more advanced. You need a base coat of light colour. Then mix glazes in grey, green, and white. Apply with a brush and feather with a dry brush. Add veins with a thin brush. Practice on a board first. Sixth, faux wood grain. This is used on metal doors and cheap wood. Base coat in a light tan. Then mix a darker brown glaze. Apply with a brush and drag a graining tool or a dry brush through it. You can also use a feather. Seventh, textured finishes. You can add sand, sawdust, or marble dust to paint to create texture. Or use a ready-made texture paint. Apply with a trowel or roller. This is good for exterior walls and feature walls. Eighth, metallic finishes. Metallic paints are available in gold, silver, copper, and bronze. They are popular for accents. Apply over a dark base coat. Use a brush or roller. Do not over-brush. Ninth, lime wash and distemper. These are traditional finishes. Lime wash is made from hydrated lime, water, and salt. It gives a soft, chalky finish. It is breathable and cheap. Distemper is made from chalk and glue. It is used in rural areas. Both are good for heritage buildings. Now, let us talk about tools for decorative finishes. You need rags, sponges, stipple brushes, graining tools, stencils, and trowels. You can make some tools yourself. For example, a rag can be an old t-shirt. A sponge can be a natural sea sponge. A graining tool can be a piece of rubber with notches cut into it. Now, pricing. Decorative finishes take more time and skill. You should charge more. A common mistake is to charge the same as a plain paint job. Do not do that. Calculate your time. A feature wall might take two days. A plain wall might take two hours. Charge accordingly. In many African cities, clients are willing to pay a premium for unique finishes. Now, marketing. Take photos of your decorative work. Post them on social media. Show them to interior designers. Offer a free sample board to potential clients. Once they see the effect, they will want it. Now, common problems. If the glaze dries too fast, add a retarder or work in smaller sections. If the effect is too strong, wipe more. If it is too weak, add more glaze. Always test on a sample board. Now, safety. Some decorative products contain solvents. Use gloves and masks. Ventilate the area. Now, business. You can offer decorative finishes as an add-on to a standard painting job. When you quote for painting, ask if they want a feature wall. It is an easy upsell. Also, consider teaching workshops. Many people want to learn these techniques. You can charge for a one-day workshop. In the next lesson, we will cover business skills and customer relations.

5. Business Skills and Customer Relations for Painters

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson in Module 9. You have the technical skills. Now you need the business skills to turn those skills into a profitable career. In Africa, the painting and decorating industry is competitive. But there is plenty of work for reliable, professional painters. This lesson will help you stand out. First, pricing. How do you price a job? There are three common methods: per square metre, per day, and per job. Per square metre is best for large, straightforward jobs. Measure the area, decide your rate, and multiply. In many African cities, rates range from 2 to 10 US dollars per square metre, depending on the quality and the region. Per day is good for small jobs or when the scope is unclear. Know your daily rate. It should cover your labour, transport, and a profit margin. Per job is a fixed price. This is risky if you underestimate. Only use it when you are confident. Always add 10 to 20 percent for contingencies. Always provide a written quote. It should include: your name and contact, the client's name and address, the date, the scope of work, the materials, the price, the payment terms, and the validity period. In many African countries, verbal agreements lead to disputes. A written quote protects you. Second, payment. Never start a job without a deposit. A common practice is 50 percent upfront, 50 percent on completion. For larger jobs, you can take 30 percent upfront, 40 percent at midpoint, and 30 percent on completion. Do not buy materials with your own money unless you trust the client. If you must, add a markup. Always give a receipt. Third, customer relations. Be polite. Arrive on time. Call if you are delayed. Keep the client informed. Do not play loud music. Do not smoke or drink on the job. Clean up every day. Protect furniture and floors. At the end, walk the client through the job. Ask if they are satisfied. Fix any defects immediately. A happy client will refer you. Fourth, marketing. Word of mouth is the best marketing. Ask satisfied clients for referrals. Give them a small discount on their next job. Also, use social media. Create a WhatsApp Business account. Post before and after photos. Join local business groups. Print business cards. Put them on notice boards at hardware stores. Fifth, record keeping. Keep a simple notebook or use a phone app. Record every job: date, client, scope, materials, price, and payment. This helps you track your profit. It also helps you quote similar jobs in the future. Sixth, managing employees. If you grow, you will need helpers. Train them well. Pay them fairly and on time. Treat them with respect. A good team is your biggest asset. Seventh, dealing with difficult clients. Some clients will try to reduce the price after the job. Some will complain about minor issues. Stay calm. Refer to your written quote. If the complaint is valid, fix it. If not, explain politely. Do not get into arguments. Your reputation is worth more than one job. Eighth, continuous learning. Attend trade shows. Watch online tutorials. Network with other painters. The industry is changing. New products and techniques emerge. Stay updated. Ninth, health and safety. As a business owner, you are responsible for safety. Provide your workers with masks, gloves, and eye protection. Have a first aid kit. Know the emergency numbers. Tenth, financial planning. Save a portion of every payment. You will have slow months. You need a buffer. Also, invest in good tools. They save time and produce better results. Finally, let us talk about the future. The African construction industry is growing. There is a shortage of skilled painters. If you are reliable, skilled, and professional, you will never lack work. You can grow from a one-person operation to a company with multiple teams. You can specialise in decorative finishes, heritage restoration, or industrial coatings. The opportunities are endless. In this module, you have learned surface preparation, paint selection, application, decorative finishes, and business skills. Practice each lesson. Apply them to your next job. Remember, your work is your signature. Make it excellent. Thank you for completing Module 9.

<h3>Why pricing fails</h3>

<p>Most painters in African markets quote by "the room" or by a rough daily rate. That works until a client asks for a colour change, a second coat, or a repair you did not see at the viewing. Then you either lose money or you go back to the client asking for more, which damages trust. The fix is a written estimate with clear line items and assumptions.</p>

<p>Your price must cover four things: materials, labour, transport, and overhead plus profit. Many painters forget transport of paint tins, water, and equipment, and they forget the time spent buying materials. In cities like Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, and Dar es Salaam, transport can be 515% of a small job. If you do not charge for it, you pay for it.</p>

<h3>Site setup that protects your work</h3>

<p>Before you open a tin, set up the site. This is not optional. It protects the client's floors, furniture, and fittings, and it protects your reputation. It also reduces the chance of a call-back, which is unpaid work.</p>

<p>Move or cover furniture with plastic sheeting or old bedsheets. Tape off skirting boards, door frames, window frames, and light switches. Cover floors with dust sheets or plastic. If you are sanding, close doors to other rooms and open a window for ventilation. Keep water and clean rags on hand. Put your tools in one place so you do not lose them.</p>

<p>On new cement or plaster, check moisture before painting. Painting damp walls traps moisture and causes blistering and mould. In humid coastal cities such as Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban, allow extra drying time. In dry inland areas, new plaster can dry too fast and crack, so dampen it lightly before applying primer.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020;background:#fff0f2;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;">
<p style="margin:0;font-weight:bold;color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING CHEMICALS AND HEALTH</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Paint, primer, thinner, and paint stripper contain chemicals that can harm you. Solvent-based products are flammable and give off fumes. Work in a well-ventilated area, keep away from open flames, and never smoke while painting. Wear gloves and eye protection when handling solvents, strippers, or two-part products. If you feel dizzy, leave the area and get fresh air immediately. Keep paint and thinner out of reach of children and away from food and drinking water. Do not pour leftover paint or thinner into drains, rivers, or on the ground. Follow the manufacturer's safety data sheet and local disposal rules. For chemical safety guidance, see the WHO International Programme on Chemical Safety at who.int.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Measure every wall, then subtract doors, windows, and other openings before estimating paint.</li>
<li>Confirm the spreading rate on the actual tin or data sheet, not from memory.</li>
<li>Add a 510% waste allowance and round up to the nearest tin size you can buy.</li>
<li>Write a one-page estimate with materials, labour, transport, overhead, and profit shown separately.</li>
<li>State your assumptions: number of coats, surface condition, and what is excluded.</li>
<li>Set up dust sheets, tape, and ventilation before opening any tin.</li>
<li>Check wall moisture and repair cracks before priming; never paint over damp or loose surfaces.</li>
<li>Keep a simple record of every job: materials used, hours worked, and final profit.</li>
</ul>
