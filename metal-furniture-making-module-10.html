1. Introduction and Why It Matters

When you make metal furniture, the first question you must ask is: will it live outside in the sun and rain, or inside a house? This choice changes everythingthe metal you pick, the thickness, the paint, the joints, even the screws. In Africa, outdoor furniture faces harsh sun, heavy rain, dust, and sometimes salty air near the coast. Indoor furniture faces less weather but still needs to handle humidity, bumps, and daily use. If you use indoor methods for outdoor furniture, it will rust and fall apart within one rainy season. If you use outdoor-heavy methods for indoor furniture, you waste money and make pieces too heavy to move. This module teaches you the difference so you can build furniture that lasts.

Start by understanding the enemy: rust. Rust happens when iron in steel meets water and oxygen. Outdoor furniture is constantly wet. Indoor furniture only gets occasional spills. So outdoor pieces need galvanised steel, stainless steel, or at least thick coats of rust-proof paint. Indoor pieces can use mild steel with a simple primer and topcoat.

Another key difference is expansion and contraction. Metal grows in heat and shrinks in cold. Outdoor furniture in hot African sun can expand enough to crack welds or loosen bolts. Indoor furniture stays at a more stable temperature. That means outdoor joints need to be flexible or reinforced, while indoor joints can be rigid.

Also think about comfort. Outdoor furniture often has slatted or mesh seats so water drains and air flows. Indoor furniture can have solid cushions and flat surfaces. You will learn to choose the right design for each setting.

Finally, consider cost. Galvanised steel costs more than mild steel, but it saves you from replacing rusty furniture every year. For a business, building furniture that lasts builds your reputation. Customers remember a chair that survived three rainy seasons. So take this module seriouslyit is the difference between a hobby and a real metal furniture business.

2. Tools and Materials

To build both indoor and outdoor metal furniture, you need a basic set of tools. For cutting: an angle grinder with 115mm or 230mm cutting discs, and a hacksaw for small cuts. For welding: a stick welder (arc welder) with 2.5mm or 3.2mm electrodes works well for mild steel; for thinner tubes, a MIG welder is better but costs more. You also need a drill with HSS bits, a wire brush, and clamps. For measuring: a steel tape, a try square, and a spirit level. For finishing: sandpaper (80, 120, 220 grit), paint brushes, and spray cans if you prefer.

Materials differ for indoor and outdoor. For indoor furniture, mild steel square tubes (20mm x 20mm x 1.5mm) are cheap and easy to weld. You can use thin sheets for panels. For outdoor furniture, use galvanised steel tubes (25mm x 25mm x 2mm) or stainless steel if you can afford it. Galvanised steel has a zinc coating that fights rust. If you weld galvanised steel, you must grind away the zinc at the weld area first because the fumes are toxic. Always weld in a well-ventilated area or wear a respirator.

For outdoor furniture, also consider aluminium. It does not rust, but it is softer and needs special welding (TIG) or bolting. Many African workshops use bolted aluminium for outdoor chairs because it is light and corrosion-resistant. For indoor, mild steel is fine.

Fasteners matter too. Use stainless steel bolts and screws for outdoor furniture. Regular steel screws will rust and stain the metal. For indoor, zinc-plated screws are okay. For outdoor cushions, use marine-grade foam and waterproof fabric. For indoor, any foam and fabric will do.

Finally, finishes: outdoor furniture needs a primer made for galvanised metal (like zinc phosphate primer) and a topcoat of enamel or epoxy paint. Indoor furniture can use red oxide primer and enamel paint. Always keep a stock of anti-rust spray for touch-ups.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Decide the furniture's location. Ask: will it be under a roof or open sky? If open sky, choose outdoor method. If inside or under a veranda, indoor method is okay. Write this down.

Step 2: Choose your metal. For outdoor: galvanised steel, stainless steel, or aluminium. For indoor: mild steel. Measure and cut your tubes to size using an angle grinder. For a simple outdoor chair, you need: 4 legs (40cm long), 2 side frames (45cm), 2 cross bars (40cm), and a seat frame (40cm x 40cm). For indoor, you can use thinner tubes.

Step 3: Clean and prepare. For galvanised steel, grind off zinc at all weld points. For mild steel, wipe with acetone or thinner to remove oil. Clamp your pieces on a flat surface (a welding table or concrete floor) and check squareness with a try square.

Step 4: Weld or bolt. For outdoor, if using galvanised steel, weld with 2.5mm electrodes at 70-90 amps. Make tack welds first, check alignment, then complete welds. For aluminium, bolt with stainless bolts. For indoor, weld mild steel at 80-100 amps. Let welds cool, then grind smooth.

Step 5: Drill drainage holes. Outdoor furniture must drain water. Drill 6mm holes in the bottom of legs and in seat frames so water does not collect. Indoor furniture does not need this.

Step 6: Finish. For outdoor: apply zinc phosphate primer, then two coats of enamel or epoxy paint. For indoor: apply red oxide primer, then one or two coats of enamel. Let dry between coats.

Step 7: Add cushions or slats. For outdoor, use slats with 10mm gaps for drainage. For indoor, you can use solid panels or cushions. Attach with stainless screws for outdoor, regular screws for indoor.

Step 8: Inspect and test. Spray water on outdoor furniture to check drainage. Sit on it to check stability. Touch up any missed spots with anti-rust paint.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using mild steel outdoors without protection. Fix: If you already built it, grind off rust, apply rust converter, then zinc phosphate primer and two coats of outdoor enamel. Next time, use galvanised steel or stainless.

Mistake 2: Welding galvanised steel without removing zinc. This creates toxic fumes and weak welds. Fix: Always grind zinc off 2cm around the weld area. Wear a respirator. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air.

Mistake 3: No drainage holes. Water collects in legs and rusts from inside. Fix: Drill 6mm holes at the lowest points. For existing furniture, drill now and spray anti-rust inside.

Mistake 4: Using indoor paint outdoors. Indoor enamel cracks under UV light. Fix: Sand off, apply outdoor-grade paint with UV inhibitors. In Africa, look for paint labelled 'exterior' or 'marine'.

Mistake 5: Over-tightening bolts on outdoor furniture. Metal expands in heat and can crack. Fix: Use nylon lock nuts or spring washers, and leave a small gap for expansion.

Mistake 6: Ignoring humidity indoors. Even indoor furniture in coastal areas rusts. Fix: Use a dehumidifier or apply a clear coat of anti-rust spray every 6 months.

Mistake 7: Mixing metals. Connecting aluminium to steel outdoors causes galvanic corrosion. Fix: Use plastic washers or nylon bushings between different metals.

Mistake 8: Not testing stability. A wobbly chair is dangerous. Fix: Always weld or bolt on a flat surface. After finishing, place on flat ground and adjust leg ends with a grinder if needed.

Mistake 9: Skipping primer. Paint peels off bare metal. Fix: Always prime first. For galvanised, use zinc phosphate primer; for mild steel, red oxide.

Mistake 10: Using wrong screws. Indoor screws rust outdoors and stain. Fix: Replace with stainless steel screws.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety starts before you pick up a tool. Wear proper gear: safety glasses, welding helmet or goggles, leather gloves, and steel-toe boots. For welding, wear a leather apron and long sleeves. Never weld in shorts or open shoes. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, but you still need protection from sparks and UV rays.

Ventilation is critical. Welding galvanised steel releases zinc fumes that cause metal fume feverflu-like symptoms. Always weld outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed. If you must weld inside, use a fan to blow fumes away from you and wear a respirator with P100 filters. Never weld near flammable materials like paint, thinner, or wood shavings.

Fire safety: keep a bucket of sand or a fire extinguisher within reach. Sparks can travel far. Clear your work area of dry grass, paper, and cloth. In dry season, be extra careful.

Electrical safety: check your welder's cables for cracks. Do not weld in wet conditions. Use a residual current device (RCD) if available. Ground your welder properly.

Best practices for quality: measure twice, cut once. Use jigs or clamps to keep pieces square. Clean welds with a wire brush after each pass. Let the metal cool naturallydo not quench in water, as it can make steel brittle. For outdoor furniture, always test drainage by pouring water on it. For indoor, check for sharp edges and grind them smooth.

Maintenance advice for customers: tell them to rinse outdoor furniture with fresh water monthly if near the coast, and to touch up scratches with anti-rust paint. For indoor, dust with a dry cloth and avoid harsh chemicals.

Finally, keep your workspace organised. Store metal off the ground on wooden pallets. Label your paints and primers. A clean shop is a safe shop.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will build a simple metal stool that can be adapted for indoor or outdoor use. You will make two versions: one indoor (mild steel, indoor finish) and one outdoor (galvanised steel, outdoor finish). This will teach you the differences hands-on.

Materials for one stool: 4 legs (30cm of 20mm square tube for indoor, 25mm for outdoor), 4 cross bars (30cm), 1 seat frame (30cm x 30cm), and a seat top (wood or metal sheet). For outdoor, use galvanised tube and stainless screws. For indoor, mild steel and zinc-plated screws.

Tools: angle grinder, welder (stick or MIG), drill with 6mm and 8mm bits, clamps, try square, tape measure, wire brush, paint brushes, primer, and topcoat.

Step 1: Cut all pieces. For outdoor, grind zinc off ends. Step 2: Clamp legs and cross bars into a square frame. Weld or bolt. Check with try square. Step 3: Weld seat frame on top. Step 4: For outdoor, drill 6mm drainage holes in bottom of legs and in seat frame. Step 5: Grind welds smooth. Step 6: Clean metal. For indoor: apply red oxide primer, then enamel. For outdoor: apply zinc phosphate primer, then two coats of exterior enamel. Step 7: Attach seat top. For outdoor, use slats with 10mm gaps; for indoor, a solid board. Step 8: Test. Put the indoor stool in a dry room; put the outdoor stool outside in the rain for one week. Check for rust. Touch up any rust spots with anti-rust paint.

After one week, compare: the outdoor stool should show no rust if done correctly. The indoor stool would rust if left outside. Write down what you learned. This exercise takes about 4 hours per stool. When you can build both correctly, you are ready to sell to customers who need furniture for different settings.

<h2>Practical Reference: Outdoor vs Indoor Furniture</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working document you keep in the workshop. It consolidates the decisions, materials, and treatments that separate a piece of metal furniture that lasts five years outdoors from one that fails in eighteen months. For a metal furniture business in Africa, this distinction is the difference between a one-off sale and a customer who returns with referrals. Outdoor furniture commands higher prices than indoor pieces of similar size, but only if it survives sun, rain, dust, and coastal salt. This lesson gives you the reference data, conversion tables, and checklists to price, build, and guarantee outdoor work confidently.</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#b71c1c;font-weight:bold;font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING GALVANISING AND WELDING FUMES</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#7f0000;font-size:14px;">Never weld, cut, or grind galvanised steel without forced ventilation and a P3-rated respirator. The zinc coating releases zinc oxide fumes that cause "metal fume fever" flu-like symptoms that can hospitalise a worker within hours. In enclosed African workshops with limited airflow, this risk is severe. Weld outdoors or with extraction, and keep a written fume-control procedure on the wall. Grinding galvanised surfaces also produces zinc dust: wear sealed goggles and gloves, and wash skin immediately.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Confirm exposure:</strong> Ask the client exactly where the piece will sit covered veranda, open garden, or coastal balcony. This single answer sets your metal, thickness, and coating system.</li>
<li><strong>Match wall thickness to load and weather:</strong> Never use 1.2 mm tube for outdoor seating or tables; specify 2.0 mm minimum for steel, 1.5 mm for stainless.</li>
<li><strong>Specify galvanising before painting:</strong> Hot-dip galvanising gives 2040 years of barrier protection; paint alone on mild steel gives 13 years outdoors in African UV.</li>
<li><strong>Design for drainage:</strong> Drill 6 mm weep holes at the lowest point of every closed tube section. Trapped water is the number-one cause of internal rust.</li>
<li><strong>Use stainless fasteners and hinges:</strong> Mild steel bolts rust first and stain the coating; 304 stainless is the minimum for outdoor work.</li>
<li><strong>Build in a maintenance contract:</strong> Offer 6-monthly inspection and touch-up as a paid service. This turns one sale into recurring income.</li>
<li><strong>Document your coating system:</strong> Record product brand, number of coats, and dry film thickness on the invoice. This protects you in warranty disputes and builds client trust.</li>
<li><strong>Test before delivery:</strong> Water-test the piece, check all joints for movement, and photograph it for your portfolio and warranty file.</li>
</ol>
