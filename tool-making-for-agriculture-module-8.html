1. Making Watering Cans and Buckets

A good watering can is a farmer's daily tool. You can make one from a 5-litre plastic jerrycan or a metal bucket. For a plastic jerrycan, cut the top off with a hacksaw, leaving a handle. For the spout, use a 30 cm length of 12 mm PVC pipe. Drill a hole in the side of the can near the bottom, insert the pipe, and seal it with silicone or epoxy. The rose (sprinkler head) can be made from a tin can lid punched with a 2 mm nail in a grid pattern. Attach it to the spout with wire or tape. For a metal bucket, you can solder a spout made from a piece of galvanised pipe. Alternatively, make a simple bucket from a 20-litre oil tin. Clean it thoroughly with soap and water. Cut the top off with a chisel and hammer, leaving a rim. Attach a handle made from 6 mm rebar bent into a U-shape, or use heavy wire. For a watering can, punch holes in the bottom of a tin can and attach it to a wooden handle. In Africa, where materials are scarce, recycling is key. Use old plastic containers, metal tins, and rubber from tyres. A well-made watering can should hold 5-10 litres and pour a gentle stream. Test for leaks by filling with water. If it leaks, seal with beeswax or tar. These tools are essential for nursery beds, vegetable gardens, and young trees. With basic tools like a hacksaw, drill, and pliers, you can make one in under an hour. Remember to smooth sharp edges with a file to avoid cuts. A sturdy watering can saves water and reduces back strain compared to carrying a bucket.

2. Fabricating Drip Irrigation Components

Drip irrigation saves water by delivering it directly to plant roots. You can make a simple drip system using 16 mm black polyethylene (PE) pipe, available in most African farm supply shops. Start with a main line from your water source. Use a knife or pipe cutter to cut the pipe. For emitters, you can use micro-tubes: cut 30 cm lengths of 4 mm spaghetti tubing. Insert one end into a hole punched in the main line with a 4 mm punch. The other end goes to the plant. To regulate flow, tie a knot in the tube or insert a small plastic barb. Alternatively, make emitters from old bicycle inner tubes: cut small strips and puncture a pinhole. For a 100-metre row of tomatoes, you need about 200 emitters spaced 50 cm apart. You can also make a DIY drip line by punching holes directly in the 16 mm pipe every 30 cm. But holes can clog; use a filter made from a cloth bag or a 200-mesh screen. Connect the main line to a bucket or tank using a 13 mm barbed connector and a rubber washer. Gravity feed works if the tank is at least 1 metre above the field. For larger areas, use a small 12V pump. In dry areas like the Sahel, drip irrigation can increase yields by 50% with half the water. Maintain your system by flushing the lines weekly. Use vinegar to clear mineral deposits. With a punch, barb fittings, and end caps, you can assemble a drip kit for a 100 mÂ² garden in a day. Always use a filter to prevent clogging. This technology is affordable and scalable.

3. Building Sprinkler Systems

Sprinkler systems mimic rainfall and are good for leafy greens, carrots, and pasture. A simple homemade sprinkler can be made from a 2-litre plastic bottle. Puncture many small holes (1 mm) in the sides and bottom using a hot needle. Attach the bottle to a garden hose with a clamp. When water flows, it sprays in a circle. For a larger area, use a 20-litre bucket with holes drilled in the lid, placed on a raised platform. Connect a hose to the bucket and let gravity do the work. For a more efficient system, make a rotating sprinkler from a bicycle wheel. Weld or bolt a 1-metre length of 15 mm galvanised pipe to the hub. Drill holes along the pipe at 10 cm intervals, angled to create rotation. Connect the pipe to a hose via a swivel joint. Water pressure from a tank 3 metres high can run this. You can also use PVC pipes: build a frame with 20 mm PVC, drill 2 mm holes, and connect to a tap. For a 10 m x 10 m plot, you need about 20 sprinkler heads if using bottles, or one rotating sprinkler. Space sprinklers 3-4 metres apart. Test coverage by placing cups on the ground; aim for even distribution. In windy areas, use low-angle sprinklers. Maintenance: clean holes with a wire, and drain pipes before frost (not common in Africa, but in highlands). Sprinklers use more water than drip, so schedule irrigation early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. With basic plumbing skills, you can build a sprinkler system for under $20 using recycled materials.

4. Making Hand Pumps and Treadle Pumps

Hand pumps and treadle pumps lift water from shallow wells (up to 10 metres) and are vital where electricity is absent. A simple hand pump can be made from PVC pipe and a rubber plunger. Use a 50 mm PVC pipe as the cylinder. Cut a rubber disc from an old tyre inner tube to fit snugly inside the pipe; this is the plunger. Attach the plunger to a 10 mm steel rod. The rod goes up and down through a cap on the pipe. At the bottom, install a one-way valve (a flapper made from a rubber sheet and a hinge). When you pull up, water is drawn in; when you push down, the valve closes and water is pushed up. For the handle, use a metal lever attached to the rod. A treadle pump is more efficient for irrigation. It uses two cylinders and a treadle (foot pedal) mechanism. Build a frame from 40 mm angle iron. The cylinders are 100 mm PVC pipes. Connect the pistons to a crank shaft made from a bicycle axle. The treadle is a wooden plank pivoted in the middle. As you step, the crank turns, moving the pistons up and down. A well-made treadle pump can lift 1 litre per stroke and irrigate 0.2 hectares. Use leather or rubber cups for pistons. Seal all joints with Teflon tape or rubber gaskets. Test for leaks by pumping water and checking for air bubbles. These pumps are cheap to build (under $50) and easy to maintain. In many African countries, treadle pumps have doubled incomes for smallholder farmers. Always install a foot valve to keep water in the pipe. With basic welding and plumbing skills, you can make one in a day.

5. Constructing Water Tanks and Reservoirs

Water storage is critical for dry-season irrigation. You can build a ferrocement tank using cement, sand, wire mesh, and chicken wire. For a 2,000-litre tank, dig a circular hole 1.5 m deep and 1.5 m diameter. Build a frame with 6 mm rebar: vertical bars every 20 cm, and horizontal rings every 30 cm. Wrap chicken wire around the outside and inside, then apply a 1:3 cement-sand mortar in three layers. Cure for 7 days by keeping it wet. Alternatively, make a tank from a 200-litre drum cut in half and welded together, or use old plastic containers. For a reservoir, dig a pond and line it with clay or a 1 mm UV-resistant plastic liner. A 10 m x 10 m pond, 1 m deep, holds 100,000 litres. In areas with high evaporation, cover the pond with a shade net or floating balls. For a simple above-ground tank, use a 1,000-litre plastic tank (available in most towns) and place it on a raised platform of bricks or stones. Ensure the platform can support 1,000 kg. Connect an outlet pipe with a tap. For underground tanks, use concrete blocks or bricks, plastered with waterproof cement. Add a lid to prevent mosquitoes and evaporation. In Ethiopia, farmers build underground tanks to collect rainwater from roofs. A 20 mÂ² roof can collect 20,000 litres per year in a 1,000 mm rainfall area. Always include a first-flush diverter to keep dirty water out. With basic masonry skills, you can build a 5,000-litre tank for under $100. Regularly inspect for cracks and repair with mortar.

6. Maintenance of Irrigation Equipment

Regular maintenance extends the life of your irrigation tools and saves water. For watering cans and buckets, check for leaks and cracks. Seal small holes with silicone or beeswax. Replace broken handles with rebar or heavy wire. Clean the spout and rose weekly to remove algae and dirt. For drip systems, flush the main line and laterals every two weeks. Remove end caps and let water run for 5 minutes. Clean filters weekly: if using a screen filter, rinse it; if using a sand filter, backwash it. Use a 10% chlorine solution (1 litre bleach to 10 litres water) to clear organic clogging, then flush with clean water. For sprinklers, clean holes with a fine wire. Check for worn nozzles and replace them. Lubricate rotating parts with grease. For hand and treadle pumps, inspect the plunger and valves every month. Replace worn rubber cups. Tighten all bolts and check the handle for cracks. Lubricate the pivot points with oil. For tanks and reservoirs, inspect for cracks and leaks. Patch with cement mortar or epoxy. Clean the tank annually: drain, scrub with a brush and bleach solution, then rinse. Remove sediment from the bottom. For ponds, remove weeds and debris. Check the liner for tears. In all cases, keep a maintenance log. Train all users on proper operation. A well-maintained drip system can last 10 years; a neglected one fails in one season. Use the right tools: a pipe cutter, spanners, and a wire brush. Store equipment under cover when not in use. In Africa, where spare parts may be scarce, learn to make your own gaskets from old inner tubes. Preventive maintenance is cheaper than repairs. Spend 30 minutes each week checking your system; it will pay off with better yields and less water waste.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Treadle and Hand Pump Fabrication

Treadle pumps operate under high mechanical load and can cause serious foot, shin, and back injuries if the frame is under-built or the treadle linkage is exposed. Always weld or bolt a full-length guard over the treadle pivot, use anti-slip tread plates, and test the pump at maximum stroke before delivery. Never install a pump on a well or borehole without a properly fitted well cap and a child-proof cover â open wells are a leading cause of drowning in rural Africa. For suction pumps, never exceed 7 m lift; cavitation and loss of prime can cause sudden handle recoil. Wear eye protection when cutting galvanised sheet and gloves when handling cement render.

Business Notes for the Tool Maker

Price for repair, not just for sale. A watering can that lasts five years and can be re-riveted for a small fee builds a customer relationship. A cheap can that fails in one season destroys it. Build to a standard, publish that standard, and offer a repair service.

Bundle the kit. A drip kit sold alone is a hard sell. A drip kit plus a 1,000 L tank plus a hand pump plus a one-day installation and training visit is a package a farmer can finance. Partner with a local microfinance institution or a village savings and loan association (VSLA) to offer payment over two seasons.

Standardise fittings. If every drip kit in your district uses the same 16 mm lateral and the same emitter spacing, farmers can buy replacements from any of your agents. Standardisation is a market-building strategy, not a constraint.

Train the buyer. Include a one-page maintenance card with every tool. Farmers who know how to flush a filter and clean an emitter come back to buy more, not to complain.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Leak test every tank and can for 24 hours before delivery â a dry seam today is a returned product tomorrow.
  • Match emitter flow to head: at 1â2 m head, use 2â4 L/h emitters; higher flow needs higher pressure.
  • Always fit a filter upstream of drip laterals â sand and organic matter are the number one cause of emitter blockage.
  • Keep suction lift under 7 m for hand pumps; beyond that, specify a treadle or motorised pump.
  • Size tanks for at least one day of peak demand plus 20% reserve.
  • Guard all treadle linkages and fit anti-slip plates before the pump leaves the workshop.
  • Supply a maintenance card with flushing, cleaning, and storage instructions in the local language.
  • Record every sale and repair â this data is your evidence base for credit, grants, and expansion.
