2. Snail Biology and Species

You cannot farm what you do not understand. This module teaches you which snails to farm, how they grow, how they reproduce, and the specific conditions that keep them healthy and productive.

Module 2 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — African species — Achatina achatina, A. fulica, Archachatina marginata

SpeciesCommon nameAdult sizeGrowth rateBest for
Achatina achatinaGiant African snail150–300 gSlow (18–24 months)Premium market, high price per snail
Achatina fulicaCommon African snail80–150 gFast (10–14 months)Volume production, quick turnover
Archachatina marginataBig black snail120–250 gMedium (14–18 months)West African market, restaurant supply

For most African farmers, Achatina fulica is the best starting species. It grows faster, tolerates varied conditions, and multiplies more quickly. It commands a slightly lower price per snail, but the volume compensates.

Once you have mastered A. fulica, add A. achatina for the premium market. Restaurants and exporters pay 40–60% more for the giant African snail.

Do not mix species in one pen. Different species have different growth rates, feeding patterns, and breeding behaviours. Mixing them causes stress and reduced yields. Keep each species in its own pen.
Practical exercise 2.1: Identify which species is available in your region — ask local snail sellers, extension officers, or agricultural universities. Buy 20 as a test batch and observe them for 2 weeks before investing in larger numbers.

Lesson 2.2 — The snail life cycle

Understanding the life cycle tells you when to expect eggs, how long each stage lasts, and where to focus your management.

StageDurationSizeKey needs
Egg14–30 days3–6 mmDamp soil, no disturbance
Hatchling0–1 month3–5 mmVery fine food, high humidity, predator protection
Juvenile1–6 months5–40 gRegular feeding, calcium, space to grow
Young adult6–12 months40–100 gRicher feed, breeding begins
Breeding adult12+ months100–180 gCalcium-rich feed, breeding space
Mature / market14–24 months120–300 gFinal growth before harvest

Key facts:

  • Snails are hermaphrodites — each has both male and female organs. But most species still require two snails to mate.
  • Eggs are laid in the soil, usually 3–8 cm deep.
  • A single adult lays 100–300 eggs per year, in batches of 20–60 per clutch.
  • Hatchlings are tiny and fragile — this is where most mortality happens.
The critical stage: Hatchlings. Most beginner failures happen here — the young snails dry out, get eaten by ants, or starve because the feed is too coarse. Get hatchling care right and you succeed.
Practical exercise 2.2: Sketch the snail life cycle on paper. Mark the duration of each stage and the key risks. Post it in your pen.

Lesson 2.3 — Reproduction and egg laying

Snails mate during the rainy season or when conditions are warm and humid. In Africa, mating is usually triggered by the first rains.

The mating process:

  1. Two snails approach each other, circle for 30–60 minutes
  2. They exchange sperm (each gives and receives)
  3. 3–14 days later, both lay eggs
  4. Eggs are deposited in a small hole in the soil, 5–8 cm deep
  5. Hole is covered with soil and a slime seal
  6. Eggs hatch in 14–30 days, depending on temperature and humidity

What triggers egg laying:

  • Loose, damp soil (not compacted)
  • Calcium-rich environment
  • Abundant food
  • Warm humid weather
  • Low disturbance

What prevents it:

  • Compacted or dry soil
  • Overcrowding
  • Malnutrition — especially calcium deficiency
  • Cold weather or too much direct sun
  • Constant handling and noise
Practical exercise 2.3: Prepare a breeding pen with 20–25 cm of loose soil. Add crushed eggshells on top. Place 10 adults. Watch for mating and egg-laying behaviour for 30 days.

Lesson 2.4 — Growth rates and factors affecting them

Snails reach market size in 10–24 months depending on species, feed, and conditions. Here is what affects growth.

FactorEffect on growth
Temperature (22–28°C ideal)Below 18°C = dormancy. Above 32°C = stress.
Humidity (70–85%)Below 60% = dehydration and slow growth
Feed qualityMixed fruits + vegetables + calcium = fastest growth
CalciumCritical for shell growth. Without it, shells stay thin and growth stops.
Stocking densityUnder 50 per m² is ideal. Overcrowding = stunted growth.
Water accessSnails absorb water through their skin. Spray daily.
PredatorsAny predator presence stresses snails and slows growth.
SpeciesA. fulica grows fastest; A. achatina slowest but largest

Growth targets for A. fulica:

  • Month 3: 5–10 g
  • Month 6: 20–35 g
  • Month 9: 50–70 g
  • Month 12: 80–120 g (sellable)
  • Month 14–15: 100–150 g (optimal)
The growth rule: Snails grow slowly when any factor is wrong. Fast growth requires all conditions right — temperature, humidity, feed, calcium, and space. If growth stalls, check the environment before assuming disease.
Practical exercise 2.4: Weigh 20 snails every 30 days. Log the weights. This is your growth curve. If growth is slower than the targets above, identify which factor is limiting.

Lesson 2.5 — Environmental requirements — temperature, humidity, light

FactorIdeal rangeEffect of extremes
Temperature22–28°CBelow 15°C or above 35°C = dormancy or death
Humidity70–85%Below 60% = dehydration. Above 95% = mould.
LightShade or filteredDirect sun kills snails. Dark is fine.
Soil moistureDamp, not wetDry soil = no egg laying. Wet soil = drowning.
AirflowGentleStill air promotes mould. Too much airflow dries the pen.

Maintaining conditions in Africa:

  • Shade: Use shade netting, palm fronds, or plant trees around the pen. Never direct sun.
  • Humidity: Spray the pen lightly 1–2 times per day. More in hot weather, less in cool.
  • Temperature: In cooler regions, use a greenhouse-like structure. In hot regions, shade and airflow.
  • Soil: Use loose loamy soil mixed with compost. Test with your hand — it should clump but break apart easily.
Practical exercise 2.5: Buy a cheap thermometer and hygrometer (about $15). Place them inside your pen. Log daily temperature and humidity for 2 weeks. Adjust your design until you can hold 22–28°C and 70–85%.

Lesson 2.6 — Sourcing your first snails

Where your first snails come from determines your business' future. Source carefully.

Sources, from best to worst:

  1. Established snail farm. Best source. Healthy, domesticated, proven genetics. Expect to pay $0.30–$1.00 per juvenile snail.
  2. Agricultural university or research station. Sometimes sells stock or advises on sourcing.
  3. Local market. Cheapest but risky — market snails may be wild-caught, stressed, or contaminated with pesticides. Observe for 2 weeks before mixing with your breeding stock.
  4. Wild collection. Free but unreliable. Wild snails are often sick, stressed, and carry parasites. Only use if nothing else is available, and quarantine for 30 days.

What to look for when buying:

  • Healthy shell — smooth, glossy, no cracks or holes
  • Active behaviour — moving, feeding, responding to touch
  • Right size — juveniles 5–15 g are best for starting (buying adults is expensive and they adapt slower)
  • No parasites or mites — check around the shell opening
  • Species purity — all one species
Always quarantine new stock for 30 days. Keep them in a separate pen, watch for disease or death, then introduce them to your main stock. This prevents diseases from spreading.
Practical exercise 2.6: Contact 3 snail sources. Ask about their stock, price, and health. Buy 100 juveniles from the best source. Quarantine for 30 days before mixing with any existing stock.
