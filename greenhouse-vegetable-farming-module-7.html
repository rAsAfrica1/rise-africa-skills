1. Selecting and Preparing Greenhouse Sites for African Conditions

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 1. This lesson is about the single most important decision you will make before you plant a single seed: where you put your greenhouse and how you prepare the ground under it. In Africa, the difference between a profitable greenhouse and a money pit often comes down to site selection. You can have the best tomato variety, the best drip lines, and the hardest working family labour, but if your greenhouse sits in the wrong place, you will fight nature every single day. Let us start with the business case. A greenhouse is a capital investment. In most African markets, a decent 8 by 15 metre greenhouse with insect netting, a drip system, and a small water tank costs between 1,500 and 4,000 US dollars depending on the country and materials. That is a serious amount of money for a smallholder or a young agripreneur. You need that structure to last at least five years and to produce high value crops every month. Site selection protects that investment. The first factor is water. You need a reliable water source within easy pumping distance. A greenhouse vegetable crop needs between 4 and 8 litres of water per plant per day in hot weather. For 500 tomato plants, that is 2,000 to 4,000 litres per day. If your only water is a borehole 300 metres away that you must carry by jerrycan, you will fail. Look for a well, a river, a dam, a borehole, or a municipal line that can fill a 5,000 litre tank at least every two days. Test the water for salinity. In coastal areas or dry inland areas, water can be too salty for tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. A simple electrical conductivity meter costs little and saves you from crop failure. The second factor is topography and drainage. Choose land that is gently sloping, ideally 1 to 2 percent. Flat land can flood during heavy rains, and steep land requires terracing and erosion control. In many parts of West Africa and East Africa, the rainy season can dump 50 millimetres in an hour. If your greenhouse floor becomes a pond, your plants will drown and fungal diseases will explode. Build a shallow drainage channel around the uphill side of the greenhouse to divert runoff. Inside, raise your growing beds 15 to 20 centimetres above the walking path. The third factor is wind. Greenhouses are light structures. A strong gust can tear the polythene cover or collapse the frame. In the Sahel, the Harmattan wind is dry and dusty. In southern Africa, summer thunderstorms bring violent downdrafts. In coastal Kenya and Tanzania, monsoon winds are steady and strong. Orient the long axis of the greenhouse perpendicular to the prevailing strong winds, and plant a windbreak of fast growing trees such as Casuarina, Grevillea, or moringa 10 to 15 metres away on the windward side. Do not plant trees so close that they shade the greenhouse. The fourth factor is sunlight. Vegetables need 6 to 8 hours of direct sunlight per day. Avoid sites shaded by tall buildings, mature trees, or hills. In equatorial regions, the sun is intense, so a little afternoon shade can actually help, but morning sun is essential to dry leaves and reduce disease. The fifth factor is soil. You can grow in poor soil if you use raised beds with compost, but starting with decent soil saves money. Avoid heavy clay that waterlogs and pure sand that drains too fast. A loam or sandy loam with organic matter is ideal. Test the soil pH. Most vegetables prefer pH 6.0 to 6.8. If your soil is below 5.5, add agricultural lime. If above 7.5, add compost and elemental sulphur. The sixth factor is access and security. You need a path that a motorcycle, wheelbarrow, or small truck can use to bring in manure, compost, and inputs and to carry out harvests. If buyers cannot reach you, your market is limited. Security matters too. Greenhouses contain valuable drip lines, pumps, and crops. A site near your home or within a fenced compound reduces theft. The seventh factor is market proximity. The whole point of greenhouse farming is to grow high value crops for sale. If your farm is 4 hours from the nearest town, your tomatoes will bruise and rot before they reach the buyer. Aim to be within 1 hour of a market, hotel, school, or urban centre. Now let us talk about preparation. Once you have selected the site, clear the land of grass, bushes, and roots. Do not burn everything; compost the organic matter. Level the ground with a rake and a spirit level. Mark the corners of the greenhouse with pegs and string. Dig holes for the corner posts and intermediate posts. In most African greenhouses, wooden poles treated with used engine oil or creosote last longer, but galvanised steel is better if you can afford it. Set posts at least 60 centimetres deep in concrete for stability. The floor of the greenhouse should be covered with a weed barrier fabric or a thick layer of gravel to suppress weeds and reduce mud. If you use gravel, make sure it does not block the drainage. Inside, lay out your beds. A common design is beds 1 metre wide with 50 centimetre paths between them. This allows you to reach the centre of each bed from either side. If you use drip irrigation, install the main line and sub-main lines before you fill the beds. Test the system for leaks. Install a filter to prevent clogging. If you use a tank, place it on a raised platform so gravity can feed the drip lines, or use a small solar pump. Solar pumps are increasingly affordable in Africa and save on fuel. Finally, think about the nursery. You need a clean, shaded area near the greenhouse to raise seedlings. A small nursery with a fine mesh net and a raised bench keeps seedlings safe from pests and heavy rain. In summary, site selection and preparation are not glamorous, but they determine whether your greenhouse business succeeds. Take your time, test your water and soil, plan your drainage, and build for the long term. In the next lesson, we will look at climate control inside the greenhouse, including temperature, humidity, and ventilation. Before you move on, write down the answers to these questions for your own farm: Where is my water source and how many litres per day can it supply? What is the slope of my land and where will rainwater go? What is the prevailing wind direction in the rainy season? How many hours of direct sun does my site get? What is my soil pH? How far is my nearest market? Answering these questions honestly will save you thousands of dollars and many months of frustration.

2. Climate Control and Ventilation in Tropical Greenhouses

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 2. In this lesson we tackle the invisible forces that make or break a greenhouse crop: temperature, humidity, airflow, and shade. Many African farmers build a greenhouse, close it up tight, and then wonder why their tomatoes cook or their cucumbers get powdery mildew. A greenhouse is not a sealed box. It is a tool for managing the microclimate. Your job is to keep the inside conditions close to the ideal range for your crop while keeping pests out. Let us start with temperature. Most greenhouse vegetables, including tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and leafy greens, grow best between 18 and 28 degrees Celsius during the day and 15 to 20 degrees Celsius at night. Above 32 degrees, pollen becomes sterile in tomatoes and peppers, so flowers drop and you get no fruit. Above 35 degrees, many plants stop growing altogether. In the lowlands of West Africa, East Africa, and southern Africa, midday temperatures can reach 38 to 42 degrees outside. Inside a poorly ventilated greenhouse, it can be 10 degrees hotter. That is a disaster. The first tool for temperature control is ventilation. You need openings at the bottom and at the top of the greenhouse. Hot air rises and escapes through the roof vents, and cooler air is drawn in from the sides. This is called the chimney effect. In a simple African greenhouse, you can achieve this with roll-up side curtains and a ridge vent or with open gable ends covered in insect net. The key is that the openings must be covered with insect net, ideally 40 to 50 mesh, to keep out whiteflies, aphids, thrips, and Tuta absoluta. Without netting, you have a very expensive shade house, not a greenhouse. The second tool is shade. In equatorial and tropical regions, you may need to reduce sunlight by 30 to 50 percent during the hottest part of the day. You can use a shade net rated at 30 percent or 50 percent, placed on top of the greenhouse or inside under the roof. Do not use black plastic shade net that blocks too much light, because plants will become leggy and yields will drop. A white or aluminised shade net reflects heat and is better than black in hot climates. You can also paint the roof with a diluted whitewash, but this washes off in rain. The third tool is water. Misting or fogging the air inside the greenhouse can lower temperatures by 3 to 5 degrees through evaporation. A simple misting line with fine nozzles, run for a few minutes at midday, can save your crop. But be careful: too much mist raises humidity and encourages fungal diseases. Run the mist only when humidity is below 70 percent and the temperature is above 30 degrees. The fourth tool is the orientation and shape of the greenhouse. A greenhouse with a high roof, at least 3.5 to 4 metres at the ridge, holds more air and stays cooler than a low tunnel. In hot climates, a curved or gable roof with a large ridge vent works better than a flat roof. Orient the greenhouse east to west so that the long side faces the sun, but in very hot areas, orient it north to south to reduce the area exposed to midday sun. Now let us talk about humidity. Most vegetables prefer relative humidity between 60 and 80 percent. Below 50 percent, plants lose water faster than they can take it up, and leaves may curl or scorch. Above 85 percent, fungal diseases such as downy mildew, late blight, and botrytis thrive. In the rainy season, humidity inside a greenhouse can stay above 90 percent for days. To manage humidity, you need airflow. Open the vents in the morning as soon as the temperature starts to rise. Close them in the late afternoon before the temperature drops, to trap some warmth for the night. Avoid overhead watering in the evening. Use drip irrigation, which keeps the foliage dry. If you see water dripping from the roof, you have condensation, which means humidity is too high. Increase ventilation and reduce watering. Air circulation inside the greenhouse is also important. Even with vents open, pockets of stale air can sit between plants. A small solar-powered fan or a 12-volt fan connected to a battery can move air and reduce disease. In larger greenhouses, a horizontal airflow fan is a good investment. The goal is to keep leaves moving slightly, not to create a windstorm. Now let us discuss the specific challenges of different African regions. In the Sahel, the main challenge is extreme heat and low humidity. You need shade, misting, and heavy mulching to conserve water. In the humid forest zones of West and Central Africa, the challenge is high humidity and heavy rain. You need a steep roof, good drainage, and wide ventilation. In the highlands of East Africa, the challenge is cool nights and strong winds. You need a greenhouse that can be closed at night to retain heat and a windbreak to protect the structure. In southern Africa, the challenge is seasonal: hot dry summers and cold winters. You may need a greenhouse that can be closed in winter and opened in summer. In all regions, you must monitor conditions. Buy a simple max-min thermometer and a hygrometer. Record the temperature and humidity every morning and evening. After a few weeks, you will see patterns. You will know when to open vents, when to run the mist, and when to shade. This data is also useful for planning your crop calendar. For example, if your greenhouse regularly hits 35 degrees in October, do not plant tomatoes then. Plant heat-tolerant crops like amaranth, okra, or sweet pepper, or wait until the cooler months. Let us also talk about the greenhouse cover. Polyethylene film is the most common cover in Africa. It should be UV-stabilised, because the tropical sun destroys ordinary plastic in a few months. A 200-micron UV-treated film can last 3 to 5 years. Clean the film regularly with a soft brush and water to remove dust, which can reduce light transmission by 30 percent or more. Do not use soap or harsh chemicals. If the film tears, repair it immediately with greenhouse repair tape. A small tear can become a large rip in the next wind. Insect netting is the other critical component. Use 40 to 50 mesh net on all openings. Inspect it for holes and repair them. Whitefly and thrips can squeeze through a 32 mesh net, so do not compromise. Finally, consider the floor. A bare soil floor releases moisture and can be muddy. A gravel or weed mat floor keeps the greenhouse cleaner and reduces humidity. If you use gravel, choose rounded stones that are easy to walk on. In summary, climate control is about balance. You want to keep heat in when it is cold and let it out when it is hot. You want to keep humidity high enough for growth but low enough to prevent disease. You want to keep pests out but let air in. This requires daily attention, but it is not complicated. A well-managed greenhouse in Africa can produce tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers all year round, even when open-field farmers are struggling. That is your competitive advantage. 3. Irrigation and Fertigation for Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 3. Water and nutrients are the fuel of your greenhouse business. In this lesson, we will cover how to design and manage a drip irrigation system, how to calculate water needs, and how to mix and apply fertiliser through the system, a practice called fertigation. If you get this right, your plants will grow fast and healthy, and your yields will be high. If you get it wrong, you will waste water, burn your plants, and lose money. Let us start with the water source and storage. You need a tank or reservoir that can hold at least one day of water for your whole greenhouse. For a 8 by 15 metre greenhouse with 500 tomato plants, plan for 3,000 to 4,000 litres per day in peak season. A 5,000 litre tank is a good minimum. The tank should be raised at least 1.5 to 2 metres above the ground so that gravity can push water through the drip lines. If you cannot raise it that high, you need a pump. Solar pumps are ideal for Africa because they need no fuel and little maintenance. A 200 to 300 watt solar panel connected to a DC pump can fill a 5,000 litre tank from a well or river in a few hours. If you use a petrol or diesel pump, calculate the fuel cost and add it to your budget. Water quality matters. Test for salinity, pH, and iron. If the water is salty, you may need to leach the soil periodically with fresh water. If it has high iron, it can clog drip lines. A simple sand filter and a screen filter are essential. The sand filter removes organic matter and particles, and the screen filter catches finer debris. Clean the screen filter every week. Now let us design the drip system. The main components are the tank, the pump or gravity feed, the filter, the main line, the sub-main lines, the drip lines, and the emitters. For a small greenhouse, you can use a 32 millimetre main line and 16 millimetre drip lines. The drip lines are laid along the beds, one line per row of plants. The emitters are spaced 30 to 50 centimetres apart, depending on the crop. For tomatoes, use 30 centimetres. For leafy greens, use 20 centimetres. Each emitter releases 2 to 4 litres per hour. You need to calculate the flow rate. If you have 500 emitters at 2 litres per hour, that is 1,000 litres per hour. If your tank is 5,000 litres, you can run the system for 5 hours. But you do not want to run it all at once. Split the greenhouse into two or three zones and irrigate one zone at a time. This reduces the flow rate needed and gives you better control. The number of drip lines and emitters depends on your plant spacing. For tomatoes, a common spacing is 50 centimetres between plants in the row and 80 to 100 centimetres between rows. If you have 10 rows of 50 plants, that is 500 plants. Each plant needs its own emitter, or you can use one emitter for two plants if the soil is good. But do not crowd the emitters. Now let us talk about how much water to apply. This depends on the crop, the stage of growth, and the weather. A young seedling needs only 0.5 to 1 litre per day. A mature tomato plant in hot weather needs 4 to 6 litres per day. A cucumber plant needs 3 to 5 litres. Leafy greens need 1 to 2 litres. You can measure this by checking the soil. Dig down 15 centimetres with your hand. If the soil is moist but not soggy, you are doing well. If it is dry, increase irrigation. If it is waterlogged, reduce it. You can also use a tensiometer, a simple device that measures soil moisture, but it is not essential for a small greenhouse. The best time to irrigate is early morning. This gives plants water before the heat of the day and allows leaves to dry before evening. Avoid irrigating in the late afternoon or evening, because wet foliage at night encourages disease. If you must irrigate twice a day in very hot weather, do it in the morning and early afternoon. Now let us move to fertigation. Fertigation is the practice of injecting soluble fertiliser into the irrigation water. It saves labour, delivers nutrients directly to the root zone, and allows you to adjust the feed to the crop stage. The first rule of fertigation is to use only fully soluble fertilisers. Do not use manure or compost in the drip system, because it will clog the emitters. Use products like calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, monoammonium phosphate, and magnesium sulphate. These are available in most African agro-dealer shops, though you may need to order them. You can also use a complete water-soluble fertiliser blend designed for vegetables. The second rule is to mix the fertiliser in a separate tank, not in your main water tank. This is called a stock tank. Mix a concentrated solution in the stock tank, then use a venturi injector or a dosing pump to draw it into the irrigation line. A simple venturi injector is cheap and works with gravity or a pump. If you cannot find one, you can use a bucket and a siphon, but it is less accurate. The third rule is to calculate the dose. A general schedule for tomatoes in a greenhouse is: week 1 after transplanting, 50 grams of NPK 20-20-20 per 100 litres of water. Week 3, increase to 100 grams. Week 5, switch to a higher potassium formula like NPK 15-5-30, 150 grams per 100 litres. Week 8, add calcium nitrate at 100 grams per 100 litres to prevent blossom end rot. Always follow the instructions on the fertiliser bag and adjust based on leaf colour and plant vigour. The fourth rule is to flush the system. After each fertigation, run clear water through the drip lines for 10 to 15 minutes. This prevents salt buildup in the emitters. Once a week, flush the main line and the drip lines with clean water. If you see white crust on the emitters, you have salt accumulation. Remove the end caps and flush until the water runs clear. The fifth rule is to monitor electrical conductivity, or EC. EC measures the concentration of salts in the water. Most vegetables grow well at EC 1.5 to 2.5 millisiemens per centimetre. If EC is too low, plants are hungry. If too high, roots burn. You can buy a handheld EC meter for a modest price. Test the water coming out of the emitters, not just the stock tank. Now let us talk about water conservation. Mulching is your best friend. A layer of dry grass, straw, or plastic mulch on the beds reduces evaporation by 50 percent or more. It also suppresses weeds and keeps the soil temperature stable. In hot African climates, mulching can cut your water use in half. You can also use shade net to reduce evaporation from the soil and transpiration from the leaves. And you can collect rainwater from the greenhouse roof. A 8 by 15 metre roof can collect 1,000 litres from just 10 millimetres of rain. That is free water. Install gutters and a downpipe into a storage tank. Finally, keep records. Write down how much water you use, how much fertiliser you mix, and how the plants respond. After one season, you will have your own custom schedule that works for your farm. This is the difference between a hobby and a business. 4. Integrated Pest and Disease Management in Greenhouses

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 4. Pests and diseases are the biggest threat to your greenhouse investment. A single outbreak of whitefly can transmit viruses that wipe out your entire tomato crop. A few days of high humidity can trigger late blight that destroys your potatoes and tomatoes. But you are not helpless. In this lesson, we will cover integrated pest management, or IPM, which combines prevention, monitoring, biological control, and safe chemical use. The goal is to keep pests and diseases below the level that causes economic damage, not to eliminate every insect. Let us start with prevention. The first line of defence is the greenhouse structure itself. Your insect net must be intact and at least 40 to 50 mesh. Check it every week for holes, especially near the door and vents. Repair holes with netting patches and cable ties. The door should have a double layer of net or a vestibule, so that when you enter, insects do not fly straight in. Do not allow weeds to grow around the greenhouse, because they harbour pests. Keep the area around the greenhouse clean and mowed. The second line of defence is clean planting material. Buy certified seed or seedlings from a reputable nursery. Do not take cuttings from infected plants. If you raise your own seedlings, use sterilised trays and clean water. Do not reuse soil that has had diseased plants. The third line of defence is crop rotation. Do not grow the same crop in the same greenhouse season after season. Rotate tomatoes with cucumbers, peppers with leafy greens, and so on. This breaks the life cycle of soil-borne pests and diseases. If you have a serious soil-borne disease like bacterial wilt, you may need to remove the soil and replace it with fresh soil or grow in bags with clean media. The fourth line of defence is sanitation. Remove and destroy diseased plants immediately. Do not compost them unless you have a hot compost system that reaches 60 degrees Celsius. Clean your tools with a disinfectant solution, such as a 10 percent bleach solution, between uses. Wear clean clothes and shoes in the greenhouse. Do not smoke near the greenhouse, because tobacco can carry tobacco mosaic virus. Now let us talk about monitoring. You cannot manage what you do not measure. Walk through your greenhouse at least twice a week. Look at the underside of leaves, the growing tips, and the flowers. Use yellow sticky traps to monitor flying insects like whitefly, aphids, and thrips. Hang one trap every 10 square metres. Check the traps weekly and count the insects. If you see a sudden increase, act immediately. Also look for signs of disease: yellowing, wilting, spots, mould, or powdery growth. Learn to identify the common problems. In African greenhouses, the most common pests are whitefly, aphids, thrips, spider mites, leafminers, and Tuta absoluta. The most common diseases are powdery mildew, downy mildew, late blight, early blight, fusarium wilt, and bacterial wilt. Now let us discuss biological control. This is the use of living organisms to control pests. It is safe, sustainable, and increasingly available in Africa. For whitefly, you can release Encarsia formosa, a tiny parasitic wasp. For aphids, you can release ladybirds or lacewings. For spider mites, you can release Phytoseiulus persimilis, a predatory mite. These beneficial insects are available from commercial insectaries in Kenya, South Africa, and some West African countries. They are more expensive than chemicals, but they do not leave residues and they can establish a permanent population in your greenhouse. You can also use botanical pesticides. Neem oil, made from the neem tree, is effective against many soft-bodied insects. Mix 5 millilitres of neem oil with 1 litre of water and a few drops of liquid soap, and spray in the evening. Garlic and chilli sprays also repel pests. These are cheap and easy to make, but they must be applied frequently and they do not kill eggs. Now let us talk about chemical pesticides. Sometimes you must use them, especially during a severe outbreak. But use them as a last resort, and use them safely. Always read the label. Choose a product that is registered for your crop and your pest. Wear protective clothing: gloves, a mask, long sleeves, and trousers. Do not spray in the heat of the day or when it is windy. Spray in the early morning or late evening. Follow the pre-harvest interval, which is the number of days you must wait after spraying before you can harvest. This is usually 3 to 14 days. Never spray on the day you harvest. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Do not mix chemicals unless the label says it is safe. Store pesticides in a locked cabinet away from children, food, and animal feed. Dispose of empty containers safely. Do not burn them or throw them in the river. Now let us look at specific disease management. Powdery mildew appears as white powder on leaves. It thrives in warm, dry conditions with high humidity at night. To control it, increase airflow, avoid overhead watering, and spray with potassium bicarbonate or a sulphur-based fungicide. Downy mildew appears as yellow patches on the upper leaf surface and grey mould on the underside. It thrives in cool, wet conditions. Remove infected leaves, increase ventilation, and use a copper-based fungicide. Late blight appears as dark, water-soaked spots on leaves and stems, and white mould on the underside. It can destroy a crop in days. Remove and destroy infected plants immediately. Use a protective fungicide before the disease appears if the weather is cool and wet. Bacterial wilt causes sudden wilting of the whole plant while the leaves remain green. There is no chemical cure. Remove the plant, including the roots, and destroy it. Do not plant susceptible crops in that spot for at least two years. Now let us talk about the business side. Every pest and disease problem costs you money. You lose yield, you spend on chemicals, and you spend time. IPM reduces these costs. A healthy crop also gets a better price. Buyers, especially hotels and supermarkets, prefer vegetables with no chemical residues. If you can document your IPM practices, you can sell to premium markets. Keep a spray diary. Write down the date, the pest, the product, the dose, and the pre-harvest interval. This record is also useful for traceability. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who enters the greenhouse should know the signs of pests and diseases and should report them immediately. A worker who spots whitefly on a sticky trap on Monday can save you a spray on Friday. In summary, IPM is a system, not a single product. It combines a strong structure, clean seed, rotation, sanitation, monitoring, biological control, and careful chemical use. It takes discipline, but it pays off. 5. Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 5. You have built your greenhouse, managed the climate, irrigated and fed your plants, and protected them from pests and diseases. Now comes the moment of truth: harvest and sale. This is where you turn your hard work into money. But harvesting is not just picking. It is a series of decisions that determine the quality, shelf life, and price of your produce. In this lesson, we will cover when and how to harvest, how to handle produce after harvest, and how to find and keep profitable markets in Africa. Let us start with harvest timing. Every crop has a peak moment. Tomatoes for fresh market should be harvested at the breaker stage, when they are 10 to 30 percent coloured. They will ripen during transport and arrive at the market firm and attractive. If you harvest fully red tomatoes, they bruise easily and have a short shelf life. If you harvest green tomatoes, they may never ripen properly. For cherry tomatoes, harvest when fully coloured, because they are usually sold in punnets and eaten fresh. Peppers should be harvested when they are firm and fully sized. Green bell peppers are harvested before they turn red or yellow. If you want coloured peppers, wait until they change colour, but note that the plant will stop producing new fruit while the old fruit ripens. Cucumbers should be harvested young and tender, before the seeds harden. A cucumber can double in size in two days, so check every day. Leafy greens like lettuce, spinach, and kale should be harvested in the cool of the morning. Cut them with a sharp knife, leaving the roots in the ground if you want a second cut. Do not harvest wet leaves, because they rot quickly. Now let us talk about harvest technique. Use clean, sharp tools. A blunt knife or scissors will crush stems and create entry points for disease. Harvest into clean crates or baskets. Do not use sacks that have held fertiliser or chemicals. Do not pile produce too high, because the weight will bruise the bottom layers. For tomatoes, harvest into shallow crates, no more than three layers deep. For leafy greens, harvest into crates and keep them in the shade. Never leave harvested produce in the sun. In tropical Africa, the sun can heat a crate of tomatoes to 40 degrees Celsius in 20 minutes, and that heat will reduce shelf life by days. Move produce to a cool, shaded area immediately. Now let us discuss post-harvest handling. The goal is to slow down respiration and prevent moisture loss. The first step is to remove field heat. If you have a cold room, that is ideal, but most small farmers do not. You can use evaporative cooling. This is a simple technique: place your crates in a shaded, well-ventilated area and cover them with a damp cloth or place them near a fan that blows over wet sand. Evaporative cooling can lower the temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius. It is cheap and effective. The second step is sorting and grading. Remove any damaged, diseased, or undersized produce. Buyers pay more for uniform, clean produce. Grade your tomatoes into large, medium, and small. Grade peppers by size and colour. Grade leafy greens by leaf size and freshness. This takes time, but it increases your price. The third step is packaging. Use clean, food-grade crates or boxes. For tomatoes, a 20 kilogram crate is common in many African markets. For leafy greens, use smaller crates or bundles tied with string. If you are selling to supermarkets or hotels, they may require specific packaging, such as punnets for cherry tomatoes or sealed bags for salad mixes. Ask your buyer what they want. The fourth step is storage. If you cannot sell immediately, store your produce in the coolest place you have. Do not store tomatoes with onions or apples, because they release ethylene gas that speeds ripening. Do not store leafy greens with tomatoes for the same reason. Keep different crops separate. If you have a solar-powered fan, use it to circulate air in the storage room. Now let us talk about markets. There are several market channels for greenhouse vegetables in Africa. The first is the local open market. This is the easiest to access, but prices are low and volatile. You can sell directly to consumers or to market traders. 2. Climate Control and Ventilation

Temperature management is the single most critical factor in African greenhouses. Optimal daytime temperatures for tomato, pepper, and cucumber range from 22â28Â°C. Above 32Â°C, pollen viability drops sharply; above 35Â°C, fruit set fails. In the Sahel, internal temperatures can exceed 45Â°C without active cooling.

Passive ventilation is the first line of defence. Ridge vents combined with roll-up side curtains can achieve 20â40 air changes per hour, sufficient for most highland and mid-altitude zones. Drip irrigation is the standard for greenhouse vegetable production in Africa. It delivers water directly to the root zone, reducing evaporation losses by 30â50% compared to furrow irrigation. In water-scarce regions such as Kenya's Machakos County or northern Nigeria, this efficiency is decisive for profitability.

4. Integrated Pest and Disease Management

Greenhouse environments in Africa favour rapid pest multiplication because of continuous cropping, high humidity, and limited natural enemies. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is not optionalâit is a business necessity. The FAO estimates that whitefly-transmitted viruses alone can cause 40â70% yield loss in tomato greenhouses in sub-Saharan Africa.

Key IPM practices for African greenhouses:

  • Sanitation: Remove and destroy crop debris between cycles. Solarise soil or growing media where feasible.
  • Screening: Install 40â50 mesh insect netting on all vents and doors. This alone can reduce whitefly ingress by 80â90%.
  • Monitoring: Use yellow sticky traps (whitefly, aphids) and blue sticky traps (thrips) at 1 trap per 50 mÂ². Scout twice weekly.
  • Biological control: Introduce Encarsia formosa for whitefly, Phytoseiulus persimilis for spider mites, and Bacillus thuringiensis for lepidopteran larvae. These are commercially available in Kenya, South Africa, and Egypt.
  • Targeted chemical control: Use only when thresholds are exceeded, rotate modes of action, and observe PHI.

5. Harvesting, Post-Harvest, and Marketing

Harvest timing determines shelf life. Tomatoes for distant markets should be harvested at the breaker or pink stage; for local markets, at light red. Cucumbers and peppers are harvested at full size but before colour change. In African conditions, where cold chain infrastructure is often limited, harvesting in the early morning and pre-cooling with evaporative coolers or shade can extend shelf life by 2â4 days.

Grading and packaging are critical for market access. Supermarkets in Nairobi, Accra, and Lagos require uniform size, colour, and blemish-free produce. Use clean, ventilated cratesânever reuse pesticide containers. For export markets, GlobalG.A.P. certification is increasingly mandatory.
