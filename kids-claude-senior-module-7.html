Pricing AI Work - What Is Your Time Worth?

~13 min read20 min gameAdvanced
Real customers

🧠 Brain Opener

A common mistake is to price AI work low because the AI does most of the typing. This misses the point. The client is paying for the result, not the keystrokes.

  • Why is it wrong to price based on how long the AI took?
  • What is value-based pricing?
  • How do you find out what a client will pay?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

The client is paying for the result. If your AI-assisted work saves them three hours of their own time, they will pay for three hours, not for the ten minutes you spent typing. Value-based pricing means charging what the result is worth to the client.

📖 Amara Names Her Price

Amara, fifteen, in Lagos, had just finished her first AI-assisted project - a product catalogue for a small jewellery maker. Sixty items, each with a photo, a description, and a price. The whole thing had taken her three hours, mostly because she had been careful with the details.

The client asked: "How much?" Amara panicked. She had not thought about the price. She said the first number that came to mind: "Fifteen dollars."

The client paid without arguing. Amara felt sick. She had just made fifteen dollars for three hours of careful work. That was five dollars an hour. She could have made more selling water.

She told her aunt, who ran a small business. Her aunt laughed, not unkindly. "You priced the hours," she said. "You should price the value."

"What does that mean?"

"What does the client get? A catalogue with sixty items. What would it cost her to make it herself? Twenty hours. What is twenty hours of her time worth? At least five hundred dollars. You charged fifteen. You are not wrong - you are just cheap."

Amara thought about it. The next week another jeweller asked her for the same thing. This time she asked the client: "What would it cost you to make this yourself?" The client said, "Two weeks and four hundred dollars if I hired someone."

Amara quoted eighty dollars. The client paid. Amara delivered in three hours. The client referred two more jewellers.

Amara learned the lesson. The price was not for the three hours. The price was for the two weeks and four hundred dollars the client did not have to spend.

💡 The Big Idea

Price the value, not the hours

Value-based pricing is simple: ask what the result is worth to the client. If they would pay four hundred dollars to get it done another way, you can charge eighty and still be an obvious bargain. This is what makes AI-assisted work profitable. The client is paying for the result, not the keystrokes.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

Cost, time, and value

There are three ways to set a price. Cost-plus: what it costs you plus a margin. Useful when your costs are significant. Time-based: your hourly rate times the hours you spent. Simple, but it punishes you for getting faster. Value-based: what the result is worth to the client. This is the professional method, and it is the one that works best with AI-assisted work.

For AI work, always ask: what would it cost the client to get this some other way? If they could hire someone for four hundred dollars, charge eighty. If they could do it themselves in an afternoon, charge thirty. The number is not about your hours. It is about their alternatives.

2

How to find out what a client will pay

You cannot guess. You have to ask. During the brief, ask: what is your budget for this? If they will not say, ask: what would it cost you to do this without me? Their answer is your upper limit.

You also learn from the market. Charge low on your first three projects - not to be cheap, but to learn. Raise your price by 20 percent for each new client until someone says no. When someone says no, you are at the top of your market. Stay there.

3

How to raise your price without losing clients

When you raise your price, explain what has changed: "I have raised my rate because the last three projects took longer than expected and I want to give every client the same quality." Most existing clients accept a small raise. New clients only know the new price.

The biggest mistake is undercharging for too long. Undercharging attracts cheap clients who complain a lot and refer other cheap clients. Raising your price slowly filters your client list. Within a year, you are earning three times as much for the same work.

🛠 Hands-On - Price Three Real Jobs

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • Three real potential clients or three real jobs
  1. For each job, ask the client: what would it cost you to get this done another way?
  2. Write their answer. That is the upper limit.
  3. Write down how many hours the job will take you.
  4. Write down what you want to earn per hour for AI-assisted work ($10 to $30 is a good range for beginners).
  5. Multiply hours by hourly rate. Compare with the upper limit.
  6. Quote the higher of the two - but never above the upper limit.
Why it works: Cost-plus, time-based, and value-based all give different numbers. The right price is the one the client accepts without feeling cheated.

🎮 Game - Price It Right

3 to 10 players - 20 min

The teacher describes five jobs: a poster, a 60-item catalogue, an event plan, a three-page report, a social media launch. Everyone writes a price for each job. Then the teacher reveals what a real client paid for the same job. The player closest to the real price wins each round. Highest total wins the game. After each round, discuss why the winning price was higher or lower than most guesses.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

AI can help you price work - but only if you give it the right information.

Try this prompt: "I am a 15-year-old offering a 60-item product catalogue to a small jewellery maker. Making it myself would take them 20 hours. Hiring someone would cost 400 dollars. How should I price this? Give me three pricing options with reasoning."

Check the reasoning against your own. Then decide what to quote. The final decision is yours, not the AI's.

🏆 Project Challenge

Price Ten Real Jobs

Price ten real potential jobs. Get at least one client to say yes.

  1. List ten real things people need - a poster, a menu, a report, a plan.
  2. For each one, ask the potential client: what would it cost you to get this done another way?
  3. Write their answer as the upper limit.
  4. Write your hours and hourly rate. Multiply.
  5. Quote the higher number, capped at the upper limit.
  6. Track what each client says: yes, no, or counter.
  7. After ten quotes, look at the pattern. You now know your market price.
