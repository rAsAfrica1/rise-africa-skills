1. Clothing and Colors Vocabulary

Start by learning the names of common clothing items in Mandarin. For a shirt, say (chnshn). Trousers are (kzi). A dress is (linyqn). Shoes are (xi). A hat is (m ozi). A bag is (bo). These are the words you will use in markets from Lagos to Nairobi to Johannesburg. Practice saying them aloud. In African markets, you might see second-hand clothing from China, so knowing these words helps you ask for what you want.

Colors are equally important. Red is (hngs). Blue is (lns). Green is (ls). Yellow is (hungs). Black is (his). White is (bis). To say a red shirt, put the color before the noun: (hng chnshn). For a blue dress: (ln linyqn).

In many African markets, you will find fabrics like Ankara or Kente. You can ask for (b) meaning cloth. If you want a specific color, say (w y o hngs de b) I want red cloth. The word (de) links the color to the noun. Practice with a friend: point to items and name them in Mandarin.

Numbers are also useful when shopping. Learn 1 to 10: y, r, sn, s, w, li, q, b, ji, sh. You will need these for prices and quantities. For example, (sn ji n chnshn) means three shirts. The measure word (ji n) is used for clothing items. For trousers, use (tio): (ling tio kzi) two trousers.

Finally, practice asking ? (zh sh shnme yns?) What color is this? And (w xhun zhge yns) I like this color. Use these phrases in real shops or with online sellers. Consistency is key: spend 10 minutes daily naming items around you in Mandarin.

2. Sizes, Trying On, and Fitting Rooms

When buying clothes, you need to know sizes. In Mandarin, small is (xio), medium is (zhng), and large is (d ). Extra large is (jid ) or (td ). To ask for your size, say ? (yu miyu d h o?) Do you have a large size? The word (h o) means size. For shoes, sizes are often in European numbers, but you can ask 38 ? (yu miyu snshb m?) Do you have size 38? (m) is used for shoe sizes.

Trying on clothes is common in markets and shops. To ask to try something on, say ? (w ky shchun ma?) Can I try it on? The verb (shchun) means to try on. If you want to try a specific item, point and say (w xing shchun zh ji n) I want to try this one. For trousers, use (zh tio).

Fitting rooms are called (shyjin). In many African markets, there may not be a formal fitting room, but you can ask ? (nl ky shchun?) Where can I try it on? Often, vendors will let you try over your clothes or behind a curtain. If you need a mirror, ask ? (yu jngzi ma?) Do you have a mirror?

When trying on, check the fit. If it's too tight, say (t i jn le). If it's too loose, say (t i sng le). If it's just right, say (hsh). These phrases help you communicate clearly. In bargaining, knowing the fit helps you decide whether to negotiate. If it doesn't fit, you can say (b hsh) and walk away.

Remember to be polite. In many African cultures, politeness is key. Use (qng) when asking, and (xixie) when done. Practice these phrases with a partner. Role-play a scenario: you are in a shop, you ask for a size, try it on, and decide. This builds confidence for real interactions.

3. Asking for Prices and Discounts

Asking for prices is the first step in any shopping trip. In Mandarin, ? (dusho qin?) means How much money? You can point to an item and say ? (zhge dusho qin?) How much is this? For multiple items, say ? (zhxi dusho qin?) How much are these? The word (qin) means money.

In African markets, prices are often not fixed. You will need to ask for a discount. The phrase for discount is (dzh) or (pinyi ydin) cheaper a little. To ask for a lower price, say ? (ky pinyi ydin ma?) Can it be a little cheaper? Or (t i gu le, pinyi din ba) Too expensive, make it cheaper. The word (gu) means expensive, and (pinyi) means cheap.

Numbers are crucial. Practice saying prices in Mandarin. For example, (wsh ku i) means 50 yuan or 50 of the local currency. In many African countries, you might use local currency like naira, shillings, or rand, but the Mandarin numbers remain the same. Learn to count from 1 to 100. For 100, say (ybi). For 200, (lingbi). For 500, (wbi).

When bargaining, start with a counter-offer. If the vendor says 100, you can say 50? (wsh ky ma?) Is 50 okay? Often, you will meet in the middle. Use phrases like (zhge ji g bxng) This price won't do. Or (w z i k n k n) I'll look around more, to signal you might walk away.

In African markets, bargaining is expected. Be friendly and respectful. A smile goes a long way. Also, learn to ask ? (zud dusho qin?) What's the lowest price? This shows you are serious. Practice with a friend: one plays vendor, one plays buyer. Use real numbers and items. This will prepare you for real negotiations.

4. Bargaining Phrases and Etiquette

Bargaining in Mandarin requires both language and cultural awareness. In many African markets, bargaining is a social activity. Start with a greeting: (n ho) Hello. Then ask about the item: ? (zhge znme m i?) How do you sell this? The vendor will give a price. Your response: (t i gu le) Too expensive. Then counter: (pinyi ydin ba) Make it a bit cheaper.

Key phrases: ? (ky sho ydin ma?) Can you reduce a little? (z i sho din) A little less. (zhge ji g t i go le) This price is too high. (w mi ling g, pinyi din) I'll buy two, make it cheaper. Vendors may say (bxng) No, or (zhge ji g hn pinyi le) This price is already very cheap.

Etiquette: Don't be aggressive. In many African cultures, bargaining is done with a smile. Use (qng) and (xixie). If you walk away, do it politely: (w z i k n k n) I'll look around more. Sometimes the vendor will call you back with a lower price. Also, don't start bargaining if you don't intend to buy. It wastes time.

In some markets, like in Nairobi or Accra, vendors may speak some Mandarin due to Chinese imports. But don't assume; use simple phrases. If you don't understand, say (qng shu m n ydin) Please speak slower. Or (w tng b dng) I don't understand.

Practice bargaining for a souvenir. For example, a wood carving priced at 200. You say 100? (ybi ky ma?) Is 100 okay? Vendor: 150. You: 120. Finally agree at 130. Use phrases like (chngjio) Deal! This is the fun part. Remember, bargaining is not a fight; it's a conversation. Build rapport and you'll get a better price.

5. Paying: Cash, Card, and Mobile Payment

After agreeing on a price, you need to pay. In Mandarin, cash is (xi njn). Card is (k). Mobile payment is (shuj zhf). In many African countries, mobile money like M-Pesa is common. In China, WeChat Pay and Alipay are standard. Learn to say ? (w ky yng xi njn ma?) Can I use cash? Or ? (ky shu k ma?) Can I swipe a card? For mobile: ? (ky yng shuj zhf ma?) Can I use mobile payment?

If you pay with cash, you might need to ask for change. (zhoqin) means to give change. Say ? (n yu lngqin ma?) Do you have change? Or (qng zho w qin) Please give me change. In markets, vendors may not have change for large notes. Try to carry small bills. In Africa, this is also true. For example, in Nigeria, you might have 1000 naira notes and need smaller ones.

If you pay by card, say (w shu k). The vendor may ask (shr mm) Enter PIN. Or (qinmng) Sign. For mobile payment, you might scan a QR code: (so rwim). Then confirm: (zhf chnggng) Payment successful.

Always ask for a receipt. (shuj) means receipt. Say (qng gi w shuj). This is important for expensive items. In some African markets, receipts may not be common, but it's good to ask.

Finally, thank the vendor: (xixie). If you enjoyed bargaining, say (xi c z i li) I'll come again. Practice these phrases. Role-play paying with different methods. Knowing how to pay smoothly makes shopping easier and builds trust.

6. Role-Play: Buying a Souvenir

Let's put everything together in a role-play. You are at a market in Cape Town or Marrakech. You see a beautiful beaded necklace. You want to buy it as a souvenir. Start with a greeting: (n ho). The vendor replies (n ho). You point and ask: ? (zhge dusho qin?) How much is this? Vendor: 200 (lingbi ku i) 200. You: ! 100? (t i gu le! ybi ky ma?) Too expensive! Is 100 okay? Vendor: 150 (ybi w ba) 150. You: 120, ? (ybi r, chngjio?) 120, deal? Vendor: (ho ba, chngjio) Okay, deal.

Now you need to pay. You say: ? (w ky yng shuj zhf ma?) Can I use mobile payment? Vendor: (ky) Yes. You scan the QR code: (so rwim). You confirm payment: (zhf chnggng). Then ask for a receipt: (qng gi w shuj). Vendor gives it. You say (xixie) and (xi c z i li).

If you want to try on a bracelet, you can ask: ? (w ky shd i ma?) Can I try it on? The vendor says (ky). You try it, it fits. You say (hsh) It fits. Then you negotiate.

Practice this dialogue with a friend. Swap roles. Use real items and real numbers. This builds fluency. In real life, you might encounter different responses, but the structure is similar. Remember to be polite and patient. Bargaining is a skill that improves with practice.

<h2>Practical Reference: Shopping and Bargaining in Chinese An African Buyer's Toolkit</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the <strong>Shopping and Bargaining</strong> module into a single working document for African entrepreneurs, traders, and procurement officers who source clothing, textiles, and souvenirs in Chinese-speaking markets. Whether you are buying wholesale garments in Guangzhou, negotiating fabric in Yiwu, or picking up gifts in Nairobi's Chinatown, the language and etiquette you use directly affects your margin. This reference gives you the vocabulary, the numbers, the payment routes, and the negotiation scripts plus the cultural guardrails to close deals confidently and profitably.</p>

<h3>1. Clothing and Colours: The Vocabulary You Will Actually Use</h3>

<p>Colour and garment vocabulary is the foundation of any clothing transaction. In Chinese wholesale markets, sellers will often quote a price per piece and then ask which colour and size you want. If you cannot name the colour quickly, you lose momentum and the seller may assume you are a tourist rather than a buyer. Learn the core colour terms and the garment nouns before you enter the market.</p>

<h3>2. Sizes, Trying On, and Fitting Rooms</h3>

<p>Chinese sizing runs smaller than European, American, and many African standard sizes. A Chinese "L" () often corresponds to a European "M" or even "S". Always ask to try the item on (? ky shchun ma?) and confirm the fitting room location ( shyjin). In wholesale markets, fitting rooms may be shared or non-existent; in that case, ask the seller to hold the item against you or measure it flat. For bulk orders, request a size chart ( chm bio) in writing so you can compare against your home market measurements before shipping.</p>

<h3>3. Asking for Prices and Discounts</h3>

<p>The core question is ? (dusho qin?) "How much?" For bulk pricing, ask ? (pf ji sh dusho?) "What is the wholesale price?" When the quote is too high, respond with (t i gu le) "Too expensive" and follow with (pinyi ydin) "A little cheaper." A stronger lever is volume: (w mi hn du) "I am buying a lot" or ? (du mi j ji n ky pinyi ma?) "If I buy several pieces, can it be cheaper?"</p>

<h3>4. Bargaining Phrases and Etiquette</h3>

<p>Bargaining in Chinese markets is expected, but it is a social ritual, not a confrontation. Keep it friendly, smile, and never insult the goods. Useful phrases include (z i pinyi ydin ba) "Make it a bit cheaper" and (g r ng y b) "Let's each give a little." If the price is still wrong, thank the seller and walk away slowly; a genuine seller will often call you back with a better offer. In wholesale markets, the price is often already close to the floor, so aggressive bargaining can damage a long-term supplier relationship. For repeat business, prioritise reliability and quality over squeezing the last few yuan.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> Safety Warning Cash, Cards, and Mobile Payment</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Never carry large amounts of cash in open markets. In China, mobile payment (WeChat Pay and Alipay ) is dominant; foreign cards are often not accepted. If you are a foreign buyer, set up WeChat Pay or Alipay with an international card before you travel, or use a licensed currency exchange. Avoid informal "money changers" on the street they are a common source of counterfeit notes and theft. In African markets with Chinese traders, confirm the accepted payment method before agreeing a price, and always ask for a receipt ( shuj) for business records and customs clearance.</p>

<h3>5. Paying: Cash, Card, and Mobile Payment</h3>

<p>Payment method affects your bargaining power. Cash ( xi njn) is still accepted in many markets and can sometimes secure a small discount, but it carries risk. Bank cards ( ynhngk) are accepted in larger stores and malls, but not in most wholesale stalls. Mobile payment via WeChat Pay ( Wixn zhf) and Alipay ( Zhfbo) is the standard in China; as of 2024, both platforms support international cards for foreign visitors, but you must set this up before you arrive. In African markets, mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) is often the fastest route, but Chinese wholesalers may prefer cash or WeChat. Always confirm the exchange rate and any transaction fees before paying.</p>
