Module 9

Marketing, Grading and Selling

The money in fattening is made on the day you sell, but it is decided in the weeks before. This module covers how to tell when an animal is actually finished, how fat cover is judged, the real difference between selling on live weight and carcass weight, how grading systems reward or punish you, and how to negotiate, transport and time a sale without giving away the margin you spent 100 days building.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to assess fat cover and decide whether an animal is finished, under finished or over fed.
  • You will be able to explain the difference between live weight and carcass weight selling and choose the basis that suits your animals.
  • You will be able to find out how your local grading scheme works and what it pays for.
  • You will be able to compare three buyer offers on a common basis before agreeing to anything.
  • You will be able to negotiate from prepared numbers rather than from hope.
  • You will be able to plan transport and marketing timing to limit shrink and catch demand peaks.

Lesson 9.115 min

Knowing When an Animal Is Finished

In this lesson
  • Judge finish by handling, not by eye alone
  • Use the standard touch points on the live animal
  • Decide between selling now and feeding on

Finished does not mean big. It means the animal has laid down enough fat cover for the market you are selling into, without going past it. Selling under finished animals costs you grade and price. Feeding past finish costs you feed at the worst conversion of the whole period and can push you into a fat penalty. Both mistakes are common.

Handle, do not just look

Coat, gut fill and hide thickness all deceive the eye. Fat cover is judged with your hands. Run your palm and fingertips over the same points on every animal, in the same order, every time. The standard live animal touch points are:

  • Last ribs. Feel across the back of the ribcage. On an unfinished animal the individual ribs are sharp and easy to count. As fat lays down, they become progressively harder to feel, then smooth over.
  • Loin and short ribs. The ends of the loin bones go from sharp, to rounded, to buried.
  • Tail head. The hollows either side of the tail head start deep and hard, then soften and fill with a spongy, movable fat pad.
  • Brisket and flank. The brisket fills and drops; the flank thickens and stops swinging loose.
  • Cod or udder area. Fat deposits here late; noticeable fat in the cod usually means the animal is at or past finish.

What the sequence tells you

Cattle lay down fat in a fairly predictable order: around the gut first, then between the muscles, then under the skin, and last as marbling inside the muscle. The external points you can feel are the later ones, which is exactly why handling works. If the ribs are still sharp and the tail head hollows are still hard, the animal is not finished no matter how much it weighs.

The practical decision

Ask three questions at each monthly weighing:

  1. Is the fat cover right for my buyer? A butcher selling to a market that wants lean meat, a supermarket abattoir, and an export buyer can want quite different levels of finish. Ask your buyers what they want before you decide what finished means.
  2. Is the animal still gaining well? Once ADG falls off sharply and conversion worsens badly, the extra kilograms are expensive.
  3. Does the extra kilogram still earn more than it costs? This is the rule from Module 8. Cost of gain against value per kilogram.

Signs you have gone too far

  • Soft, wobbly fat pads either side of the tail head that move under the hand
  • A deep, filled brisket and heavy cod fat
  • ADG falling while intake stays high
  • Animals lying down for long periods and reluctant to move

Over fat animals cost you twice: the feed to put the fat on, and often a lower price, because excess fat is trimmed and discarded at the abattoir. Many grading schemes actively penalise the fattest classes.

Age and sex change the target

Younger animals finish at lighter weights than older, larger framed ones. Bulls and steers of the same age and frame do not lay down fat at the same rate; bulls generally stay leaner for longer and grow more muscle, which is why some markets pay differently for them. Heifers commonly lay fat down earlier and at lighter weights than steers of the same type, so a mixed pen will not all be ready on the same day.

Sort, do not sell the whole pen

Walk the pen with your hands two weeks before you plan to sell and split it: ready, nearly ready, not ready. Sell the ready ones. Feeding a finished animal alongside unfinished penmates just to keep the group together is one of the quietest ways to lose money in this business.

Lesson 9.215 min

Live Weight Versus Carcass Weight Selling

In this lesson
  • Understand who carries the risk under each selling basis
  • Convert between the two bases correctly
  • Choose a basis that suits the animals you have

There are two basic ways to be paid for a finished animal, and mixing them up is the most expensive arithmetic error in cattle marketing.

Live weight selling

You are paid a price per kilogram of the live animal, weighed on a scale, or you agree a price for the animal as a whole. The buyer estimates how much carcass they will get and prices accordingly. Under this basis:

  • You know your money before the animal is slaughtered.
  • The buyer carries the risk that the animal dresses out poorly, so the buyer discounts for that risk.
  • Gut fill matters enormously. An animal weighed full of feed and water weighs more, which is why buyers want to weigh after a stand and why weighing conditions must be agreed in advance.
  • You get no reward for an animal that turns out to be exceptional, because the price was set before anyone saw the carcass.

Carcass weight selling

You are paid per kilogram of the dressed carcass, usually with a grade applied. Under this basis:

  • You carry the dressing risk and the grading risk.
  • A well finished, well muscled animal is rewarded, because it dresses higher and grades better.
  • A poorly finished animal is punished twice: lower dressing percentage and a lower grade price.
  • You do not know your money until after slaughter, and you must trust the weighing and grading.

The conversion, again

Live weight equivalent = carcass price per kg times dressing percent divided by 100

Dressing percentage for finished cattle is commonly about 50 to 58 percent, depending on condition, breed, sex, gut fill and whether the carcass is weighed hot or cold. Hot carcass weight is higher than cold; carcasses lose weight as they chill. Always ask which one the price refers to.

Same animal, same offers, and the winner flips entirely on four percentage points of dressing that you cannot know in advance. That uncertainty is the real content of the decision.

Which basis suits you

  • Choose live weight if your animals are moderately finished, if you are not confident about dressing percentage, if you cannot verify the abattoir's weighing and grading, or if you need certain cash on a certain day.
  • Choose carcass weight if your animals are genuinely well finished, if you have records showing what your cattle dress out at, and if you trust the plant's scales and grading and can get a written kill sheet.

Build your own dressing record

Every time you sell on a carcass basis, write down the final live weight before transport, the carcass weight recorded, and the calculated dressing percentage. After three or four batches you will have your own figure for your own cattle on your own ration. That figure is worth real money, because it turns the comparison above from a guess into a calculation.

Ask before you agree

Whatever the basis, get answers in advance: hot or cold weight, what shrink or stand is applied before weighing, what levies and commissions are deducted, who pays transport, when payment is made, and what happens if a carcass is condemned. Systems and rules vary by country and by plant, so confirm locally every time.

Lesson 9.315 min

Grading and What Buyers Actually Pay For

In this lesson
  • Understand the common building blocks of carcass classification
  • Find out how your own country's scheme works
  • Feed and select cattle to hit the grade that pays

Grading is how a buyer converts a carcass into a price. If you do not know how your local scheme works, you are effectively selling blind, and you will keep producing animals that miss the target by accident.

Schemes differ by country

Carcass classification schemes in Zimbabwe, Botswana, Kenya, South Africa and elsewhere are not the same. They use different class names, different codes, and different cut off points. Nothing in this lesson is a substitute for the actual scheme used by the plant that will kill your animals. Get the official classification chart from the abattoir or the national meat authority, in writing, and keep a copy.

What almost every scheme is measuring

The class names differ but the underlying factors are similar across most systems:

  • Age or maturity. Usually judged from dentition, meaning the number of permanent incisor teeth erupted, or from bone and cartilage characteristics. Younger animals generally grade better and attract a premium in most schemes, because the meat is more tender.
  • Fatness. Measured as fat cover on the carcass, scored in bands from very lean to excessively fat. Both extremes are usually discounted. There is a middle band that pays best.
  • Conformation or muscling. The shape and thickness of the muscle, particularly over the hindquarter and loin. Better muscled carcasses yield more saleable meat per kilogram.
  • Damage and defects. Bruising, abscesses at injection sites, brand damage on the hide, condemned organs or parts. These cost you directly.
  • Sex class. Many schemes separate bulls, steers, heifers and cows, and pay differently.

The three things you control

  1. Age at slaughter. This is largely decided at purchase. Buying younger store animals, where the price and availability allow, puts you into better age classes. Check dentition when you buy and record it. If you buy an animal that already has several permanent incisors, no amount of feeding will move it into a young class.
  2. Fat cover. This is decided by how long and how well you feed. Handling the animals as taught in Lesson 1 is how you land in the middle fat band rather than under or over it.
  3. Damage. This is decided by handling, facilities and injection technique. Bruises from sticks, sharp fittings, crowded transport and rough loading turn into trimmed and discarded meat. Give all injections in the neck where the scheme or your vet advises, never in the hindquarter, because damage in a valuable cut costs far more.

Find the price grid

Ask your abattoir for its price grid: the actual prices paid for each class in the last few weeks. Many plants will share recent averages even if they will not commit to future prices. With the grid in hand you can work out what one grade step is worth in money. Then compare that against the feed cost of the extra days it would take to move up a fat class. Sometimes it pays. Sometimes it clearly does not. Now you can tell which.

Ask for the kill sheet

After every carcass sale, ask for the kill sheet or grading slip showing carcass weight, grade or class, and any deductions or condemnations. Keep them all. Over a few batches they teach you exactly what your cattle and your ration produce, which is information no course and no neighbour can give you.

Ask when things go wrong

If a carcass grades far worse than you expected, ask why, politely and specifically. Was it fat cover, muscling, age, bruising or a condemnation. A concrete answer is a management instruction for the next batch.

Lesson 9.414 min

Finding Buyers and Comparing Offers Properly

In this lesson
  • Map every realistic buyer channel available to you
  • Convert all offers to one comparable basis
  • Build price knowledge before you need to sell

Fatteners usually accept the first offer they get, from a buyer who came to them. That is not marketing. Marketing is having three real numbers in front of you before you commit anything.

The channels

  • Local butchers. Often the highest price per animal for well finished cattle, because they cut out the middle steps. Usually small volumes, sometimes slow payment, sometimes cash on collection. Verify that they can actually pay for what they order.
  • Abattoirs and processors. Volume, formal weighing and grading, kill sheets, and usually payment terms of days rather than cash on the spot. Their price grid rewards genuinely good carcasses.
  • Live auctions and sale pens. Transparent price discovery on the day, but you accept whatever the ring gives you, plus commission and transport. Useful as a price signal even if you do not sell there.
  • Speculators and traders. Convenient, come to your gate, take everything. That convenience is paid for out of your price.
  • Direct to consumer, institutions and events. Schools, mines, lodges, funerals, weddings, festival buying. Best prices are often here, but it is work and it requires that you meet slaughter and food safety rules.

Which channels are legal and practical for you depends on movement permits, slaughter regulations and licensing, which differ by country and sometimes by district. Confirm the rules with your local veterinary or livestock office before planning a channel, not after.

Build price knowledge before you need it

The worst time to learn about prices is the week you must sell. Every month, whether or not you have animals ready, phone two buyers and note the price and basis in your ledger. After six months you have your own price series and you can see the seasonal pattern with your own eyes rather than relying on what people say at the dip tank.

Never sell under pressure you created

Most bad prices are accepted by people who need money this week: a school fee, a debt, feed running out. The buyer can smell it. Marketing power comes from having a small cash buffer and a feed stock that lets you say no for a fortnight. Build that buffer before you need it, because it is worth more than any negotiating trick.

Verify the buyer

Before releasing animals, confirm who you are dealing with, get contact details and a physical address, agree terms in writing even if only by message, and be cautious of first time buyers wanting animals released before payment clears.

Lesson 9.514 min

Negotiating With Butchers and Abattoirs

In this lesson
  • Prepare the three numbers you must know before negotiating
  • Negotiate on terms as well as price
  • Set and hold a walk away figure

Negotiation is not talking. It is preparation. The person who has calculated more knows more, and knowing more is the whole advantage.

The three numbers you must have

  1. Your break-even price. From Module 8. The price at which you make nothing.
  2. Your target price. Break-even plus the margin the batch was planned to earn.
  3. Your walk away price. The number below which you will not sell to this buyer this week. Write it down before the conversation, because you will not think clearly during it.

Also know your animals: number, estimated live weights, age class from dentition, and your honest assessment of finish. A buyer will test whether you know your own cattle within the first two minutes.

Negotiate the whole deal, not just the price per kilogram

Price is one of at least seven terms, and the others are often easier to move:

  • Weighing. Where, when, on whose scale, after how long a stand, and with what shrink allowance.
  • Basis. Live or carcass, hot or cold.
  • Deductions. Levies, commission, condemnation rules, bank charges.
  • Transport. Who pays, who loads, who carries the risk of an injury or death in transit.
  • Payment. How much, when, by what method. Faster payment has real value to you; say so and trade for it.
  • Volume and repeat business. A commitment to supply regularly is worth something to a buyer who needs steady throughput. Offer it explicitly.
  • Sorting. Whether the buyer takes the whole pen or picks the best. Selling only your best animals leaves you with a pen of leftovers that get progressively harder to move.

How to open

Let the buyer name a number first where you can. If asked to open, quote based on your target price and the evidence for it: age class, finish, weights, and what a comparable buyer quoted this week. Facts are the only pressure that works.

Then be quiet. Silence after an offer does more work than argument.

Useful phrases that are honest

  • What is your current grid for this class, and what did similar animals make last week
  • That is on a carcass basis. On live weight, at 53 percent, that is X. Buyer B is at Y live. Can you improve
  • I can supply four animals a month for the next year. What does that get me
  • I can accept that price with payment on collection, or your price plus 2 with payment in 14 days. Which do you prefer

Things that lose you money

  • Selling because you need cash this week and the buyer knows it
  • Agreeing a price before agreeing weighing conditions and deductions
  • Accepting a verbal deal with no message or note confirming terms
  • Loading animals before you have a firm price and firm terms
  • Letting a buyer sort out only your best two and leave the rest
  • Being unable to say no because you have nothing to feed them next week

Hold the walk away

If the best offer is below your walk away price, and your cost of gain is still below the value of a kilogram, feeding on for two more weeks is a legitimate answer. If cost of gain has risen above the value of a kilogram, then feeding on is not a bluff you can afford, and you should take the best available deal and record why. Either way, decide from your numbers, not from the pressure in the room.

After the sale

Record the agreed terms, actual weights, actual deductions and the date paid. Compare what was promised against what arrived. A buyer whose deductions consistently exceed what was discussed has told you something important about next time.

Do this today: Write your break-even, target and walk away prices for your current pen on a card and keep it in your pocket for the next buyer conversation.
Lesson 9.615 min

Transport, Shrink and Timing the Sale

In this lesson
  • Estimate and limit transport shrink
  • Reduce bruising and injury losses on the way to market
  • Plan sale dates around predictable demand peaks

You can lose several percent of a finished animal's value between the pen gate and the scale, without anything visibly going wrong. Shrink and bruising are quiet losses, and both are largely under your control.

What shrink is

Shrink is live weight lost between farm and weighing. It has two parts. The first is gut and bladder fill, lost quickly and regained quickly with feed and water. The second is tissue shrink, actual body water lost from the animal, which takes days of good feeding to recover and which does affect carcass weight.

Transport shrink commonly runs in the region of about 2 to 6 percent of live weight, with short local hauls at the low end and long journeys with holding and delays at the high end. Curved, dusty roads, heat, mixing strange animals, rough loading, feed and water withdrawal, and long waits at the plant all push it up.

Worked example, using CU as a placeholder unit. A 420 kg steer sold at 44 CU per kg live.

  • At 2 percent shrink: weighs 411.6 kg, gross 18,110 CU
  • At 6 percent shrink: weighs 394.8 kg, gross 17,371 CU
  • Difference: about 739 CU on one animal, from transport handling alone

Reducing shrink

  • Travel in the cool of the day. Heat is the biggest single multiplier.
  • Do not deprive animals of water for long before loading; agree in advance how long any stand before weighing will be.
  • Load calmly. No sticks, no shouting, no dogs. Excited cattle shrink harder.
  • Load at the right density: tight enough that animals cannot fall over and get trampled, loose enough that they are not crushed or overheating.
  • Do not mix unfamiliar groups on the truck if you can avoid it. Fighting causes both shrink and bruising.
  • Plan the journey so animals are not held for hours at the plant before weighing.

Bruising

Bruised meat is trimmed and thrown away, and you pay for it in the grade or in a deduction. Almost all bruising comes from the last day of the animal's life: sharp edges and broken boards in the race and crate, gaps animals can jam a leg into, slippery floors, sticks, horns in a crowded truck, and hurried loading. Walk your own loading race and crate looking for anything a hand could catch on. Fix it before the truck arrives, not after.

Documents and legality

Movement permits, stock removal or clearance certificates, brand or tag requirements, disease control zone restrictions and slaughter regulations differ by country and often by district, and they change. Confirm exactly what documents you need with your local veterinary or livestock office before you plan the trip. Animals turned back at a checkpoint or refused at a plant cost you a day of shrink and sometimes the sale.

Timing the sale

Demand for beef is not flat through the year. Predictable patterns in many East and Southern African markets include:

  • Festive and holiday periods, when household and institutional buying rises
  • Major religious and cultural festivals and ceremonial seasons
  • Month end and salary weeks in urban markets
  • The end of the dry season, when many farmers are forced to sell condition losing animals and supply rises, pushing prices down
  • School fee periods, when distress selling rises locally

Do not take these as facts for your area. Prove them. Record the price and basis you are quoted every month for a year, and your local seasonal shape will appear in your own numbers. Then plan purchase dates backwards from the sale window: if you want to sell into a peak, and finishing takes about 90 to 120 days, you must buy roughly three to four months ahead of it.

The limit of timing

Never hold a finished animal waiting for a better price. Past finish, gain slows, feed cost of gain rises steeply, and fat penalties become possible. The gain you are waiting for has to beat all of that, and usually it does not.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. You handle a steer and find sharp, easily counted last ribs and hard, hollow areas either side of the tail head. What does this tell you?

Subcutaneous fat is laid down late, so sharp ribs and hard tail head hollows mean fat cover has not yet developed. Weight alone does not indicate finish.

2. Under carcass weight selling, who carries the risk that the animal dresses out poorly?

On a carcass basis the seller is paid on the actual dressed weight and grade, so poor dressing and poor grading both come out of the seller's money.

3. Which of these is normally NOT one of the factors a carcass classification scheme measures?

Schemes classify what is visible on the carcass: maturity, fatness, muscling, sex class and damage. A declared breed name is not itself a grading factor, and schemes differ by country in any case.

4. A 400 kg steer suffers 5 percent transport shrink and is sold at 45 CU per kg live. How much value was lost to shrink?

5 percent of 400 kg is 20 kg. At 45 CU per kg that is 900 CU lost on one animal. Part of shrink is gut fill and part is real tissue loss, but the scale does not distinguish.

5. You want to sell into a festive season demand peak. When should you buy your store cattle?

Finishing typically takes about 90 to 120 days, so you count backwards roughly three to four months from the intended sale window.
