Lessons

1. Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Starter (0-3 weeks)

Broiler starter feed is critical for the first three weeks of a chick's life. During this period, chicks grow very fast and their digestive system is still developing. A good starter feed must be high in protein and energy, and easy to digest. In Africa, common ingredients include maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, and sunflower cake. The typical nutrient specifications for broiler starter are: crude protein 22-24%, metabolizable energy 3000-3100 kcal/kg, crude fiber 3-5%, calcium 0.9-1.0%, available phosphorus 0.45-0.50%, and lysine 1.2-1.3%, methionine 0.5-0.55%. These numbers ensure proper bone development, muscle growth, and overall health. For example, a 100 kg batch of starter feed might include 55 kg maize, 30 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 5 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, and 0.5 kg premix. Always use a feed formulation software like Pearson's Square or a simple Excel sheet to balance nutrients. In Africa, where ingredient quality varies, test your ingredients for protein and moisture before mixing. Also, consider adding a coccidiostat to prevent coccidiosis, a common disease in brooding chicks. Remember, starter feed should be in crumble form to encourage eating. Feed and water should be available at all times. Clean feeders and drinkers daily. Monitor chick weight weekly; by 3 weeks, broilers should weigh around 800-900 g. If they are lighter, adjust the feed. Proper starter nutrition sets the stage for a profitable broiler cycle.

2. Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Grower (3-6 weeks)

From 3 to 6 weeks, broilers enter the grower phase. Growth is still rapid but the bird's digestive system is more developed, so you can use slightly less expensive ingredients. The goal is to maintain steady growth and prepare the bird for the finisher phase. Nutrient specifications for broiler grower are: crude protein 20-22%, metabolizable energy 3100-3200 kcal/kg, crude fiber 4-6%, calcium 0.8-0.9%, available phosphorus 0.40-0.45%, lysine 1.0-1.1%, and methionine 0.45-0.50%. In Africa, you can replace some fishmeal with cheaper protein sources like groundnut cake or cottonseed cake, but be careful with cottonseed because of gossypol toxicity. A typical 100 kg grower mix might be: 60 kg maize, 25 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 5 kg wheat bran, 2 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg premix, and 0.5 kg lysine. Use a hammer mill to grind ingredients to a medium texture. Mix thoroughly for at least 10 minutes. Feed grower as mash or pellets. Pelleting improves feed conversion but requires a pellet machine. If you don't have one, mash is fine. Keep feed troughs clean and provide fresh water. At 6 weeks, broilers should weigh about 2.0-2.2 kg. If they are underweight, check for diseases or poor feed quality. In hot African climates, add vitamin C to the water to reduce heat stress. Also, ensure proper ventilation in the poultry house. Grower feed is a bridge between starter and finisher, so balance cost and nutrition carefully.

3. Nutrient Specifications for Broiler Finisher (6+ weeks)

The finisher phase starts from 6 weeks until market weight, usually 7-8 weeks. At this stage, the bird's growth slows down, and feed conversion ratio worsens. The aim is to maximize weight gain while minimizing feed cost. Finisher feed is lower in protein but higher in energy to promote fattening. Nutrient specifications: crude protein 18-20%, metabolizable energy 3200-3300 kcal/kg, crude fiber 4-6%, calcium 0.7-0.8%, available phosphorus 0.35-0.40%, lysine 0.85-0.95%, and methionine 0.40-0.45%. In Africa, maize is the main energy source, but you can use cassava chips or sorghum if maize is expensive. However, cassava must be dried properly to avoid cyanide poisoning. A sample 100 kg finisher mix: 65 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 3 kg wheat bran, 2 kg limestone, 1.5 kg dicalcium phosphate, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg premix, and 0.5 kg methionine. Some farmers add vegetable oil (1-2%) to increase energy density. Mix well and feed as pellets if possible. Pelleting reduces waste and improves intake. Feed finisher ad libitum. By 7 weeks, broilers should weigh 2.5-3.0 kg. Withdraw feed 8-12 hours before slaughter to reduce gut content, but provide water. In Africa, many markets prefer live birds, so weigh before selling. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain to calculate feed conversion ratio (FCR). A good FCR is 1.6-1.8. If FCR is above 2.0, review your feed formulation and management. Finisher feed is the last chance to maximize profit, so use high-quality ingredients and ensure proper mixing.

4. Formulating Broiler Diets for Optimal Feed Conversion Ratio

Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) is the amount of feed required to produce 1 kg of body weight. A low FCR means better efficiency and profit. To achieve optimal FCR in broilers, you must formulate diets that match the bird's nutrient needs at each stage. Start with accurate nutrient specifications for starter, grower, and finisher. Use a feed formulation software like Feedsoft, Brill, or a simple Excel spreadsheet. In Africa, many farmers use Pearson's Square for two-ingredient mixes, but for complete diets, software is better. First, list available ingredients and their nutrient composition. For example, maize has about 9% protein, soybean meal 44%, fishmeal 60%. Use linear programming to find the cheapest mix that meets all nutrient requirements. Consider constraints like maximum inclusion levels: fishmeal up to 10%, soybean meal up to 30%, limestone up to 2%. Also, account for anti-nutritional factors in ingredients like cottonseed (gossypol) and cassava (cyanide). To improve FCR, add enzymes like phytase (500 FTU/kg) to release phosphorus from plant sources, and xylanase to break down fiber. Probiotics and organic acids improve gut health, reducing mortality and improving absorption. Always mix feed thoroughly; poor mixing leads to nutrient segregation and poor FCR. Use a horizontal mixer or mix on a clean concrete floor with shovels for at least 15 minutes. Store feed in dry, cool place to prevent mold. Moldy feed reduces intake and causes disease. Monitor FCR by recording feed consumed and weekly weights. If FCR is poor, check for feed wastage, disease, or incorrect formulation. In Africa, where feed costs are high, improving FCR by 0.1 can save significant money. For example, if a bird eats 4 kg feed to reach 2.5 kg, FCR is 1.6. If FCR becomes 1.5, you save 0.25 kg feed per bird. With 1000 birds, that's 250 kg feed saved. So, invest time in proper formulation.

5. Use of Feed Additives in Broiler Diets: Enzymes, Probiotics, Organic Acids

Feed additives are non-nutritive substances added to broiler diets to improve health, digestion, and performance. In Africa, where disease pressure is high and feed quality varies, additives can make a big difference. The three main types are enzymes, probiotics, and organic acids. Enzymes are proteins that break down anti-nutritional factors and improve nutrient availability. Common enzymes include phytase, which releases phosphorus from phytate in maize and soybean meal. Adding 500 FTU of phytase per kg of feed can reduce the need for dicalcium phosphate by 1-1.5 kg per 100 kg feed, saving money. Xylanase and glucanase break down fiber in wheat and barley, improving energy use. Proteases break down proteins. Probiotics are live beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bacillus subtilis. They colonize the gut, preventing harmful bacteria like Salmonella and E. coli. Add 0.5-1 kg of probiotic per ton of feed. Probiotics improve feed conversion and reduce mortality. Organic acids like citric acid, formic acid, and lactic acid lower gut pH, inhibiting pathogens. They also improve nutrient absorption. Add 2-3 kg per ton of feed. In Africa, many commercial premixes already contain these additives, but you can buy them separately. Always follow manufacturer's instructions for dosage. Overuse can be harmful and costly. Store additives in a cool, dry place. When mixing, add small amounts to a premix first, then blend with the main feed. For example, mix 1 kg probiotic with 10 kg maize, then add to 990 kg feed. This ensures even distribution. Use additives especially during stress periods like vaccination, transport, or heat waves. They are not a substitute for good management, but they boost performance. In a study in Nigeria, broilers fed probiotics had 5% better FCR than controls. So, consider adding them to your formulation.

6. Case Study: Formulating a Three-Phase Broiler Feeding Program

Let's walk through a case study of a small-scale broiler farm in Kenya with 500 birds. The farmer wants to formulate starter, grower, and finisher feeds using locally available ingredients: maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, wheat bran, limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, premix, lysine, and methionine. The target weights are: 0.9 kg at 3 weeks, 2.2 kg at 6 weeks, and 3.0 kg at 8 weeks. The farmer uses a simple Excel spreadsheet to formulate least-cost diets. For starter (0-3 weeks), the formulation is: maize 55%, soybean meal 30%, fishmeal 5%, wheat bran 5%, limestone 1%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, salt 0.5%, premix 0.5%, lysine 0.3%, methionine 0.2%. This provides 23% protein and 3050 kcal/kg energy. For grower (3-6 weeks): maize 60%, soybean meal 25%, fishmeal 5%, wheat bran 5%, limestone 1%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, salt 0.5%, premix 0.5%, lysine 0.3%, methionine 0.2%. This gives 21% protein and 3150 kcal/kg. For finisher (6-8 weeks): maize 65%, soybean meal 20%, fishmeal 5%, wheat bran 3%, limestone 1%, dicalcium phosphate 1.5%, salt 0.5%, premix 0.5%, lysine 0.3%, methionine 0.2%. This yields 19% protein and 3250 kcal/kg. The farmer mixes each batch in a 500 kg horizontal mixer for 15 minutes. Feed is provided ad libitum. The farmer records feed intake: starter 1.2 kg/bird, grower 2.5 kg/bird, finisher 2.0 kg/bird. Total feed per bird = 5.7 kg. Average final weight = 3.0 kg. FCR = 5.7/3.0 = 1.9. The farmer sells birds at $3.5 each. Feed cost per bird: starter $0.60, grower $1.00, finisher $0.70, total $2.30. Profit per bird = $1.20. The farmer also adds a probiotic to the finisher feed, improving FCR to 1.85. This case shows that with careful formulation and record-keeping, broiler production can be profitable in Africa. Key lessons: use local ingredients, balance nutrients, mix well, and monitor performance.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Broiler Feed Formulation for Starter, Grower and Finisher Phases

For African broiler producers, feed represents 6575% of total production cost. Getting the three-phase feeding programme right starter, grower and finisher is therefore the single most powerful lever on farm profitability. This practical reference distils the nutrient specifications, formulation logic, additive choices and business considerations that determine whether a batch of broilers returns a margin or a loss.

The broiler's nutrient requirements change rapidly. A day-old chick needs roughly 2223% crude protein and 3,000 kcal/kg metabolisable energy to build skeletal and immune foundations. By week six, the same bird needs only 1819% protein but a higher energy-to-protein ratio to deposit breast muscle efficiently. Feeding one diet throughout wastes money overfeeding protein early or underfeeding energy late both destroy margin.

Formulating for Optimal Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR)

FCR kilograms of feed per kilogram of live weight gain is the key economic metric. A broiler flock achieving FCR 1.60 is significantly more profitable than one at 1.80, even if both reach the same final weight. In African markets where feed costs are high and selling prices volatile, every 0.05 improvement in FCR can swing a batch from loss to profit.

Four formulation levers drive FCR:

1. Energy density. Higher metabolisable energy (ME) improves FCR because birds eat less feed per unit of gain. However, energy is the most expensive nutrient. The formulator must balance the cost of added oil or maize against the FCR benefit. In practice, starter diets at 3,000 kcal/kg and finisher diets at 3,1503,250 kcal/kg give the best economic return in most African markets.

2. Amino acid balance. Crude protein percentage is a crude metric. What matters is the balance of essential amino acids particularly lysine, methionine, threonine and valine. Formulating to digestible amino acid specifications rather than crude protein allows the use of lower-protein diets supplemented with synthetic amino acids, reducing soybean meal inclusion and feed cost. This is especially important in Africa where soybean meal is often imported or expensive.

3. Ingredient quality and variability. African feed producers frequently deal with variable maize quality moisture content, mycotoxin contamination, and variable energy content. Regular near-infrared (NIR) analysis or at minimum proximate analysis of incoming maize is essential. A 2% variation in maize energy content can shift FCR by 0.050.10.

4. Feed form. Pelleting improves FCR by 38% compared to mash, primarily by reducing feed wastage and improving nutrient availability. Starter diets are best fed as crumbs or mini-pellets; grower and finisher as 35 mm pellets. In markets where pelleting is unavailable, adding 12% molasses or vegetable oil to reduce dust and improve palatability is a practical alternative.

Feed Additives: Enzymes, Probiotics and Organic Acids

Additives are not optional extras they are risk-management and performance tools. In African conditions where ingredient quality varies and disease pressure is high, the right additive package can be the difference between profit and loss.

Enzymes. Phytase is the single most valuable enzyme in broiler diets. It releases phosphorus bound in plant ingredients, reducing the need for expensive dicalcium phosphate by 3050% and cutting phosphorus excretion. In Africa, where phosphate imports are costly and environmental regulations are tightening, phytase delivers both economic and environmental returns. Xylanase and glucanase improve energy availability from wheat and barley by 35%.

Probiotics. Live microbial feed supplements (Bacillus subtilis, Lactobacillus spp.) support gut health, reduce Salmonella and E. coli colonisation, and improve FCR by 24%. They are particularly valuable in African production systems where antibiotic growth promoters are increasingly restricted or banned. Probiotics also reduce mortality, which directly improves margin.

Organic acids. Formic, propionic and lactic acid blends lower gut pH, inhibit pathogenic bacteria, and improve nutrient digestibility. They are especially useful in the starter phase when chicks are most vulnerable. In hot, humid African climates where feed spoilage and mould growth are constant threats, organic acids also act as preservatives, extending feed shelf life.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Mycotoxin Risk

Maize and groundnut meal in African markets frequently contain aflatoxins above safe limits (20 ppb total aflatoxin for broiler feed). Aflatoxin B1 is a potent liver toxin and carcinogen that causes reduced growth, immunosuppression, increased mortality and liver damage. Never use mouldy, discoloured or musty-smelling maize or groundnut cake in broiler feed. Always include a proven toxin binder (hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicate or yeast cell wall product) at 12 kg per tonne. Test incoming maize with a rapid aflatoxin test kit before acceptance. Store feed in dry, ventilated conditions and use within 46 weeks. Contaminated feed can cause residues in meat and eggs, creating food safety and export market risks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Analyse your maize. Test moisture (max 13%), crude protein and aflatoxin before formulating. Never assume book values.
  • Formulate to digestible amino acids, not crude protein. This reduces soybean meal cost and improves FCR.
  • Match feed form to age. Crumbs for starter, 34 mm pellets for grower, 45 mm for finisher.
  • Include phytase at 5001,000 FTU/kg. It saves 3050% of inorganic phosphate cost.
  • Use a toxin binder in every diet. Aflatoxin risk in African maize is real and constant.
  • Transition phases by body weight, not calendar. Move to grower when birds reach 700800 g; to finisher at 1,8002,000 g.
  • Monitor FCR weekly. If FCR exceeds 1.75, review energy density, feed form and bird health immediately.
  • Keep feed fresh. Do not store finished feed longer than 46 weeks, especially in humid conditions.
