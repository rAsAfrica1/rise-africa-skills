1. Lesson 1: Sizing Roof Catchment for Your Tank - The Business Case

Before you pour a single bag of cement or buy one length of gutter, you must answer one question: how much rain can this roof actually deliver to my tank? Get this wrong and you either build a tank that overflows every March or one that sits half empty through the dry season. In this lesson we size the catchment properly, using real African rainfall data and real money. The core formula is simple: Harvestable Volume in litres equals Roof Footprint Area in square metres times Rainfall in millimetres times Runoff Coefficient times 0.9 for system losses. A 100 square metre roof in Nairobi receiving 900mm annual rain gives 100 x 900 x 0.9 x 0.9, which is 72,900 litres per year. That is the raw potential. Now, a 10,000 litre plastic tank costs roughly KES 85,000 to 120,000 in Nairobi depending on brand and wall thickness. A 10,000 litre ferrocement tank built on site costs KES 55,000 to 75,000 in materials if you do the labour yourself with two helpers. A 10,000 litre concrete tank cast in situ runs KES 90,000 to 130,000. A brick tank with cement plaster and waterproof render lands between KES 65,000 and 95,000. So the material choice is partly about budget and partly about lifespan. Plastic lasts 10 to 15 years in strong UV, ferrocement 20 to 30 years with maintenance, concrete 30 to 50 years, brick 25 to 40 years. In Lagos, a 1,000 square foot roof is about 93 square metres. With 1,500mm annual rainfall, that is 93 x 1500 x 0.9 x 0.9, which is 113,000 litres per year. A 5,000 litre plastic tank at NGN 350,000 to 480,000 captures only 4.4 percent of that potential, so you will need multiple tanks or a larger ferrocement tank. In Accra with 800mm rainfall on a 120 square metre roof, you get 120 x 800 x 0.9 x 0.9, which is 77,760 litres. In Johannesburg with 700mm on a 150 square metre roof, you get 150 x 700 x 0.9 x 0.9, which is 85,050 litres. The runoff coefficient matters. A corrugated iron roof loses about 10 percent to evaporation, splashing, and first-flush. A tile roof loses 15 to 20 percent. A thatched roof loses 40 percent and is not recommended for drinking water. Always use 0.9 for metal and 0.8 for tiles. The first-flush diverter must be sized to discard the first 1 to 2mm of rain, which on a 100 square metre roof is 100 to 200 litres. That is a 200 litre drum or a commercial first-flush unit costing KES 8,000 to 15,000. Business angle: if you are selling tank installation, you must present the client with a simple spreadsheet showing annual harvest versus tank size versus dry season demand. A family of six in Kenya uses about 100 litres per day for drinking, cooking, and basic hygiene, which is 36,500 litres per year. If they have a 10,000 litre tank and 900mm rain, they can meet about 27 percent of annual demand from a 100 square metre roof, but the tank will fill and overflow during the long rains. The smart business move is to install two 10,000 litre tanks in series, or one 20,000 litre ferrocement tank, and add a simple sand filter for potable use. Cost per litre of storage: plastic is KES 8.5 to 12 per litre, ferrocement is KES 5.5 to 7.5 per litre, concrete is KES 9 to 13 per litre, brick is KES 6.5 to 9.5 per litre. Ferrocement wins on cost per litre and durability, but it requires skilled labour and 28 days of curing. Plastic wins on speed and portability. Concrete wins on prestige and resale value. Brick wins where local burnt bricks are cheap, such as in parts of Nigeria and Ghana. Safety note: never size a tank larger than the roof can fill in a normal year, because stagnant water breeds mosquitoes and bacteria. Always include a mosquito screen on the inlet and a tight lid. Also, check roof structural capacity. A 10,000 litre tank weighs 10 tonnes when full. That is 10,000kg spread over maybe 4 square metres of ground, which is 2,500kg per square metre. Most residential slabs are designed for 200 to 300kg per square metre live load, so the tank must sit on a reinforced concrete pad, not on the roof. If you must put a tank on a roof, consult a structural engineer and expect to pay KES 50,000 to 150,000 for reinforcement. Finally, document everything for the client: roof area, rainfall data from the nearest meteorological station, tank size, cost, and expected annual harvest. This builds trust and justifies your price. A typical installation business in Kenya charges KES 15,000 to 30,000 labour for a 10,000 litre tank setup, plus materials. In Nigeria, labour is NGN 80,000 to 150,000. In South Africa, ZAR 4,000 to 8,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,500 to 3,000. Your margin comes from doing the sizing correctly, sourcing materials in bulk, and finishing the job in two days instead of five.

2. Lesson 2: Gutter Selection, Slope, and Installation for Maximum Yield

Gutters are the veins of your roof catchment system. If they are undersized, badly sloped, or poorly joined, you lose 20 to 40 percent of your harvestable water and you create a maintenance nightmare. This lesson covers gutter types, sizing, slope, hangers, and the business of selling and installing gutters in African markets. First, gutter types. PVC gutters are the most common in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa. A 4-inch PVC gutter costs KES 350 to 550 per metre in Nairobi, NGN 2,500 to 4,000 per metre in Lagos, GHS 45 to 70 per metre in Accra, and ZAR 90 to 140 per metre in Johannesburg. They are light, easy to cut with a hacksaw, and last 10 to 15 years in UV-stabilised form. Non-UV PVC becomes brittle in 3 to 5 years, so always ask for UV-stabilised. Aluminium gutters cost KES 800 to 1,200 per metre, NGN 6,000 to 9,000 per metre, GHS 100 to 150 per metre, and ZAR 180 to 260 per metre. They last 20 to 30 years and are good for coastal areas because they do not rust, but they dent easily. Galvanised steel gutters cost KES 600 to 900 per metre, NGN 4,500 to 7,000 per metre, GHS 70 to 110 per metre, and ZAR 130 to 200 per metre. They last 8 to 15 years in inland areas but only 3 to 5 years near the coast unless powder-coated. Zinc gutters are common in South Africa and cost ZAR 150 to 220 per metre, lasting 15 to 25 years. Concrete or ferrocement gutters are cast in place and cost KES 1,200 to 2,000 per metre, but they last 30 to 50 years and are excellent for large institutional buildings. Brick gutters are rare but possible in Ghana and Nigeria, using burnt bricks laid on edge and plastered with waterproof mortar. They cost GHS 80 to 120 per metre and NGN 3,500 to 5,500 per metre, lasting 20 to 30 years if well maintained. Sizing: the rule of thumb is 1 square centimetre of gutter cross-section for every 1 square metre of roof area, but that is for moderate rainfall. In tropical downpours, you need 1.5 to 2 square centimetres per square metre. A 100 square metre roof needs a gutter with 150 to 200 square centimetres of cross-section. A 4-inch half-round PVC gutter has about 80 square centimetres, so you need two runs or a 6-inch gutter. A 6-inch PVC gutter has about 180 square centimetres and costs KES 550 to 800 per metre. Slope: gutters must slope toward the downpipe at 1mm per metre minimum, ideally 2 to 3mm per metre. That means a 10 metre run drops 20 to 30mm. Use a string line and a spirit level. If the gutter is level, water pools and breeds mosquitoes. If the slope is too steep, water shoots past the downpipe outlet. Hangers: space them every 600mm for PVC and every 900mm for metal. In heavy rain areas, use 500mm spacing. Hangers cost KES 50 to 120 each, NGN 400 to 800 each, GHS 8 to 15 each, ZAR 15 to 30 each. A 20 metre gutter run needs 34 hangers at 600mm spacing, costing KES 1,700 to 4,080. Installation sequence: first, mark the high point and low point on the fascia board. Second, install the highest hanger and the lowest hanger, then run a string line between them. Third, install intermediate hangers every 600mm. Fourth, cut the gutter to length with a hacksaw or tin snips, leaving 5mm expansion gap at joints. Fifth, apply PVC solvent cement or silicone sealant at joints and install joint connectors. Sixth, install stop ends at the high end and outlet drops at the low end. Seventh, connect the downpipe. Downpipe sizing: a 100 square metre roof needs a 75mm to 110mm downpipe. A 110mm PVC downpipe costs KES 450 to 700 per metre, NGN 3,000 to 5,000 per metre, GHS 55 to 85 per metre, ZAR 110 to 170 per metre. One downpipe can serve 80 to 100 square metres of roof. If you have a 200 square metre roof, use two downpipes. Downpipe elbows cost KES 150 to 300 each. Leaf guards: install leaf guards or gutter mesh to keep out leaves, especially in areas with trees. A 20 metre roll of gutter mesh costs KES 1,500 to 3,000, NGN 12,000 to 25,000, GHS 200 to 350, ZAR 400 to 700. Without leaf guards, you will clean gutters every month during rainy season. Business angle: gutter installation is a high-margin service. A typical 20 metre gutter job with downpipes and hangers costs the client KES 25,000 to 45,000 in materials and KES 8,000 to 15,000 in labour. Your material cost is KES 18,000 to 32,000, so your gross profit is KES 7,000 to 13,000 per job. In Nigeria, a 20 metre job bills NGN 180,000 to 320,000 with NGN 60,000 to 100,000 labour. In Ghana, GHS 2,500 to 4,500 with GHS 800 to 1,500 labour. In South Africa, ZAR 5,000 to 9,000 with ZAR 1,500 to 3,000 labour. You can install 2 to 3 gutter jobs per day with a two-person team. Safety note: always use a sturdy ladder, never lean a ladder against a gutter because it will bend or break. Use a ladder standoff or tie the ladder to the fascia. Wear gloves when cutting metal gutters because edges are sharp. If working at height above 3 metres, use a fall arrest harness. Never install gutters during a storm or when the roof is wet. Finally, test the system with a hose before you leave the site. Run water for 5 minutes and check for leaks at every joint and for proper flow to the tank. A leaky gutter system wastes water and erodes the foundation. Document the installation with photos and give the client a simple maintenance schedule: clean gutters every 3 months, check hangers every 6 months, and replace sealant every 2 years.

3. Lesson 3: First-Flush Diverters, Filters, and Downpipe Connections

The first rain after a dry spell is the dirtiest. It carries bird droppings, dust, leaves, and bacteria from the roof. If that water goes straight into your tank, you will have smelly, contaminated water that costs more to treat than to discard. A first-flush diverter discards the first 1 to 2mm of rain, which on a 100 square metre roof is 100 to 200 litres. This lesson covers first-flush diverters, filters, and downpipe connections, with real costs and practical installation steps for African conditions. First-flush diverter types: the simplest is a manual diverter, which is a T-junction with a valve. You open the valve and let the first rain run to waste, then close it. This costs KES 500 to 1,500 for the valve and fittings, but it requires someone to be home when it rains, which is unreliable. The automatic diverter is a chamber that fills with the first flush, then a float ball seals the waste outlet and water flows to the tank. A commercial automatic first-flush diverter costs KES 8,000 to 15,000 in Nairobi, NGN 60,000 to 120,000 in Lagos, GHS 700 to 1,200 in Accra, and ZAR 1,500 to 2,800 in Johannesburg. You can build a DIY automatic diverter using a 200 litre plastic drum, a float valve, and PVC fittings for KES 3,500 to 6,000. The drum sits at the base of the downpipe. The downpipe enters the top of the drum. A standpipe inside the drum rises to the level of the first flush volume. When the drum fills, water flows through the standpipe to the tank. A small drip hole at the bottom of the drum empties it slowly after the rain stops, ready for the next event. The drip hole must be sized so the drum empties in 2 to 4 hours. A 3mm hole empties 200 litres in about 3 hours. Filters: after the first-flush diverter, install a coarse filter to catch leaves and debris. A simple gutter mesh or a plastic bucket with holes works. For better quality, install a sand filter or a commercial cartridge filter. A slow sand filter for a 10,000 litre tank costs KES 15,000 to 30,000 to build, using a 200 litre drum, graded sand, gravel, and a geotextile layer. It removes 90 to 99 percent of bacteria and turbidity. A commercial 10-inch cartridge filter costs KES 8,000 to 15,000 and removes 5 to 20 microns. In Nigeria, a sand filter build costs NGN 120,000 to 250,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,200 to 2,500. In South Africa, ZAR 2,500 to 5,000. Downpipe connections: the downpipe must connect to the first-flush diverter, then to the tank inlet. Use 110mm PVC pipe for the main downpipe and 50mm or 75mm for the diverter waste line. The tank inlet should be at the top of the tank, with a mosquito screen. A typical inlet kit costs KES 2,500 to 5,000, NGN 20,000 to 40,000, GHS 250 to 450, ZAR 500 to 900. The inlet must be sealed to the tank wall with silicone or a rubber gasket. If the tank is plastic, use a bulkhead fitting. If the tank is ferrocement or concrete, cast a PVC sleeve into the wall during construction. Overflow: every tank needs an overflow pipe that discharges 2 metres away from the foundation. A 50mm or 75mm PVC overflow pipe costs KES 300 to 600 per metre. The overflow must have a mosquito screen and a flap valve to prevent rodents from entering. Business angle: first-flush and filtration is where you add value and increase your ticket size. A basic gutter and tank job might be KES 60,000. Adding a first-flush diverter, sand filter, and overflow brings it to KES 95,000 to 120,000. Your material cost for the add-ons is KES 20,000 to 35,000, so your gross profit increases by KES 15,000 to 25,000. In Nigeria, the add-ons cost NGN 150,000 to 280,000 in materials and you can bill NGN 350,000 to 500,000 for the complete system. In Ghana, GHS 1,800 to 3,200 materials, bill GHS 4,000 to 6,500. In South Africa, ZAR 3,500 to 6,500 materials, bill ZAR 8,000 to 14,000. Safety note: never drink water directly from a roof catchment without treatment. Even with a first-flush diverter and sand filter, boil water for 1 minute or use chlorine tablets (2.5mg per litre) or a UV filter. A UV filter costs KES 25,000 to 50,000 and needs electricity. Chlorine tablets cost KES 500 for 100 tablets. Test water quality every 6 months at a laboratory. A basic bacteriological test costs KES 3,000 to 6,000 in Nairobi, NGN 25,000 to 50,000 in Lagos, GHS 300 to 600 in Accra, ZAR 600 to 1,200 in Johannesburg. If the test shows E. coli, shock-chlorinate the tank with 200g of granular chlorine per 1,000 litres, let it sit for 12 hours, then flush. Finally, train the client on maintenance: clean the first-flush drum every month, replace the filter cartridge every 6 months, and check the mosquito screens every 3 months. A well-maintained system delivers safe water for decades.

4. Lesson 4: Building Ferrocement and Concrete Tanks for Roof Catchment

Ferrocement and concrete tanks are the workhorses of African water storage. They cost less per litre than plastic, last longer, and can be built in any shape or size. But they require skill, patience, and strict quality control. This lesson gives you the step-by-step for building a 10,000 litre ferrocement tank and a 10,000 litre concrete tank, with real costs in KES, NGN, GHS, and ZAR. Ferrocement tank: a 10,000 litre tank is 2.5 metres in diameter and 2.1 metres high, with a wall thickness of 25 to 40mm. Materials: 3 rolls of 1-inch chicken mesh (KES 3,500 each, total KES 10,500), 2 rolls of 1/2-inch chicken mesh (KES 2,800 each, total KES 5,600), 20kg of 1.2mm galvanised tie wire (KES 4,000), 10 bags of cement (KES 700 each, total KES 7,000), 1 cubic metre of river sand (KES 3,500), 0.5 cubic metre of ballast (KES 2,500), 10kg of waterproofing admixture (KES 3,000), 1 roll of polythene sheeting (KES 2,000), 20 lengths of 6mm steel rod for the ring beam (KES 500 each, total KES 10,000), and 1 bag of lime (KES 1,000). Total material cost in Nairobi: KES 49,100. Add labour for 2 masons and 2 helpers for 7 days: KES 35,000. Total: KES 84,100. In Lagos, the same tank costs NGN 650,000 to 850,000. In Accra, GHS 6,500 to 8,500. In Johannesburg, ZAR 12,000 to 16,000. Construction steps: first, cast a reinforced concrete base 100mm thick with a 200mm ring beam. The base must be level and cure for 3 days. Second, bend the 6mm rods into two rings, one at the top and one at the bottom, and tie them with wire. Third, wrap the chicken mesh around the rings, overlapping 100mm, and tie with wire every 100mm. Fourth, plaster the outside with a 1:3 cement-sand mix, working from bottom to top. Fifth, after 24 hours, plaster the inside with the same mix, adding waterproofing admixture. Sixth, cure the tank by filling it with water after 7 days and keeping it wet for 21 days. Seventh, install the inlet, outlet, overflow, and a tight lid. Concrete tank: a 10,000 litre tank is 2.5 metres in diameter and 2.1 metres high, with 150mm walls. Materials: 15 bags of cement (KES 10,500), 1.5 cubic metres of river sand (KES 5,250), 2 cubic metres of ballast (KES 10,000), 100kg of 12mm steel rod (KES 12,000), 20kg of binding wire (KES 4,000), 10kg of waterproofing admixture (KES 3,000), and formwork timber (KES 8,000). Total material cost in Nairobi: KES 52,750. Labour for 2 masons and 3 helpers for 10 days: KES 50,000. Total: KES 102,750. In Lagos, NGN 800,000 to 1,100,000. In Accra, GHS 8,000 to 11,000. In Johannesburg, ZAR 15,000 to 20,000. Construction steps: first, cast a 150mm reinforced base. Second, erect inner and outer formwork using 18mm plywood or steel panels. Third, place the steel reinforcement cage: 12mm vertical bars every 200mm and 8mm horizontal bars every 200mm. Fourth, pour concrete in 300mm lifts, vibrating each lift with a poker vibrator or by rodding. Fifth, remove formwork after 3 days. Sixth, plaster the inside with a 1:2 cement-sand mix with waterproofing. Seventh, cure for 28 days. Brick tank: a 10,000 litre tank is 2.5 metres in diameter and 2.1 metres high, with 230mm walls. Materials: 1,200 burnt bricks (KES 15 each, total KES 18,000), 8 bags of cement (KES 5,600), 1 cubic metre of river sand (KES 3,500), 10kg of waterproofing (KES 3,000), and 20kg of binding wire (KES 4,000). Total material cost: KES 34,100. Labour: KES 30,000. Total: KES 64,100. In Nigeria, NGN 500,000 to 700,000. In Ghana, GHS 5,000 to 7,000. In South Africa, ZAR 10,000 to 14,000. Business angle: ferrocement is the best value for money. You can build a 10,000 litre tank in 7 days with a 4-person team. Your material cost is KES 49,100, labour KES 35,000, total KES 84,100. You bill the client KES 120,000 to 150,000, giving you a gross profit of KES 36,000 to 66,000 per tank. In Nigeria, bill NGN 1,200,000 to 1,500,000, profit NGN 350,000 to 650,000. In Ghana, bill GHS 12,000 to 15,000, profit GHS 3,500 to 6,500. In South Africa, bill ZAR 22,000 to 28,000, profit ZAR 6,000 to 12,000. You can build 3 to 4 tanks per month with one team, giving you a monthly gross profit of KES 108,000 to 264,000. Safety note: cement burns. Wear gloves, goggles, and a dust mask when mixing. Never enter a tank under construction without proper ventilation and a safety harness. Concrete curing releases heat and can cause burns. Always have a first aid kit on site with burn gel and eye wash. Finally, test every tank for leaks before handing over. Fill it with water and mark the level. After 24 hours, if the level drops more than 5mm, you have a leak. Patch it with a 1:1 cement-sand mix plus waterproofing. A leaky tank is a failed job and will destroy your reputation.

5. Lesson 5: Maintaining Roof Catchment Systems and Scaling Your Business

A roof catchment system is not a one-time installation. It is a living asset that needs maintenance, and maintenance is a recurring revenue stream for your business. This lesson covers maintenance schedules, common failures, repair costs, and how to scale from a one-man operation to a registered company with a team and a waiting list of clients. Maintenance schedule: every 3 months, clean gutters and downpipes. Remove leaves, bird nests, and sediment. Check hangers and tighten loose screws. Every 6 months, inspect the first-flush diverter and clean the drum. Replace the filter cartridge or clean the sand filter by backwashing. Check the tank inlet, overflow, and mosquito screens. Every 12 months, drain and clean the tank. Remove sediment from the bottom. Scrub the walls with a soft brush and a mild chlorine solution (50ml of bleach per 10 litres of water). Rinse and refill. Inspect the tank structure for cracks, especially at the base and around fittings. Repair any cracks with waterproof mortar. Every 24 months, repaint metal gutters and downpipes if needed. Replace worn sealants. Test water quality. Common failures and repair costs: leaking gutter joints are the most common. Repair cost: KES 500 to 1,500 per joint in Nairobi, NGN 5,000 to 12,000 in Lagos, GHS 50 to 120 in Accra, ZAR 100 to 250 in Johannesburg. Sagging gutters due to loose hangers: repair cost KES 1,000 to 3,000 per 5 metres. Blocked downpipes: clear with a plumber's snake or a pressure hose. Cost KES 2,000 to 5,000. Cracked tank walls: patch with waterproof mortar. Cost KES 3,000 to 8,000 for a small crack. Failed first-flush diverter float valve: replace the valve. Cost KES 1,500 to 3,000. Contaminated water: shock-chlorinate and flush. Cost KES 2,000 to 5,000 including labour. Business model: offer an annual maintenance contract. For KES 15,000 to 25,000 per year, you visit the client 4 times, clean the system, check for leaks, and test the water. Your cost per visit is KES 1,500 to 2,500 in labour and transport, so your gross profit is KES 9,000 to 15,000 per contract per year. If you have 50 maintenance contracts, that is KES 450,000 to 750,000 per year in recurring revenue. In Nigeria, charge NGN 120,000 to 200,000 per year, profit NGN 70,000 to 130,000 per contract. In Ghana, GHS 1,500 to 2,500 per year, profit GHS 900 to 1,600. In South Africa, ZAR 3,000 to 5,000 per year, profit ZAR 1,800 to 3,200. Scaling: start as a sole operator with a motorbike and a toolkit. Your toolkit: hacksaw, tin snips, spirit level, string line, measuring tape, screwdriver set, pliers, silicone gun, PVC solvent cement, ladder, safety harness, gloves, goggles, and a first aid kit. Total cost: KES 25,000 to 40,000. After 6 months, hire one helper. After 12 months, hire a second team and buy a pickup truck (KES 500,000 to 800,000 used). Register your business with the relevant authority: in Kenya, register with the Business Registration Service (KES 1,000 for a sole proprietorship) and get a KRA PIN. In Nigeria, register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (NGN 20,000 to 50,000). In Ghana, register with the Registrar General's Department (GHS 200 to 500). In South Africa, register with CIPC (ZAR 500 to 1,500). Marketing: create a simple one-page flyer with your services, prices, and phone number. Print 1,000 copies for KES 3,000. Distribute in hardware stores, churches, mosques, and community notice boards. Offer a 10 percent discount for referrals. Join a WhatsApp group for plumbers and builders in your city. Post before-and-after photos of your installations. Partner with hardware stores: give them a 5 percent commission for every client they refer. Pricing strategy: do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, speed, and warranty. Offer a 2-year warranty on workmanship. If a leak appears within 2 years, you fix it free. This builds trust and justifies a 15 to 20 percent higher price than your competitors. Financial projections: in year 1, install 30 tanks and 20 gutter systems. Average tank profit KES 40,000, average gutter profit KES 10,000. Total gross profit: KES 1,400,000. Expenses: transport KES 200,000, tools KES 50,000, marketing KES 50,000, licences KES 20,000. Net profit: KES 1,080,000. In year 2, hire a team and double your output. Net profit: KES 2,500,000 to 3,000,000. In Nigeria, year 1 net profit: NGN 4,000,000 to 6,000,000. In Ghana, GHS 40,000 to 60,000. In South Africa, ZAR 80,000 to 120,000. Safety note: never compromise on safety. Every team member must have gloves, goggles, and a dust mask. Every vehicle must have a first aid kit and a fire extinguisher. Every site must have a safety briefing before work starts. If you see a unsafe condition, stop work and fix it. A dead worker or a serious injury will destroy your business and your reputation. Finally, give back. Train young people in your community on roof catchment and tank building. Offer a 3-day workshop for KES 5,000 per person. This creates a pipeline of skilled labour and positions you as a leader in the industry. The demand for water storage in Africa is growing every year. Climate change is making rainfall less predictable, and populations are growing. If you build a reputation for quality, honesty, and fair pricing, you will never lack work. Start today, do the first job perfectly, and let your work speak for you.
