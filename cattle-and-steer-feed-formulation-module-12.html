1. Case study: Formulating a finishing diet for steers

You are a feed formulator for a feedlot in South Africa's Free State. You have 200 steers averaging 350 kg, and you want them to gain 1.5 kg per day for 120 days. The finishing diet must be 12% crude protein (CP) and 2.8 Mcal ME/kg. Available ingredients: maize (9% CP, 3.3 Mcal ME/kg), soybean meal (44% CP, 2.8 Mcal ME/kg), wheat bran (15% CP, 2.2 Mcal ME/kg), molasses (5% CP, 2.5 Mcal ME/kg), and a mineral-vitamin premix. You need to find the cheapest mix that meets the specs.

Start with a Pearson square for protein. You want 12% CP. Mix maize (9% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP). The difference between 12 and 9 is 3 parts soybean meal; between 44 and 12 is 32 parts maize. So 32/35 = 91.4% maize, 8.6% soybean meal. That gives 12% CP. Now check energy: maize has 3.3 Mcal, soybean meal 2.8. The mix gives 3.3*0.914 + 2.8*0.086 = 3.26 Mcal, which is above 2.8, so energy is fine. But this mix is 91% maize, which can cause acidosis. You decide to include 15% wheat bran to add fibre and 5% molasses for palatability and dust control. Recalculate: 15% wheat bran (15% CP) and 5% molasses (5% CP) contribute 2.25 + 0.25 = 2.5 parts CP. The remaining 80% must provide 9.5 parts CP. Using maize and soybean meal: let x = soybean meal, 0.8 - x = maize. 0.44x + 0.09(0.8 - x) = 0.095. Solve: 0.44x + 0.072 - 0.09x = 0.095 => 0.35x = 0.023 => x = 0.0657, so 6.57% soybean meal and 73.43% maize. Final mix: 73.43% maize, 6.57% soybean meal, 15% wheat bran, 5% molasses, plus 0.5% premix. Check energy: 0.7343*3.3 + 0.0657*2.8 + 0.15*2.2 + 0.05*2.5 = 2.42 + 0.18 + 0.33 + 0.125 = 3.06 Mcal, still above 2.8. Adjust by adding more wheat bran if needed. This diet should cost about R4.50 per kg. Feed 2.5% of body weight per day, so a 350 kg steer gets 8.75 kg. Monitor intake and adjust.

2. Case study: Developing a supplement for grazing cattle

In the dry season, grazing cattle in Botswana lose weight because grass is low in protein (less than 6% CP) and energy. You need to formulate a supplement to provide 200 g of crude protein per head per day and enough energy to maintain weight. You have 100 cattle on communal grazing. Available ingredients: maize meal (9% CP, 3.3 Mcal ME/kg), cottonseed cake (36% CP, 2.5 Mcal ME/kg), urea (280% CP equivalent, but only for ruminants), molasses (5% CP, 2.5 Mcal ME/kg), and mineral premix. Urea is cheap but must be introduced gradually and not exceed 1% of total diet.

First, decide how much supplement to feed. A common practice is to feed 1 kg per head per day. To get 200 g CP from 1 kg, the supplement must be 20% CP. Using urea: urea is 280% CP, so 1 kg urea provides 2.8 kg CP equivalent. But urea is not a protein; it provides nitrogen for rumen microbes. You can use up to 30% of the CP from urea. So 200 g CP * 30% = 60 g CP from urea, which is 60/280 = 0.214 kg urea. The remaining 140 g CP must come from natural protein. Use cottonseed cake (36% CP) and maize meal (9% CP). Let x = cottonseed cake, y = maize meal, and we want x + y = 1 - 0.214 - 0.05 (molasses) - 0.01 (mineral) = 0.726 kg. Protein from cottonseed and maize: 0.36x + 0.09y = 0.140. Also x + y = 0.726. Solve: y = 0.726 - x. 0.36x + 0.09(0.726 - x) = 0.140 => 0.36x + 0.06534 - 0.09x = 0.140 => 0.27x = 0.07466 => x = 0.2765 kg cottonseed cake, y = 0.4495 kg maize meal. Add 0.05 kg molasses, 0.01 kg mineral, and 0.214 kg urea. Total = 1 kg. This supplement provides 200 g CP. Energy: 0.2765*2.5 + 0.4495*3.3 + 0.05*2.5 + 0.214*0 (urea has no energy) = 0.691 + 1.483 + 0.125 = 2.30 Mcal ME, which is good for maintenance. Mix well and feed daily. Introduce urea over 2 weeks to avoid toxicity.

3. Case study: Least-cost formulation with by-products

A feed mill in Kenya wants to formulate a growing diet for steers using locally available by-products to reduce cost. The target is 14% CP and 2.7 Mcal ME/kg. Ingredients and prices per kg: maize (9% CP, 3.3 Mcal, KES 40), maize bran (10% CP, 2.5 Mcal, KES 20), sunflower cake (28% CP, 2.2 Mcal, KES 30), molasses (5% CP, 2.5 Mcal, KES 25), limestone (0% CP, 0 Mcal, KES 10), and salt (0% CP, 0 Mcal, KES 15). You also have a mineral premix at KES 100/kg, include 0.5%. Use the least-cost method: set up equations for CP and ME, and minimize cost. Since you have more than two ingredients, use linear programming (e.g., Excel Solver). But you can approximate with a Pearson square for protein first.

Let's fix by-products at maximum inclusion: maize bran up to 30%, sunflower cake up to 20%, molasses up to 5%. These are typical limits to avoid digestive issues. So fixed: 30% maize bran, 20% sunflower cake, 5% molasses, 0.5% premix, 1% limestone, 0.5% salt. That totals 57%. The remaining 43% will be maize and maybe more sunflower cake. Calculate CP from fixed: 0.30*10 + 0.20*28 + 0.05*5 = 3 + 5.6 + 0.25 = 8.85% CP. Need 14% total, so remaining 43% must provide 5.15% CP. If we use only maize (9% CP), 0.43*9 = 3.87% CP, short by 1.28%. So we need more protein. Replace some maize with sunflower cake (28% CP). Let x = additional sunflower cake (beyond 20%), and 0.43 - x = maize. Then 0.28x + 0.09(0.43 - x) = 0.0515. Solve: 0.28x + 0.0387 - 0.09x = 0.0515 => 0.19x = 0.0128 => x = 0.0674, so 6.74% extra sunflower cake, and 36.26% maize. Total sunflower cake = 20 + 6.74 = 26.74%, which exceeds typical limit of 20%? Actually we set max 20%, so we need another protein source. Use cottonseed cake (36% CP, KES 35) instead. Let x = cottonseed cake, y = maize, x + y = 0.43. 0.36x + 0.09y = 0.0515 => 0.36x + 0.09(0.43 - x) = 0.0515 => 0.27x + 0.0387 = 0.0515 => 0.27x = 0.0128 => x = 0.0474, y = 0.3826. So 4.74% cottonseed cake, 38.26% maize. Now check energy: fixed: 0.30*2.5 + 0.20*2.2 + 0.05*2.5 = 0.75 + 0.44 + 0.125 = 1.315 Mcal. Maize: 0.3826*3.3 = 1.263, cottonseed: 0.0474*2.2 = 0.104. Total ME = 1.315 + 1.263 + 0.104 = 2.682 Mcal, close to 2.7. Add 0.5% premix (no energy). So diet meets specs. Cost: maize bran 0.30*20=6, sunflower 0.20*30=6, molasses 0.05*25=1.25, maize 0.3826*40=15.30, cottonseed 0.0474*35=1.66, limestone 0.01*10=0.10, salt 0.005*15=0.075, premix 0.005*100=0.50. Total = KES 30.89 per kg. This is cheaper than using more maize and soybean meal. Always verify with software for exact least-cost.

4. Case study: Troubleshooting a feedlot diet

A feedlot in Namibia reports that steers are off feed, with some showing bloating and diarrhea. The diet is supposed to be a finishing diet with 70% maize, 15% wheat bran, 10% soybean meal, 4% molasses, and 1% premix. The feedlot manager says the cattle are not gaining as expected and some have died. You are called to troubleshoot. First, check the mixing. If the mixer is not calibrated, ingredients may be unevenly distributed. For example, if the premix is not mixed well, some cattle get too much and others too little. Also, if maize is ground too fine, it can cause acidosis. The ideal particle size for maize in a finishing diet is 600-800 microns. If it's finer, reduce by adding whole maize or coarser grind.

Second, check the diet composition. The diet as formulated has 70% maize, which is high in starch. When cattle consume a high-starch diet, the rumen pH drops, leading to acidosis. Symptoms include off feed, diarrhea, and bloating. To fix, increase effective fibre. Replace some maize with more wheat bran or add 5% chopped hay. Also, ensure the cattle are adapted to the diet gradually over 3 weeks. If they were switched suddenly, that causes acidosis. Third, check for mycotoxins. In wet conditions, maize can have mould. Test a sample. If mycotoxins are present, use a binder like bentonite at 0.5% of the diet. Fourth, check water availability. Cattle need free access to clean water. If water is restricted, intake drops. Fifth, check the premix. If the premix is expired or not properly stored, it may lose potency. Use a fresh premix and ensure it contains ionophores like monensin, which help prevent acidosis. Monensin should be included at 25-30 g per tonne of feed. If not present, add it. Also, consider adding sodium bicarbonate at 0.5% to buffer rumen pH. After adjustments, monitor cattle daily. If bloating continues, consult a veterinarian. In many cases, simple changes like coarser grind, more fibre, and proper adaptation solve the problem.

5. Practical exercise: Using software to formulate a diet

In this exercise, you will use a free or low-cost software to formulate a diet for growing steers. We recommend 'Feedsoft' or 'Excel with Solver'. If you don't have a computer, you can use a smartphone app like 'Feed Formulation' (available on Android). The goal is to formulate a diet with 13% CP and 2.6 Mcal ME/kg for 300 kg steers gaining 1 kg/day. Available ingredients: maize (9% CP, 3.3 Mcal, $0.30/kg), soybean meal (44% CP, 2.8 Mcal, $0.50/kg), wheat bran (15% CP, 2.2 Mcal, $0.20/kg), molasses (5% CP, 2.5 Mcal, $0.25/kg), limestone ($0.10/kg), salt ($0.15/kg), and premix ($1.00/kg). Include 0.5% premix, 1% limestone, 0.5% salt. Set maximum inclusion: wheat bran 20%, molasses 5%.

Open Excel. In column A, list ingredients. In column B, put CP%. In column C, ME. In column D, cost per kg. In column E, put the percentage of each ingredient you want to solve for (start with equal amounts). In column F, calculate the contribution: for CP, =B2*E2; for ME, =C2*E2; for cost, =D2*E2. At the bottom, sum columns E, F, and G (cost). You want total CP = 13%, total ME = 2.6, and total cost minimized. Use Solver: set objective to minimize total cost (sum of cost column), by changing E2:E8 (the percentages). Add constraints: sum of E2:E8 = 1 (100%), total CP = 0.13, total ME = 0.026 (since ME is in Mcal/kg, 2.6 Mcal/kg means 2.6 per kg, but if you use per 100 kg, adjust). Actually, if you work per 100 kg, then ME per 100 kg is 260 Mcal. But easier: work with percentages as decimals. Let E2:E8 be fractions summing to 1. Then CP = sum(B2:B8 * E2:E8) = 0.13. ME = sum(C2:C8 * E2:E8) = 2.6. Also set constraints: E for wheat bran <= 0.20, molasses <= 0.05, premix = 0.005, limestone = 0.01, salt = 0.005. Run Solver. It will give you the least-cost mix. For example, it might suggest 60% maize, 10% soybean meal, 20% wheat bran, 5% molasses, 0.5% premix, 1% limestone, 0.5% salt, and adjust maize and soybean to meet CP. Check the solution: if CP is 13% and ME is 2.6, good. If not, adjust constraints. Save the file. This exercise teaches you how software can quickly find the cheapest diet. Practice with different targets.

6. Final project: Complete diet formulation and justification

For your final project, you will formulate a complete diet for a specific cattle operation. Choose one of the following scenarios: (1) A cow-calf operation in Tanzania with 50 cows and calves, where you need a supplement for dry season grazing. (2) A feedlot in Zambia finishing 100 steers from 300 kg to 450 kg. (3) A dairy farm in Uganda with 20 lactating cows. You must formulate a diet that meets the nutritional requirements, using locally available ingredients, and justify your choices. Your project should include: a list of ingredients with their nutritional composition (CP, ME, cost), the target nutrient requirements (use NRC tables or local extension guidelines), the formulation method (Pearson square, algebraic, or software), the final diet composition (percentages), and a cost analysis. Also, include a justification for each ingredient: why you chose it, any limits (e.g., urea max 1%, cottonseed max 20% due to gossypol), and how you will mix and feed it. For example, if you choose scenario 2, you might use maize, wheat bran, soybean meal, molasses, limestone, salt, and premix. Your target might be 12% CP and 2.8 Mcal ME/kg. You could use Pearson square to find the maize:soybean ratio, then add by-products. Calculate the cost per kg and per day per steer. Show your calculations step by step. Then, write a one-page justification: why this diet is nutritionally adequate, why it is cost-effective, and any potential risks (e.g., acidosis, bloat) and how you will mitigate them. Submit your project as a written report (2-3 pages) or a presentation. This project will test your ability to apply all the concepts from this course. Remember, a good formulation balances nutrition, cost, and practicality. Always consider the farmer's context: available ingredients, mixing equipment, and storage. Good luck!

<h2>Practical Reference: Quick-Use Tools for Cattle &amp; Steer Feed Formulation</h2>

<p>This reference lesson consolidates the most useful numbers, tables, and procedures from the case studies and practical exercises in this module. It is designed to sit open on your desk (or phone) while you formulate a finishing diet, build a grazing supplement, or troubleshoot a feedlot ration. <p><strong>Why this matters commercially:</strong> feed is typically 6075% of the cost of beef production in African feedlots and semi-intensive systems. A 5% error in a finishing diet's energy density or a 10% over-supply of protein supplement can wipe out the entire margin on a pen of steers. Getting the reference numbers right and knowing when to call in a nutritionist is the difference between a profitable cycle and a loss-making one.</p>

<p style="margin:0; color:#7a1f14;">Never exceed <strong>1% urea in the total diet DM</strong> (or roughly 30% of the total crude protein equivalent) for cattle. Urea must be mixed thoroughly and introduced over 710 days. Never feed urea to starving animals, to animals on low-energy diets without adequate fermentable carbohydrate, or in drinking water. Acute ammonia toxicity can kill within 3090 minutes. Keep a supply of dilute acetic acid (vinegar) on hand and call a vet immediately if symptoms appear (bloat, staggering, excessive salivation). Store urea separately and clearly labelled accidental over-inclusion is a common and fatal error on African farms.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Define the animal and the target.</strong> Live weight, frame size, sex, breed type, expected ADG, and days on feed before you open any software.</li>
<li><strong>Sample and test your ingredients.</strong> A book value is a starting point; your maize bran or cottonseed cake may differ by 1020%. NIR or wet chemistry pays for itself.</li>
<li><strong>Use delivered cost, not farm-gate price.</strong> Include transport, handling, storage losses, and milling. A cheap ingredient 400 km away is often not cheap.</li>
<li><strong>Set realistic constraints.</strong> Minimum roughage, maximum urea, maximum molasses, and toxicity limits (gossypol, aflatoxin, copper).</li>
<li><strong>Check the cationanion balance and Ca:P ratio.</strong> Target Ca:P between 1.5:1 and 2.5:1 for finishing cattle; higher in high-grain diets.</li>
<li><strong>Validate on paper, then in a small pen.</strong> Feed 510 animals for 1014 days. Watch intake, dung consistency, bloat, and any refusals.</li>
<li><strong>Re-run the model when prices move.</strong> A 10% shift in maize bran or molasses price usually changes the optimal mix. Formulate weekly, not yearly.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records and a safety margin.</strong> Record batch weights, intakes, and ADG. Build in a 35% nutrient safety margin for variability in tropical conditions.</li>
</ol>
