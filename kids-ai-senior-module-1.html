Neural Networks by Hand - What the Machine Actually Does

~15 min read20 min gameIntermediate
No device needed

🧠 Brain Opener

A neural network is not magic. It is arithmetic - millions of tiny multiplications and additions, done very fast. You can build a working one on paper in one afternoon.

  • What is a neuron in a neural network?
  • What is a weight?
  • If you know the inputs, the weights, and the maths, can you predict the output?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

A neuron takes a few numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up, and passes the result through a simple rule. A weight is how much each input matters. Yes - if you know the inputs and weights, you can compute the output by hand.

📖 Tinashe Builds a Neuron

Tinashe was fourteen and had heard the phrase "neural network" a hundred times. It sounded like science fiction. His computer science teacher, Mr Ncube, said something strange one day: "A neural network is just arithmetic. Nothing more."

Tinashe did not believe him. He asked Mr Ncube to prove it.

Mr Ncube gave him a piece of paper and a pencil. "A single neuron," he said, "takes some numbers in. Each number is multiplied by a weight. Then all the results are added up. Then we apply a rule to the total. That is it."

"That is not a network," Tinashe said.

"Correct. It is a neuron. Now put three of them side by side, and their outputs feed into a fourth. Now you have a network. The maths is the same. Just done more times."

Tinashe took the paper home. He picked a simple task: deciding whether to carry an umbrella. Inputs: is it cloudy? Did the forecast say rain? Is it already raining? Each of these is either 0 or 1.

He set weights by guessing. Clouds mattered a little - weight 0.3. Forecast mattered a lot - weight 0.6. Already raining mattered most - weight 1.0.

He multiplied, added, and applied the rule. The neuron said: "Yes, carry an umbrella." He checked against reality. It was cloudy, forecast said rain, and it was not yet raining. The neuron was right.

After an hour, his neuron was right on nine out of ten test days. "This is a neural network," he told his sister. "It is arithmetic. I can hold it in my hand."

💡 The Big Idea

A neuron is a weighted sum and a rule

Every neuron does the same three things: multiply each input by a weight, add all the products together, and apply a simple rule to the total. Stack many neurons together and you get a neural network. Stack many networks together and you get deep learning. But every single one is doing the same basic arithmetic - multiply, add, decide.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three-step neuron

Step one: multiply. Each input has a weight. Input times weight gives a contribution. Step two: add. Sum every contribution. Step three: decide. Apply a rule. The rule is usually simple: if the sum is above zero, pass it forward; if below zero, block it.

That is the whole neuron. You can do it on paper in under a minute. The reason real neural networks are so powerful is that they have millions of these neurons, arranged in layers, each one feeding the next.

2

What weights mean

A weight says how much an input matters. A high weight means the input matters a lot. A low weight means it barely matters. A negative weight means the input pushes the answer the other way.

Training is the process of finding the right weights. You start with random weights. The network makes guesses. You tell it whether it was right. It adjusts the weights slightly. Do this a million times and the weights become good.

3

Why depth helps

A single neuron can only draw a straight line through the data. A network of two layers can draw curves. A network of ten layers can draw almost anything - faces, handwriting, voices.

Each layer takes the output of the previous layer and multiplies it by new weights. Layer one might learn edges. Layer two learns shapes. Layer three learns objects. Layer ten learns faces. It is the same arithmetic, repeated at different scales.

🛠 Hands-On - Build a Paper Neuron

You need

  • A notebook or a large piece of paper
  • A pencil
  • A ruler
  1. Write three inputs: cloudy, forecast rain, raining now.
  2. Give each input a weight between 0 and 1. Write them next to the inputs.
  3. For a specific day, decide each input is 0 or 1.
  4. Multiply each input by its weight. Write the products.
  5. Add the products. Write the sum.
  6. Apply the rule: if the sum is above 0.5, carry an umbrella. If below, do not.
  7. Test on ten real days. Adjust the weights until you get at least nine right.
Why it works: This is a real neuron. It is doing exactly what a computer neuron does. The computer does it millions of times per second, but the maths is the same.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

Real neural networks have millions of weights. You can see one in action by asking an AI to explain its own decision.

Try this prompt: "If I train a neuron to decide whether to carry an umbrella, and I set the weight for clouds to 0.9 and the weight for forecast rain to 0.1, what kinds of mistakes will it make?"

Test the answer yourself. Build two neurons with those weights. Run them on 10 real days. Count the mistakes. Compare with the AI prediction.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Google Translate, 2016

Before 2016, Google Translate used rules and statistics. In 2016, it switched to a neural network. Quality jumped overnight. The reason was not a new idea - it was the same arithmetic done with more layers and more weights, trained on more examples.

Nigerian fraud detection

Nigerian banks use small neural networks to spot fake transactions. Each transaction is broken into inputs: amount, time, location, device, recipient. Each input gets a weight. The neuron decides: fraud or not fraud. Millions of times a day.

🏆 Project Challenge

Build a Working Neuron

Build, test, and improve a neuron for a real decision.

  1. Pick a decision you make often - what to eat, what to wear, whether to study now.
  2. List three or four inputs. Each must be 0 or 1.
  3. Assign a starting weight to each.
  4. Write down the last ten times you made this decision. For each, note the inputs and the outcome.
  5. Run your neuron on those ten cases. Count how many it gets right.
  6. Adjust the weights and re-test.
  7. Keep adjusting until you get at least eight out of ten right.
  8. Write 3 sentences about which input has the highest weight and why.
