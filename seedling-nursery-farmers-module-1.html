1. The Seedling Nursery Business Model

Every farmer at the start of every season needs seedlings. Most do not raise their own. This simple gap is one of the most reliable, highest-margin agricultural businesses in Africa — a business that regenerates every planting cycle, year after year.

Module 1 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 1.1 — The seedling nursery model

A seedling nursery is a business that grows young plants (seedlings) from seed or cuttings, then sells them to farmers who plant them into their fields. Simple concept, huge market.

The reason it works:

  • Farmers want to plant. Tomatoes, cabbages, onions, peppers, fruit trees — all start with seedlings.
  • Farmers rarely raise their own. Raising seedlings requires different skills, space, and time from farming. Most farmers would rather buy.
  • Seedlings are cheap. A tomato seedling costs $0.05–$0.15 to produce and sells for $0.15–$0.50. High margin.
  • Volume is enormous. A single hectare of tomatoes needs 25,000–40,000 seedlings. One farmer = one big order.
  • Seasonal repeat. Every planting season, the same customers come back.

What you actually do:

  1. Buy or save quality seed.
  2. Raise seedlings in trays or beds for 3–8 weeks.
  3. Harden them off (accustom to outdoor conditions).
  4. Deliver to farmers at planting time.
  5. Get paid. Repeat next season.
The regeneration rule: Unlike most businesses, this one resets every season with the same customers. A farmer who buys from you in March will buy again in September. The relationship compounds across years.
Practical exercise 1.1: Identify 5 farmers in your area. Ask each: do you buy seedlings or raise your own? What do you grow? What do you pay per seedling? This is your first market survey.

Not all seedlings sell equally. Here are the highest-demand crops in African markets.

Priority crops for a start-up:

  • Tomato. Highest-value common seedling. High margin, big demand, year-round.
  • Cabbage. Cool-season staple. Reliable volume.
  • Kale / spinach. Very high volume, low price per unit but sells in huge numbers.
  • Fruit trees. High price per unit, low volume, long growing season. Good add-on.
The demand rule: Grow what farmers are already planting. Do not try to introduce new crops — that is a different business. Sell into proven demand.
Practical exercise 1.2: Ask 10 farmers what crops they plant. Tally the answers. Your top 3 crops are your starter lineup.

Lesson 1.3 — Profitability per seedling

Here is the real economics for a tomato seedling.

Cost itemPer 1,000 seedlings
Seed (hybrid, high quality)$15
Trays or seedling beds$3 (amortised)
Growing media$8
Water and electricity$4
Labour (your time + helpers)$12
Loss and culling (10–15%)$3
Packaging, transport$5
Total cost$50
Sale price per 1,000 (at $0.15)$150
Gross profit$100 per 1,000 seedlings

Margin: 200% on cost. Or 66% gross margin on sale price.

Scaling:

Seedlings per seasonRevenueGross profit
10,000$1,500$1,000
30,000$4,500$3,000
100,000$15,000$10,000
300,000$45,000$30,000
1,000,000$150,000$100,000

These are realistic for a small nursery with 2–3 employees. A single hectare of tomatoes buys 25,000–40,000 seedlings. Land a contract with 2–3 commercial tomato farmers and you are at 100,000 seedlings per season without much marketing.

The 3-customer rule: Three commercial farmers can sustain a full-time nursery. Do not chase 100 small customers. Find 3 serious farmers, deliver reliably, and grow with them.
Practical exercise 1.3: Build your own profitability table. Use local seed, tray, and labour costs. Compare to local seedling prices. If margin is under 100%, find where costs can be cut.

Lesson 1.4 — Buyer types

Buyer typeVolumePriceReliability
Smallholder farmers100–500 per seasonRetailLow
Commercial farmers20,000–100,000 per seasonWholesaleVery high
Farmer groups / cooperatives50,000–500,000BulkHigh
NGOs and government programmes100,000+ContractVery high (with paperwork)
Agro-dealers and garden shops5,000–20,000WholesaleMedium
Home gardeners10–100Retail (higher)Low, seasonal

Best plan for a start-up:

  1. Start with 5–10 commercial farmers. Each needs 20,000+ seedlings per season. That is 100,000–200,000 seedlings volume right there.
  2. Add 1–2 farmer groups or cooperatives. Bulk orders, reliable payment.
  3. Ignore NGO contracts until you have proven production capacity. Too much paperwork for beginners.
  4. Add home gardeners later as a small retail channel.
Practical exercise 1.4: List 10 commercial farmers in your area. Visit each. Ask about their planting plans and seedling needs. Convert 2 of them into committed customers.

Lesson 1.6 — Common beginner mistakes

MistakeConsequenceFix
Cheap seedPoor germination, weak plantsBuy certified or hybrid seed from trusted source
Overcrowding traysSpindly, weak seedlingsCorrect density (see module 4)
Inconsistent wateringDamping-off, die-offWater regularly, not too much
Not hardening offSeedlings die in the field7-day hardening off
Selling too earlyCustomer disappointmentOnly sell when ready
No recordsCannot identify problemsTrack every tray
Promising more than you can produceLost customers, reputationPromise 80% of your capacity
Starting with 20 cropsSpread thin, quality suffersStart with 2 crops, master them
The focus rule: Master 2 crops before adding a third. Quality beats variety in this business. Farmers care about consistent, reliable seedlings — not a wide selection.
