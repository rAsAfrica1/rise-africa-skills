1. Identifying the rootstock, scion, and bud in local fruit trees

When you graft, you join two different trees into one. The bottom part, with the roots, is the rootstock. The top part, which you cut from a good fruiting tree and join on, is the scion. A bud is a small growing point taken from the scion tree and inserted into the rootstock. In a mango tree in your compound, the rootstock is the seedling grown from a seed with strong roots. The scion is a piece of branch from a tree that gives sweet fruit. The bud is a single eye cut with a small shield of bark.

You can find these parts on any local fruit tree. Look at a mature avocado. The trunk below the graft union is the rootstock. Feel for a slight swelling or scar about 20-30 cm above the soil where the original graft was made. Above that line is the scion, the part that produces the fruit you eat. On a citrus tree, the rootstock is often rough lemon or rough lemon-type seedling, and the scion is the sweet orange or tangerine. The buds are the small points along the green young branches, sitting in the leaf axils.

To identify a bud, look for a small, slightly raised eye where a leaf joins the stem. On mango, buds are plump and green when active. On citrus, they are tiny and may be hidden under the leaf stalk. Use a sharp knife or a budding knife to cut a bud with a thin slice of wood and bark. The bud is the future branch. The rootstock supplies water and minerals. The scion supplies the fruit quality. When you understand these three parts, you can plan your grafting work.

In Africa, many farmers use local rootstocks because they survive drought and poor soil. For example, in Kenya, mango rootstock is often a local seedling that tolerates heat. The scion comes from a named variety like Apple or Ngowe. The bud is taken from the same scion tree. Always label your trees so you know which part is which. A simple paint mark at the graft union helps. If you cannot tell the rootstock from the scion, you may cut the wrong part and lose the tree.

Practice on a branch of a guava or citrus. Cut a small branch and look for the bud eyes. Then trace down to the older wood. This is the rootstock side if the tree was grafted. With time, you will see the difference clearly. This skill is the foundation of all grafting and budding work.

2. How the cambium layer works and why it's key to grafting success

The cambium is a thin layer of living cells just under the bark. It is only a few cells thick, but it is the engine of growth in stems. When you cut a branch, the cambium is the green or white slippery layer between the bark and the wood. In grafting, the cambium of the scion must touch the cambium of the rootstock. If they do not touch, the graft fails. This is the single most important rule.

Think of the cambium as a sheet of tiny bricks that can divide. When it divides, it makes new wood on the inside and new bark on the outside. When you wound a tree, the cambium around the wound starts to grow and form a callus. In a graft, the callus from the scion and the rootstock meet and join. This is called graft union. The union is strong only if the cambium layers are aligned. You need at least one side of the cambium to match, but matching both sides is better.

To see the cambium, cut a young branch of mango or citrus. Peel back the bark gently with your thumb. The slippery, wet layer you see is the cambium. It is easy to damage, so never touch it with dirty hands or let it dry out. When you make a grafting cut, work quickly. The cambium dies if it dries. In dry African conditions, you may need to wrap the graft with polythene tape to keep it moist.

The cambium also explains why the size of the scion and rootstock matters. If the scion is much thinner than the rootstock, you can still align one side. But if the scion is thicker, you may need to shave the rootstock to match. For budding, you slip a bud with a small piece of cambium under the bark of the rootstock. The cambium of the bud must touch the cambium of the rootstock. This is why you cut a T-shaped slit and slide the bud in.

In practice, keep your knife sharp and clean. A blunt knife crushes the cambium. Use a grafting knife or a new razor blade. Make one clean cut. Do not saw. After joining, tie the union tightly with budding tape or polythene strips. The tape holds the cambium together until it heals. Check for cambium contact by looking at the cut surface. You want a smooth, wet line. If it is dry or brown, recut. With practice, you will feel the cambium and know it is right.

3. Recognizing active growth stages in mango, avocado, and citrus

Grafting works best when the tree is actively growing. Active growth means the cambium is dividing and sap is flowing. In mango, avocado, and citrus, you can see active growth by looking at the new leaves and shoots. In mango, active growth shows as new reddish-brown leaves that turn green. The buds swell and new shoots appear. This usually happens at the start of the rains or after a dry spell. In East Africa, mango flushes two to three times a year. The best time to graft is when the flush is just starting, not when it is finished.

Avocado growth is different. Avocado grows in flushes too, but the leaves are pale green and soft. The buds are large and green. Active growth in avocado often happens in warm, wet months. In Kenya, avocado grafting is done in March to May and September to November when the weather is mild and humid. Avoid grafting when the tree is flowering or when it is too dry. If the tree is stressed, the graft will not take.

Citrus is ever-growing in warm areas. It flushes several times a year. Look for new shoots with light green leaves and small thorns. The cambium is active when the bark slips easily. This is the best time for budding. In citrus, you can test if the bark slips by making a small cut and trying to lift the bark. If it peels cleanly, the tree is ready. If it sticks, wait. In South Africa, citrus budding is done in spring and autumn. In West Africa, it is done at the start of the rains.

You can also look at the weather. Graft when the sap is rising. In many parts of Africa, this is just before the rains or during the early rains. Avoid the hot, dry season when the tree is dormant. Avoid heavy rains too, because water can rot the graft. A simple rule: if the tree is making new leaves, it is a good time to graft. If it is resting, wait.

To check growth stage, mark a branch and observe it for a week. Look for swelling buds and new shoots. If you see them, prepare your tools. If not, wait for the next flush. This timing is as important as the cut. A perfect graft on a dormant tree will fail. A simple graft on an active tree will often succeed.

4. The difference between grafting and budding in simple terms

Grafting and budding are two ways to join a scion to a rootstock. In grafting, you use a piece of branch with several buds. In budding, you use a single bud. That is the main difference. Both methods join the cambium layers, but the size of the scion differs. Grafting is used when the rootstock and scion are about the same thickness, or when you want a strong, quick union. Budding is used when the rootstock is bigger than the bud, and when you want to save scion wood.

In grafting, you cut the rootstock and the scion to fit together. Common methods are cleft grafting, whip grafting, and side grafting. For example, in mango, cleft grafting is common. You cut the rootstock stem and split it, then insert a wedge-shaped scion with 2-3 buds. You tie it and cover with a polythene bag to keep humidity. In budding, you make a T-shaped cut in the rootstock bark, lift the bark, and slide in a bud with a small shield of bark. You then tie it. The bud stays dormant or grows into a new shoot.

Budding is faster and uses less scion wood. One bud can make one new tree. A single branch can provide many buds. Grafting uses more wood but gives a stronger union faster. In citrus, budding is the standard method. In avocado, grafting is more common because the wood is easier to handle. In mango, both are used, but grafting is often preferred for large-scale nurseries.

The choice also depends on the season. Budding requires the bark to slip, which happens during active growth. Grafting can be done when the bark is not slipping, as long as the cambium is active. In dry areas, grafting with a polythene cover is more reliable because it keeps moisture. Budding is more delicate and needs careful handling.

For a beginner, start with budding on citrus because the bark slips easily and the bud is small. Then try grafting on mango. Always use a sharp knife. For budding, a budding knife with a special bark lifter is helpful. For grafting, a grafting knife is fine. Whichever method you choose, the cambium must touch. The difference is only in the size of the scion and the technique. Both give you a new tree with the fruit you want.

5. How sap flow and season affect graft take

Sap flow is the movement of water and nutrients in the tree. When sap is flowing, the cambium is active and the graft can heal. When sap is slow, the graft may dry out or fail. Sap flow depends on the season, the weather, and the health of the tree. In Africa, the best time to graft is when the sap is rising, usually at the start of the rains. During this time, the tree is waking up and making new growth. The cambium is dividing, and the graft union can form quickly.

In the dry season, sap flow slows down. The tree may drop its leaves and go dormant. Grafting then is risky. The scion may dry out before the union forms. If you must graft in the dry season, you need to water the rootstock regularly and cover the graft with a polythene bag to keep humidity. In very hot areas, shade the graft. In the rainy season, sap flow is high, but too much rain can cause rot. Avoid grafting in heavy rain. A light rain or humid weather is ideal.

Temperature also affects sap flow. In cold highland areas, sap flow starts later. In warm lowlands, it starts earlier. For example, in the Kenyan highlands, avocado grafting is best in March to May and September to November. In the hot coastal lowlands, mango grafting can be done in May to July and October to December. Know your local climate. Talk to other farmers. Keep records of when your grafts take.

Sap flow also depends on the rootstock. A healthy, well-watered rootstock has good sap flow. A stressed rootstock has poor sap flow. Before grafting, water the rootstock well for a week. Do not graft on a sick tree. The scion also needs to be fresh. Collect scion wood in the morning when the tree is full of sap. Wrap it in moist paper or banana leaf. Use it within a day or two. If you store it, keep it cool and moist.

After grafting, sap flow continues to move through the union. If the graft takes, you will see new shoots from the scion in 2-4 weeks. If the scion turns brown and shrivels, the graft failed. Do not give up. Try again in the next season. With practice, you will learn the best time for your area. Sap flow is your guide. Watch the tree, and it will tell you when to graft.

6. Hands-on: Dissecting a branch to see cambium and buds

For this lesson, you need a sharp knife, a young branch of mango, avocado, or citrus, and a magnifying glass if you have one. Cut a branch about 30 cm long and as thick as your finger. Choose a branch that is green or slightly brown, not old and woody. Sit in a shaded place. Work on a clean surface. The goal is to see the cambium and the buds clearly.

First, look at the branch. Find the buds. They are small, raised points where leaves or leaf scars are. On mango, buds are plump and green. On citrus, they are tiny and may be hidden. Use your knife to make a shallow cut just above a bud. Then slice a thin layer of bark away from the bud. You will see a small, green or white core inside the bud. That is the growing point. Do not damage it. Now, make a lengthwise cut along the branch, about 5 cm long. Peel the bark back gently with your thumb. The slippery, wet layer between the bark and the wood is the cambium. It may look like a thin film of green or white. That is what you need for grafting.

Next, cut the branch across. Look at the cut end. You will see three parts: the outer bark, the cambium (a thin ring), and the inner wood. The cambium is the ring just under the bark. In a young branch, it is easy to see. In an old branch, it is thinner. Use your magnifying glass to see it better. This ring is where new wood and bark are made. When you graft, you must line up this ring on the scion with the ring on the rootstock.

Now, practice making a graft cut on the branch. Cut a small scion with 2 buds. Cut the rootstock branch and make a matching cut. Try to fit them so the cambium rings touch. Do not tie them. Just look. Can you see the cambium contact? If not, recut. This is a safe way to practice without losing a real tree. Do this with 5 branches. Each time, check the cambium. You will learn to feel it.

After practice, clean your knife. Store it dry. Collect the cut branches and use them as mulch. Wash your hands. This hands-on work builds your confidence. When you graft a real tree, you will know what to look for. The cambium and buds are not mysterious. They are right there under your knife. With practice, you will make clean cuts and successful grafts.

<h2>Practical Reference: Understanding Plant Parts and How They Grow</h2>
<p><strong>Module:</strong> Understanding Plant Parts and How They Grow &nbsp;|&nbsp; <strong>Course:</strong> Grafting, Budding &amp; Orchards &nbsp;|&nbsp; <strong>Level:</strong> RISE AFRICA Skills</p>

<p>Grafting is not just a horticultural technique for African fruit growers it is a business tool. A well-grafted mango, avocado or citrus tree can start producing marketable fruit years earlier than a seedling, carry a superior variety on a locally adapted root system, and multiply a high-value mother tree into dozens of saleable nursery plants. But none of that happens unless you understand the four plant parts that make grafting work: the <strong>rootstock</strong>, the <strong>scion</strong>, the <strong>bud</strong>, and the thin, living ribbon of cells between bark and wood called the <strong>cambium</strong>. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the field-level knowledge you need before you make your first cut.</p>

<h2>1. Identifying the Rootstock, Scion, and Bud</h2>
<p>Every grafted tree is a partnership of two individuals. The <strong>rootstock</strong> is the lower portion usually a seedling or cutting grown for its roots. It supplies water, anchors the tree, and often confers resistance to soil pests and drought. The <strong>scion</strong> is the upper portion a piece of shoot, or a single bud, taken from the variety you want to sell. The <strong>bud</strong> is a compressed, undeveloped shoot sitting in the leaf axil; in budding, a single bud plus a sliver of bark is inserted under the rootstock bark instead of a whole shoot.</p>

<p>In African orchards, rootstocks are chosen for local realities. Mango growers in Kenya and Tanzania often use polyembryonic seedlings of local mango as rootstock because they are vigorous and adapted to hot, dry conditions. Avocado growers use seedling rootstocks (often 'Hass' seedling or local races) where <em>Phytophthora</em> root rot is not severe, and clonal rootstocks where it is. Citrus growers in South Africa and Zimbabwe use rough lemon, sour orange, or 'Carrizo' citrange depending on soil pH, salinity, and tristeza virus pressure. The scion is always the market variety 'Kent' or 'Apple' mango, 'Hass' avocado, 'Valencia' or 'Navel' orange.</p>

<h2>2. How the Cambium Layer Works</h2>
<p>The cambium is a single-cell-thick cylinder of meristematic tissue between the bark (phloem) and the wood (xylem). It is the only tissue capable of producing the new cells that form a successful graft union. When scion and rootstock cambium are pressed together and kept moist, the cambium cells divide and produce <strong>callus</strong> a mass of undifferentiated cells. Within days to weeks, callus bridges the gap, then differentiates into new xylem and phloem, reconnecting the plant's plumbing. If cambium is not in contact, no union forms, no matter how neatly you cut.</p>

<p>This is why graft cuts must be smooth, fresh, and made with a sharp, clean blade. A ragged cut exposes dead cells, and the cambium dries out within minutes in African sun. Wrap the union tightly with grafting tape or polythene strip to exclude air and hold cambium to cambium. For budding, the cambium of the bud shield must sit against the cambium of the rootstock not against dead bark.</p>

<h2>3. Recognizing Active Growth Stages</h2>
<p>Grafting succeeds when the plant is actively growing but not flushing so hard that it rejects the graft. In mango, the best window in East Africa is the cool, dry period after the main harvest, when the tree is between flushes. In avocado, graft when sap is rising in early spring or after the rains, avoiding the peak of the dry season. In citrus, the classic window is when the bark slips easily usually during active spring growth so buds can be inserted cleanly.</p>

<h2>4. Grafting vs. Budding in Simple Terms</h2>
<p><strong>Grafting</strong> joins a piece of shoot (with several buds) onto a rootstock. <strong>Budding</strong> joins a single bud, with a small shield of bark, under the rootstock bark. Grafting is faster where the scion is thick and the rootstock is large; budding uses less scion wood, is ideal when scion material is scarce, and often gives a cleaner union. For commercial nurseries in Africa, budding is usually the more economical method for citrus and avocado, while grafting is common for mango and for top-working mature trees.</p>

<h2>5. How Sap Flow and Season Affect Graft Take</h2>
<p>Sap flow drives graft take. When the rootstock is actively translocating water and nutrients, callus forms quickly and the union knits. When the plant is dormant, drought-stressed, or flushing hard, the graft either fails or is pushed off. In African conditions, the two biggest enemies of graft take are <strong>heat</strong> and <strong>water stress</strong>. Graft early in the morning, shade the nursery, and irrigate the rootstock the day before. Avoid grafting during the hottest weeks or when the tree is carrying a heavy fruit load.</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#b71c1c;"><strong> SAFETY WARNING SHARP TOOLS AND SAP</strong></p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Grafting knives and budding blades are razor-sharp and cause deep cuts. Always cut away from your body, keep fingers behind the blade, and never test sharpness on your skin. Wear cut-resistant gloves when handling scion wood. Some plant saps notably mango and some <em>Euphorbia</em> relatives can cause skin irritation or allergic reactions; wash sap off immediately with soap and water. Disinfect blades between trees with 70% alcohol or a 10% bleach solution to prevent spreading disease. Keep a first-aid kit within reach of every grafting bench.</p>

<h2>6. Hands-On: Dissecting a Branch to See Cambium and Buds</h2>
<p>Before you graft, dissect. Take a healthy branch about 1 cm thick from a mango, avocado, or citrus tree. With a sharp knife, make a shallow longitudinal cut and peel back the bark. You will see:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Outer bark</strong> dead, protective tissue.</li>
<li><strong>Inner bark (phloem)</strong> the greenish, slippery layer that carries sugars down.</li>
<li><strong>Cambium</strong> the thin, wet, almost invisible layer between bark and wood. This is your target.</li>
<li><strong>Wood (xylem)</strong> the hard, pale tissue carrying water up.</li>
<li><strong>Buds</strong> small, pointed structures in the leaf axils, each capable of becoming a new shoot.</li>
</ul>
<p>Practise aligning cambium to cambium on discarded branches before you work on valuable nursery stock. A grower who can find the cambium by feel will graft successfully; a grower who cannot will fail even with perfect technique on paper.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Identify rootstock, scion, and bud on a live branch before cutting.</li>
<li>Confirm the tree is in active but not flushing growth.</li>
<li>Irrigate rootstock the day before grafting; avoid drought stress.</li>
<li>Use a sharp, disinfected blade; make smooth, single cuts.</li>
<li>Align cambium to cambium this is non-negotiable.</li>
<li>Wrap tightly with grafting tape to exclude air and hold moisture.</li>
<li>Shade the nursery and graft in the cool of the morning.</li>
<li>Label each plant with variety and date; monitor for bud break in 24 weeks.</li>
</ol>
