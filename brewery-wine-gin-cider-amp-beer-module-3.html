1. Malting Your Own Sorghum and Millet at Home

To malt sorghum or millet, start with clean grain. Use 5 kg of sorghum or millet for a 50-liter batch. Rinse the grain in a bucket, removing stones and debris. Soak the grain in water for 24 hours, then drain. Spread the grain in a thin layer (5 cm deep) on a clean concrete floor or plastic sheet in a shaded, well-ventilated area. Keep it moist by sprinkling water twice daily. After 2-3 days, you will see small white sprouts (rootlets) emerging. This is germination. Stop germination when the sprout is about 1-2 cm longâusually 3-4 days. If you let it grow too long, you lose sugars.

Next, dry the malted grain. Sun-dry it on a clean mat for 2-3 days until it is brittle. In humid regions, use a solar dryer or a low oven (50Â°C) with the door ajar. Once dry, remove the rootlets by rubbing the grain between your hands or in a sack. Winnow away the rootlets. You now have base malt. For flavor, you can lightly toast some malt in a dry pan over low heat until it smells like biscuitsâthis gives color and taste. Store malt in dry sacks in a cool place. It keeps for months.

Milling: Crush the malt coarsely. You want cracked kernels, not flour. A hand-cranked grain mill works; set the gap to about 1 mm. If you have no mill, put grain in a strong sack and pound with a wooden pestleâjust enough to crack. Too fine a crush leads to stuck sparges. For a 50-liter batch, you need about 5 kg of malt. This process saves money and gives you control over your beer's character.

2. Mashing and Lautering: Step-by-Step for a 50-Liter Batch

Mashing converts starch to sugar. Heat 30 liters of water to 65Â°C in a large pot (at least 60 liters). Add your 5 kg of crushed malt slowly, stirring to avoid lumps. The temperature will drop; adjust with small amounts of hot or cold water to hold 63-67Â°C. This is the mash. Keep it at this temperature for 60 minutes. Stir every 15 minutes. The enzymes work best in this range. If you want a drier beer, mash at 63Â°C; for fuller body, 67Â°C.

After 60 minutes, check for conversion: put a drop of mash liquid on a white plate with a drop of iodine. If it turns blue-black, starch remainsâmash longer. If it stays yellow-brown, conversion is done. Now you must separate the sweet liquid (wort) from the grain. This is lautering. You need a false bottom or a large sieve. If you have a second pot, place a colander lined with cheesecloth over it. Pour the mash into the colander. The first runnings will be cloudy; pour them back gently.

Then sparge: heat 20 liters of water to 75Â°C. Slowly pour this water over the grain bed, rinsing out remaining sugars. Collect the wort in your boiling pot. You should end up with about 45 liters of wort. Do not squeeze the grainâthat releases tannins. Stop sparging when the runoff tastes watery or reaches a specific gravity of 1.010 (if you have a hydrometer). You now have sweet wort ready for boiling. Total time: about 2 hours. Clean everything with hot water and unscented detergent.

3. Boiling and Hopping: Using Local Hops and Bittering Agents

Boil the wort vigorously for 60 minutes. This sterilizes, concentrates, and extracts bitterness from hops. If you have hops (local or imported), add 20 grams at the start of the boil for bittering. If you cannot find hops, use bittering agents: 30 grams of dried bitter leaf (Vernonia amygdalina) or 20 grams of gentian root. These are common in Africa. Add them in a muslin bag. At 15 minutes before the end, add another 10 grams of hops (or 10 grams of bitter leaf) for flavor. At 5 minutes, add 10 grams for aroma.

Stir occasionally. Watch for boiloversâreduce heat if foam rises. After 60 minutes, turn off heat. Remove hop bags. You can also add a handful of crushed grains (like roasted sorghum) at the end for color and flavor. Now cool the wort quickly. In hot climates, use a wort chiller if available; otherwise, place the pot in a tub of cold water and ice. Stir gently. You need to cool to 20-25Â°C within 30 minutes to avoid infection.

Once cooled, transfer to a sanitized fermenter, leaving behind sediment. Splash the wort to aerateâthis helps yeast. Take a hydrometer reading: original gravity should be around 1.040-1.050. If higher, add boiled and cooled water. Now it's ready for yeast. Remember: all equipment touching wort after boiling must be sanitized with a no-rinse sanitizer or bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 liters).

4. Fermentation: Managing Temperature in Hot Climates

Fermentation turns sugar into alcohol and CO2. In hot climates, keeping beer cool is the biggest challenge. Ideal temperature for ale yeast is 18-22Â°C. Above 25Â°C, you get off-flavors (fruity, solvent-like). If you have no fridge, use a swamp cooler: place fermenter in a large basin, wrap a wet towel around it, and point a fan at it. Add ice bottles to the water. This can drop temperature by 5-10Â°C. Alternatively, ferment at night and insulate during the day.

Pitch yeast: use 10 grams of dry ale yeast per 50 liters. Rehydrate in 100 ml of 30Â°C water for 15 minutes, then add to wort. Seal fermenter with an airlock. Fermentation starts within 24 hoursâyou'll see bubbles. Keep it cool for 4-7 days. Check gravity after 4 days. When gravity is stable for two days (around 1.010), fermentation is done.

If you want a sour beer, you can allow natural fermentation with wild yeast, but this is risky. For clean beer, always use commercial yeast. After fermentation, siphon beer off the yeast cake into a sanitized secondary fermenter or bottling bucket. Avoid splashingâoxygen causes stale flavors. You can dry hop now (add 20 grams of hops) for aroma, leaving for 3 days. Then cold crash if possible: put fermenter in a cool place (4Â°C) for 2 days to clear. In hot climates, skip cold crash and use finings like gelatin. Now beer is ready to bottle.

5. Bottling and Carbonation: Priming with Sugar and Capping

Before bottling, sanitize everything: bottles, caps, siphon, and bottling bucket. Use 1 tablespoon of bleach per 4 liters of water, rinse well, or use no-rinse sanitizer. Prepare priming sugar: for 50 liters, use 150-200 grams of table sugar. Boil it in 500 ml of water for 5 minutes, then cool. Pour this into your bottling bucket. Siphon beer from fermenter into bottling bucket, avoiding splashing. The sugar will mix evenly.

Fill bottles using a bottling wand or a siphon with a clamp. Leave 2-3 cm of headspace. Cap immediately with a capper. If you don't have a capper, use flip-top bottles or crown caps with a hand capper. Store bottles at 20-25Â°C for 2 weeks to carbonate. Yeast will eat the sugar and produce CO2. After 2 weeks, test one bottle: it should be fizzy. If not, wait another week.

Chill before serving. Carbonation levels: for a typical ale, aim for 2.2-2.5 volumes of CO2. That's about 150 grams of sugar for 50 liters. Too much sugar causes bottle bombsânever exceed 250 grams. Always use sturdy bottles (not thin glass). Store in a cool, dark place. Beer is best within 3 months. If you want to bottle condition with less sediment, add finings before bottling. Now you have your own beer from grain to bottle.

6. Troubleshooting Common Beer Faults: Sourness, Cloudiness, and Flatness

Sourness: If your beer tastes sharply sour, it's likely infected with bacteria (Lactobacillus or Acetobacter). This happens from poor sanitation or leaving beer exposed to air. To prevent, sanitize all equipment with bleach or no-rinse sanitizer, and keep airlocks filled. If sourness is mild, you can drink it as a sour beer, but if it smells like vinegar, discard. Cloudiness: Hazy beer can be from starch (incomplete mash), yeast in suspension, or chill haze. To fix, mash longer at 65Â°C, use finings like Irish moss (1 teaspoon per 20 liters) in the last 15 minutes of boil, or cold crash. For yeast haze, use a secondary fermenter and siphon carefully.

Flatness: If bottles don't carbonate, the yeast may be dead (too old or too hot), or you forgot priming sugar. Check that you added the right amount. If yeast is dead, you can add a pinch of fresh yeast to each bottle, but this is tricky. Better to use fresh yeast at bottling. Also, ensure bottles are sealed tightly. If you used flip-tops, check gaskets.

Other faults: Buttery flavor (diacetyl) from incomplete fermentationâlet beer sit longer. Fruity flavors from high fermentation temperatureâuse swamp cooler. Always keep records: temperature, gravity, dates. This helps you repeat good batches and avoid faults. With practice, you'll master your local ingredients.

Practical Reference: Brewing Beer from Grain to Bottle in African Small-Scale Breweries

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single working document for African brewers producing beer at 20â100 litre scale. It is written for people who intend to sell beer, not just brew it. Why Sorghum and Millet Matter for African Brewers

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) and pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) are the backbone of traditional African brewing and remain the most economically rational base grains for small commercial breweries across the continent. They are drought-tolerant, locally traded, and typically 30â60% cheaper per kilogram than imported barley malt. The trade-off is that they lack the diastatic enzyme power of barley unless properly malted, and their huskless kernels make lautering more difficult. The solution is a hybrid grist: 60â70% malted sorghum or millet plus 30â40% barley malt (if available) or exogenous enzyme (Î±-amylase and glucoamylase) to guarantee conversion. This is standard practice in Nigerian and South African commercial sorghum beer production, and it is the fastest route to a repeatable product for a small brewery.

â ï¸ SAFETY WARNING: Bottle Bombs and Methanol Risk

Never bottle before fermentation is complete. If you bottle beer that still has fermentable sugar and add priming sugar, you create a pressurised glass grenade. Bottle bombs can cause severe lacerations and blindness. Always confirm terminal gravity is stable over 3 consecutive days before priming. Use only pressure-rated glass bottles (not thin decorative bottles) or PET bottles. Store conditioning bottles in a closed box or cupboard, never near your face or children.

Never distil your beer or fermented wash without proper equipment and training. Improper distillation concentrates methanol, which causes blindness and death. Methanol is produced by pectin breakdown, especially in fruit-based washes. If you are producing gin or spirits, use only tested recipes, discard foreshots (the first 50â100 mL of distillate), and never drink heads. This module covers beer and cider onlyâdistillation requires separate certified training.

Quick Reference Checklist: 50 L Batch Day

  1. Sanitise everything that touches cooled wort. Fermenter, siphon, airlock, thermometer, hands. No-rinse sanitiser at correct dilution. Infection is the #1 cause of sour beer in African small breweries.
  2. Check your grain. Malted sorghum should smell sweet and nutty, not mouldy. Reject any grain with visible mouldâmycotoxins survive boiling.
  3. Mill coarsely. You want cracked kernels, not flour. Flour clogs the lauter and gives astringent beer. If using huskless sorghum, add 5% rice husks to the mash.
  4. Hit your mash temperature. 65 Â°C for 60â90 minutes. Stir every 15 minutes. Confirm conversion with an iodine testâa drop of mash on a white plate with iodine should stay yellow-brown, not turn blue-black.
  5. Sparge slowly. Stop when runnings drop below 1.010 gravity or pH 5.8. Faster is not betterâyou are rinsing, not flushing.
  6. Boil hard for 60 minutes. Add bittering agent at the start, aroma at 5 minutes remaining. Sanitise your fermenter during the boil.
  7. Cool fast and pitch healthy yeast. Get from 100 Â°C to 22 Â°C in under 30 minutes. Every minute above 30 Â°C is an infection risk. Pitch yeast at 22 Â°C, not 35 Â°Câhot pitching kills yeast and invites bacteria.
  8. Control fermentation temperature daily. Check twice a day. If your water bath bottles have melted, replace them. Stable 20 Â°C produces clean beer; fluctuating 28 Â°C produces solvent.
  9. Confirm terminal gravity before bottling. Two identical readings 3 days apart. Prime with 7 g/L table sugar dissolved in boiled water. Cap immediately and condition at 20 Â°C for 14 days.
  10. Calculate your cost per litre every batch. Grain, yeast, hops, sugar, bottles, gas, labour. If you do not know your cost, you cannot price for profit. Target 60% gross margin minimum.

Business-First Closing Note

Your beer is a product, not a hobby. The difference between a brewer who sells out every batch and one who pours beer down the drain is not talentâit is record-keeping and consistency. Write down every batch: grain weight, mash temperature, gravity readings, fermentation temperature, bottling date, and tasting notes. After ten batches, you will know exactly what your market wants and exactly what it costs you to make it. That is the foundation of a brewery business, whether you are in Nairobi, Accra, or a village in Malawi. Start small, measure everything, and reinvest your margin into temperature control and sanitation. Those two investments will make you profitable faster than any new recipe.
