1. Introduction to Belt Grinders and Abrasives

A belt grinder is the workhorse of any bladesmithing shop. In Africa, you can build a simple 2x72-inch belt grinder using a 1.5 kW (2 HP) electric motor, a set of wheels, and a steel frame. Many makers in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa start with a locally fabricated grinder to keep costs down. The most common belt size is 2x72 inches (50x1830 mm), but 2x48 or 4x36 also work. Belts come in different abrasives: aluminium oxide for wood and mild steel, zirconia for heavy grinding on hardened steel, and ceramic for fast cutting and long life. Grit sizes range from 24 (very coarse) to 2000 (very fine). Start with 36 or 50 grit for profiling, then move to 120, 220, 400, and up to 800 for finishing. Always grind with the belt moving away from the edge to avoid overheating and ruining the temper. Use a speed of around 1500-3000 surface feet per minute (SFM) for steel. For a 2x72 belt, that means a motor RPM of about 1750 with a 4-inch drive wheel. Keep a container of water nearby to cool the blade; dip frequently. Safety first: wear eye protection, a dust mask, and never wear loose clothing. In many African workshops, you'll find grinders made from repurposed car alternators or washing machine motors, but a dedicated motor is better. Learn to change belts quickly and track them properly. A well-tuned grinder with sharp belts will save you hours of hand sanding.

2. Grinding Bevels: Flat, Saber, and Hollow

Bevels are the ground angles that form the cutting edge. There are three main types: flat, saber, and hollow. Flat grinds are the easiest for beginners. You grind the entire side of the blade at a consistent angle, usually 2-5 degrees per side, until you reach the desired edge thickness. For a chef's knife, aim for a bevel height of about 15-20 mm and an edge thickness of 0.5 mm before sharpening. Saber grinds have a flat bevel that doesn't go all the way to the spine; the top part of the blade remains flat. This is common on bushcraft knives. To grind a saber bevel, mark a line on the blade where the bevel should stop, then grind up to that line. Hollow grinds are made with a contact wheel, usually 6 to 10 inches in diameter. The wheel creates a concave curve. Hollow grinds are popular on hunting knives and straight razors. Use a wheel that matches your desired curve. For all grinds, the key is consistency: keep the blade moving, use light pressure, and let the belt do the work. In Africa, many smiths use a simple jig made from angle iron to hold the blade at a constant angle. You can also grind freehand, but practice on scrap steel first. After grinding, check for symmetry by looking at the blade from the top and bottom. The bevels should be even. If one side is wider, grind a little more on that side. Always grind with the edge facing away from the belt rotation to avoid catching. Cool the blade often in water to prevent overheating. A good grind sets the foundation for a sharp, durable edge.

3. Achieving Symmetry and Clean Plunge Lines

Symmetry is what separates a professional-looking blade from a homemade one. Plunge lines are the vertical transitions where the bevel meets the ricasso (the flat part above the handle). Clean plunge lines are sharp and even on both sides. To achieve this, first ensure your blade is flat and parallel. Use a file guide or a grinding jig to set your angle. Many African bladesmiths make a simple jig from a piece of hardwood and a bolt. Clamp the blade in the jig, set the angle, and grind one side. Then flip the blade and grind the other side using the same jig setting. This ensures the bevels match. For plunge lines, use a file guide clamped to the ricasso. This acts as a hard stop for the belt. Grind slowly and check often. A common mistake is to grind too much on one side. Use a marker to color the bevel; as you grind, the marker disappears, showing you where you've cut. When you're close to the final shape, switch to a finer belt (e.g., 220 grit) and use light pressure. For the plunge line, you can also use a round file to refine the transition. After grinding, inspect the blade under good light. Look for any uneven spots. If one bevel is wider, grind a little more on that side. Remember, it's easier to remove more steel than to put it back. So go slow. Once you have symmetry, you can move to hand sanding. A well-ground blade with clean plunge lines will need less sanding and will look much better. Practice on mild steel until you can consistently produce symmetrical bevels.

4. Hand Sanding and Polishing Techniques

Hand sanding is the most time-consuming part of bladesmithing, but it's what gives a blade its final look. After grinding, start with a 120-grit sandpaper to remove deep scratches. Then move to 220, 400, 600, 800, and up to 2000 for a mirror polish. Always sand in one direction, then change direction 90 degrees when you switch grits. This helps you see when you've removed the previous scratches. Use a sanding block or a piece of hardwood with sandpaper wrapped around it. For African makers, locally available sandpaper from hardware stores works fine. Waterproof paper is best because you can use it with water or oil to reduce clogging. For a satin finish, stop at 400 or 600 grit and sand in one direction. For a mirror finish, continue to 2000 grit and then use a buffing wheel with white compound. When sanding, keep the blade flat on the block to avoid rounding the edges. If you're sanding a bevel, use a backing block that matches the angle. Many smiths use a piece of glass or a granite tile as a backing surface. For the handle, sand to at least 400 grit for a smooth feel. If you're working with wood, raise the grain with a damp cloth between grits. For a polished blade, after sanding, clean it with acetone or alcohol to remove any oil. Then apply a thin coat of oil or wax to protect it from rust. In humid African climates, rust is a constant enemy, so a protective finish is essential. Remember, patience is key. Rushing hand sanding will show in the final product.

5. Etching and Finishing for Pattern-Welded Steel

Pattern-welded steel, such as Damascus, shows its beauty through etching. After grinding and sanding to at least 600 grit, clean the blade thoroughly with acetone or dish soap to remove all oil and dirt. Then mix a solution of ferric chloride and water. In Africa, ferric chloride can be found at electronics supply shops or made by dissolving iron in hydrochloric acid (careful!). A common ratio is 1 part ferric chloride to 3 parts water. For a stronger etch, use 1:1. Wear gloves and eye protection. Dip the blade in the solution for 5-10 minutes. The acid eats the softer steel faster, revealing the pattern. Check every minute. When the pattern is deep enough, remove the blade and rinse with water, then neutralize with baking soda solution. For a contrast, you can lightly sand the blade with 2000 grit to remove the black oxides from the raised areas, leaving the darker etched areas. This is called "popping" the pattern. For a final finish, apply a coat of oil or wax. Some smiths use a coffee etch to darken the steel further. To do this, soak the blade in strong black coffee for 24 hours. This works well on carbon steel Damascus. After etching, the blade is susceptible to rust, so oil it immediately. In Africa, where humidity is high, consider using a protective lacquer or a thin layer of beeswax. Always work in a well-ventilated area when etching. Dispose of the acid properly; never pour it down the drain. With practice, you can achieve stunning patterns that make your knives stand out.

6. Sharpening and Honing the Edge

Sharpening is the final step. A sharp knife is safe and effective. Start by establishing a bevel angle. For most knives, 15-20 degrees per side is good. Use a sharpening stone or a belt grinder with a fine belt (e.g., 400-800 grit). If using stones, soak a water stone for 10 minutes. Place the blade on the stone at the desired angle. Use a marker on the edge to check your angle. Push the blade across the stone as if slicing a thin layer off the top. Do 10 strokes on one side, then 10 on the other. Repeat until you feel a burr on the opposite side of the edge. A burr is a tiny lip of metal that you can feel with your finger. Once you have a burr along the entire edge, switch to a finer stone (1000-3000 grit) and repeat, but with lighter pressure. Then use a leather strop with polishing compound to remove the burr and polish the edge. In Africa, you can make a strop from a piece of leather glued to a wooden board. Use green or white compound. Strop 20 times per side. Test sharpness by slicing paper or shaving arm hair. If it doesn't cut, repeat. For a convex edge, use a slack belt on your grinder. Always maintain a consistent angle. A simple trick is to use a coin under the spine to set the angle. For field sharpening, carry a small diamond stone. Remember, a sharp knife requires less force and is safer. After sharpening, clean the blade and apply a thin coat of oil. Store your knife in a dry place. With practice, you can get a razor-sharp edge every time.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Belt Grinder Setup, Abrasive Selection and Workshop Safety for African Bladesmiths

Module 6 brings you to the machine that separates hobby smiths from production bladesmiths: the belt grinder. In African workshops from the informal clusters around Kumasi and Nnewi to the growing knife-making hubs in Nairobi, Kampala and Lusaka the belt grinder is often the single largest capital investment after the forge itself. Getting the setup right determines your output rate, your abrasive cost per blade, and whether your edges sell at a premium or a discount. This practical reference consolidates the numbers you need to buy, run and maintain a grinder profitably, and it flags the health hazards that are too often ignored in small workshops where a grinder runs all day in a shared room.

Why Setup Economics Matter More Than Machine Brand

Most African bladesmiths buy a 272 inch (501830 mm) belt grinder or a locally fabricated 248 inch machine. The machine frame lasts a decade; the belts do not. Abrasive cost is therefore the dominant recurring expense, and it is directly controlled by three variables: belt speed, contact wheel hardness, and grinding pressure. Run a ceramic belt too fast on a soft wheel with heavy pressure and you will burn through it in one blade. Match the belt to the steel and the operation, and you can grind four to six blades per belt. That difference is the margin between a profitable workshop and one that merely keeps the lights on.

Belt Speed and Abrasive Selection

Belt speed is usually set by pulley ratio. For carbon and simple alloy steels (1080, 5160, 15N20), 1,4001,800 surface feet per minute (SFM) on a hard platen removes stock fast without overheating. For stainless and high-alloy steels, drop to 9001,200 SFM to avoid work hardening. For handle materials horn, bone, hardwood, G10 500800 SFM with a fresh sharp belt prevents burning and keeps the material cool. Table 1 gives a practical selection matrix.

SAFETY WARNING RESPIRATORY AND EYE HAZARD

Grinding steel, horn, bone and stabilised wood produces fine particulate dust. Metal dust from high-alloy steels can contain chromium, nickel and cobalt, which are associated with respiratory disease and, in the case of hexavalent chromium, with cancer. Bone and horn dust is a known respiratory sensitiser. Never grind without: (1) a P2/P3 or N95 respirator fitted to your face, (2) sealed safety goggles or a full face shield, (3) local exhaust ventilation or a water mist at the contact point, and (4) hearing protection when the grinder runs above 85 dB. Spark streams from carbon steel can ignite sawdust, cloth and oil rags keep a dry powder or CO extinguisher within reach and clear the bench of flammables before every session. In shared workshops, enforce these rules for every operator, not just yourself.

Health basis: WHO (2005), Hazard Prevention and Control in the Work Environment: Airborne Dust; and IARC Monographs on chromium, nickel and cobalt compounds.

Building a Grinder That Fits Your Market

Locally fabricated grinders are common and can be excellent, but three specifications determine whether they will hold tolerance: shaft runout under 0.05 mm, balanced drive and contact wheels, and a rigid tool rest that does not flex under load. A grinder that vibrates will produce wavy bevels no matter how skilled the smith. Budget for a machined contact wheel (rubber or aluminium) rather than a bare steel one; the rubber wheel runs cooler on hollow grinds, while a hard wheel or platen is better for flat and saber grinds. If you are serving a market that pays a premium for hollow-ground kitchen knives increasingly the case in urban Kenya, South Africa and Nigeria a 250 mm rubber contact wheel is worth the extra cost.

Power supply is the other African-specific constraint. Many workshops face voltage fluctuation and intermittent supply. A 1.52.2 kW (23 HP) motor with a variable frequency drive (VFD) gives you speed control and soft starts, but VFDs are sensitive to voltage spikes. Fit a surge protector and, where the grid is unreliable, run the grinder from a solar-charged battery and inverter sized at least 1.5 the motor's continuous rating. Document your actual running hours for one month before buying; many smiths discover they grind only 1520 hours per week and can share a machine with a neighbouring workshop, splitting cost and increasing utilisation.

Maintenance and Belt Storage

Belts absorb moisture. In humid coastal cities Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, Accra store belts in a sealed container with silica gel desiccant. A damp belt will slip, glaze and shed grit. Check drive wheel bearings monthly and re-tension the belt tracking before every session. Keep a logbook: date, belt grit, blades ground, and reason for replacement. After three months you will know your true cost per blade and can price accordingly. This logbook is also the evidence base for a loan application or a supply contract with a retailer who wants consistent monthly output.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Set belt speed by operation: 1,4001,800 SFM for carbon steel hogging; 500800 SFM for handle materials.
  • Match grit to stage: 3660 profile, 80120 bevel, 220400 refine, 600800 pre-polish.
  • Never grind without a fitted P2/N95 respirator, eye protection and hearing protection.
  • Keep a dry powder or CO extinguisher within arm's reach; clear flammable rags and sawdust before every session.
  • Store belts sealed with desiccant in humid climates; discard glazed or damp belts.
  • Check shaft runout and wheel balance quarterly; vibration ruins bevel symmetry.
  • Log every belt: grit, date, blades ground, replacement reason this is your cost data.
  • Protect VFDs with surge protection and size backup power at 1.5 motor rating.
