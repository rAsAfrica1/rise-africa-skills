1. Determining Harvest Maturity: Moisture and Seed Color

Sunflower is ready to harvest when the back of the flower head turns from green to yellow-brown and the seeds are plump with a moisture content of about 20-25%. At this stage, the seeds have reached physiological maturity but are still too wet for safe storage. If you wait too long, shattering and bird damage increase. In Africa, where many farmers grow open-pollinated varieties, checking seed color is key: seeds should be black or striped (depending on variety) and the white fluff at the base of the seed (the 'beard') should be dry and brown. A simple test: rub a few seeds between your fingers; the seed coat should slip off easily, revealing a firm white kernel. Another practical method is to cut the seed with a knife; the inside should be solid, not milky. For accurate moisture measurement, use a moisture meter if available, or the salt test: mix a sample of seeds with dry salt in a sealed jar, shake for 3 minutes, and if the salt sticks to the sides, moisture is above 20%. Harvest when moisture is between 15-20% for combine harvesting, or 12-14% for manual harvesting and immediate storage. In many African countries like Tanzania, Nigeria, and South Africa, sunflower is harvested from May to July in the southern hemisphere and October to December in the northern hemisphere. Delaying harvest after the head turns brown can lead to losses of up to 30% due to shattering. So, monitor your field daily as the heads turn downward. When the back of the head is brown and seeds are black, it's time to harvest.

2. Harvesting Methods: Manual and Mechanical

Manual harvesting is common among smallholder farmers in Africa. It involves cutting the sunflower heads with a sharp knife, sickle, or machete, leaving about 10-15 cm of stem attached. Workers then either thresh the seeds immediately by beating the heads with a stick or rubbing them over a wire mesh, or they dry the heads in the sun for a few days to loosen the seeds. A single person can harvest about 0.2 hectares per day manually. For larger fields, mechanical harvesting with a combine harvester is more efficient. A combine can harvest 4-8 hectares per day, depending on field size and conditions. However, combines require proper settings to minimize seed loss and damage. In Africa, contract harvesting services are available in countries like Zambia and South Africa, where farmers rent combines. When using a combine, ensure the field is level and free of obstacles. The machine cuts the heads, threshes, and separates the seeds in one pass. For manual harvesting, timing is critical: harvest early in the morning when dew is present to reduce shattering. After cutting, dry the heads on a tarpaulin or concrete floor for 3-5 days, turning them daily. Then thresh by beating with a stick or using a mechanical thresher. In some regions, farmers use a 'sunflower thresher' attachment for tractors. For both methods, avoid harvesting when seeds are too dry (below 10% moisture) as they crack easily, or too wet (above 25%) as they clog machinery. Always clean the harvester between fields to prevent mixing varieties. Post-harvest, store seeds in a cool, dry place. Proper harvesting method affects seed quality and yield: manual harvesting can result in 5-10% loss, while mechanical can be 2-5% if well-adjusted.

3. Combine Settings for Sunflower Harvest

Setting a combine for sunflower harvest is different from cereals because sunflower seeds are large, light, and easily damaged. The key adjustments are concave clearance, cylinder speed, fan speed, and sieve settings. Start with a concave clearance of 2-3 cm at the front and 1-2 cm at the rear. This allows the large seeds to pass through without cracking. Cylinder speed should be low, around 250-400 rpm, to thresh gently. Too high a speed will crack or dehull seeds, reducing quality. The fan speed should be set to medium, about 600-800 rpm, to blow away light chaff without blowing out seeds. Sieves: top sieve opening should be about 1.5-2 cm, and bottom sieve about 0.8-1.2 cm. These settings may need adjustment based on seed moisture and variety. If seeds are dry (below 12% moisture), reduce cylinder speed further to 200-300 rpm. If moist (above 18%), increase cylinder speed slightly to 400-500 rpm and reduce forward speed. Ground speed should be slow, 3-5 km/h, to allow the header to pick up heads without shattering. The header should be set to cut about 15-20 cm below the head, and the reel speed should match ground speed. In Africa, many farmers use older combines like the Claas Dominator or Massey Ferguson, which may need more frequent adjustments. Always check for seed loss behind the combine: if you see more than 2-3 seeds per square foot, adjust settings. Also, check the grain tank for cracked seeds; if more than 5% are cracked, reduce cylinder speed. After harvest, clean the combine thoroughly to prevent disease spread. Proper settings can reduce loss to 2-3% and maintain seed quality for oil extraction or planting.

4. Drying and Storage of Sunflower Seeds

After harvesting, sunflower seeds must be dried to a moisture content of 8-10% for safe storage. In Africa, sun drying is the most common method. Spread seeds in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a clean tarpaulin, concrete floor, or woven mat. Stir the seeds every 2-3 hours to ensure even drying. Under full sun, seeds can dry from 20% to 10% moisture in 2-3 days. If the weather is cloudy, drying may take 5-7 days. For large quantities, use a mechanical dryer with a temperature not exceeding 43C to avoid damaging the oil quality. Once dried, seeds should be cool before storage. Store in clean, dry jute bags or polypropylene sacks. Never use bags that previously held fertilizers or chemicals. Place bags on wooden pallets in a well-ventilated, dry room. Stack bags no more than 10 high to prevent crushing. Keep away from walls by 50 cm to allow air circulation. For long-term storage (more than 6 months), keep seeds in airtight containers or silos with moisture-proof liners. Add desiccants like silica gel if available. In humid regions, use hermetic storage bags like Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags, which prevent moisture and insect damage. Check stored seeds monthly for signs of mold, insects, or heating. If temperature rises above 25C, aerate the storage. Sunflower seeds are susceptible to insects like the red flour beetle and Indian meal moth. Use approved insecticides like Actellic dust at 1-2 kg per tonne, or natural methods like neem leaves. Proper drying and storage can maintain seed quality for up to 12 months. Poor storage leads to aflatoxin contamination, which is a health risk. Always test moisture before storage: bite a seed; it should crack, not be soft.

5. Quality Grading and Standards

Quality grading of sunflower seeds is based on moisture content, foreign matter, damaged seeds, and oil content. In Africa, standards vary by country but often follow regional or international norms. For example, the East African Community (EAC) standard for sunflower seeds requires moisture 10%, foreign matter 2%, broken seeds 3%, and oil content 40% for oilseed grade. In Nigeria, NAFDAC and SON set similar standards. Grading is done visually and with simple tools. First, take a representative sample of 1 kg from each lot. Spread it on a clean surface and remove foreign matter like stones, sticks, and other crop seeds. Weigh the foreign matter; if it exceeds 2%, clean the lot. Next, check for damaged seeds: cracked, insect-damaged, or discolored seeds. Count them; if more than 5%, the lot is downgraded. Moisture is measured with a moisture meter; if not available, use the salt test or oven method. Oil content is tested in labs, but farmers can estimate by pressing a small sample. Grades: Grade 1 (premium) has 1% foreign matter, 2% damaged, moisture 9%, oil 45%; Grade 2 (standard) has 2% foreign matter, 5% damaged, moisture 10%, oil 40%; Grade 3 (substandard) has >2% foreign matter, >5% damaged, moisture >10%, oil <40%. For seed production, germination rate must be 85%. In markets, higher grades fetch better prices. For example, in Tanzania, Grade 1 sunflower seeds sell for 20-30% more than Grade 3. To improve grade, clean seeds with a sieve or winnowing, dry properly, and store in clean bags. Always label bags with grade, moisture, and date. Selling ungraded seeds often results in lower prices and rejection by processors. So, invest time in grading to maximize returns.

6. Post-Harvest Loss Prevention

Post-harvest losses in sunflower can be 10-30% if not managed well. Major causes include shattering during harvest, poor drying, insect pests, rodents, and mold. To prevent losses, start at harvest: harvest at the right maturity to avoid shattering. Use tarpaulins under plants when cutting heads to catch fallen seeds. After harvest, dry seeds properly to 8-10% moisture; mold grows above 12% moisture. Store in airtight containers or hermetic bags to prevent insects. For rodent control, keep storage areas clean, use traps, or keep cats. For insects, use approved pesticides or natural repellents like neem. Regularly inspect stored seeds; if you see webbing or live insects, re-dry or fumigate. In Africa, the larger grain borer (Prostephanus truncatus) is a major pest of stored seeds; it can cause up to 40% loss. Use hermetic storage like PICS bags to control it without chemicals. Also, avoid mixing old and new seeds; old seeds may carry pests. For transport, use clean, dry bags and avoid overloading to prevent tearing. During storage, keep bags off the floor on pallets and away from walls. Control humidity by ventilating the store on dry days. If using a warehouse, ensure it is bird-proof and rat-proof. For long-term storage, consider silos with aeration. Another loss is from birds in the field; use bird scarers like reflective tape or noise makers. In some regions, farmers lose seeds to theft; secure your store. Finally, keep records of harvest and storage to identify loss points. By following these practices, you can reduce losses to less than 5%. Remember, every seed saved is money in your pocket. Proper post-harvest handling is as important as good agronomy.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting & Post-Harvest Handling of Sunflower in Africa

Sunflower is one of Africa's fastest-scaling oilseed crops. From Tanzania's central corridor to Zambia's Eastern Province, South Africa's Free State, Ethiopia's Rift Valley, and Nigeria's Middle Belt, smallholder and commercial growers are planting sunflower because it is short-season (90120 days), drought-tolerant, and has a ready industrial buyer base crushers, feed millers, and confectionery processors. But the money is not made in the field alone. It is made or lost in the 30 days after physiological maturity. Poor harvest timing, high moisture, dirty seed, and bad storage routinely destroy 1530% of the crop's value in Africa, according to FAO post-harvest loss estimates. This lesson is a field-ready reference for getting sunflower from plant to buyer with maximum weight, maximum oil content, and minimum rejections.

1. Determining Harvest Maturity: Moisture and Seed Colour

Sunflower does not ripen uniformly, and the single biggest mistake African growers make is harvesting by calendar rather than by crop signal. The correct trigger is a combination of physiological maturity (seed moisture ~35%) and harvest maturity (seed moisture 912%). Between these two points, seed dry matter and oil accumulation are complete but if you harvest too early (above 1415% moisture), you get shrivelled seed, low oil recovery at the crusher, and heavy drying costs. If you wait too long, you get shattering losses, bird damage, and head rot in humid zones.

Field signals to watch:

  • Back of the head: turns from green to lemon-yellow, then to brown. Harvest when the back is yellow-brown, not fully brown.
  • Bracts (the leafy collar behind the head): turn brown and begin to dry.
  • Seed colour: black-hulled varieties turn glossy black; striped varieties show full stripe definition. Milky-white seed = immature.
  • Seed moisture: test with a portable moisture meter (e.g., Kett or Dickey-john). Target 912% for direct delivery, up to 14% if you have on-farm drying.
  • Head droop: heads nod downward as they dry a strong visual cue in open-pollinated varieties.

In practice, most African smallholders harvest at 1215% moisture and sell to a local aggregator who then dries. If you are selling to a formal crusher (e.g., Mount Meru Soyco in Tanzania, Zamanita in Zambia, or Willowton in South Africa), the buyer will reject loads above 10% moisture or apply a moisture discount of 12% per point. Knowing your moisture before you load the truck is worth real money.

2. Harvesting Methods: Manual and Mechanical

Manual harvesting dominates in most African smallholder systems (Tanzania, Uganda, Malawi, Nigeria, Ethiopia). The standard practice is to cut the head with 1520 cm of stalk using a sickle or knife, then either (a) dry heads in the field on tarpaulins, or (b) transport heads to a threshing floor. Manual harvesting costs US$4080 per hectare in labour but allows selective picking of mature heads and reduces shattering. The main risks are threshing losses (510% when beating with sticks) and contamination with soil and stones.

Mechanical harvesting uses a combine harvester fitted with a sunflower header (row-crop or all-crop). In South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, contract combine hire runs US$60120 per hectare. Combine harvesting is faster (24 ha/day) and cleaner, but requires uniform maturity, low weed pressure, and a well-adjusted machine. In Africa, the biggest cause of combine loss is running the cylinder too fast, which cracks seed and shatters heads before the header reaches them.

Business decision: If you farm under 5 ha, manual harvest + custom threshing is usually cheaper. Above 20 ha, contract combine is almost always more economical once you account for labour supervision, weather risk, and seed quality. Between 520 ha, the decision depends on labour availability and local contractor rates.

4. Drying and Storage of Sunflower Seeds

Safe storage moisture for sunflower is 810% for long-term storage and up to 12% for short-term (under 30 days). Above 12%, mould (especially Aspergillus and Penicillium) develops within days, and free fatty acid (FFA) levels rise which directly cuts the price a crusher will pay. In humid African zones (e.g., Uganda, southern Nigeria, Malawi), artificial drying is not optional.

Drying options:

  • Natural sun drying: spread seed 58 cm deep on tarpaulins or concrete, rake every 2 hours. Takes 37 days depending on weather. Cheapest but risky in the rainy season.
  • Solar bubble dryer: US$300800 for a 500 kg unit; popular in Kenya and Tanzania. Reduces drying time to 12 days.
  • Mechanical dryer: US$2,00015,000; used by aggregators and cooperatives. Air temperature must not exceed 43C for seed, 60C for oilseed destined for crushing higher temperatures damage oil quality.

Storage: Use clean, dry jute or polypropylene bags stacked on pallets in a well-ventilated store. Never store on bare earth or against walls. Treat the store for rodents and insects before filling. Hermetic bags (e.g., GrainPro, PICS) are increasingly used in Africa and give 612 months of protection without chemicals, at US$24 per 100 kg bag.

5. Quality Grading and Standards

Sunflower is graded on four main parameters: moisture, foreign matter, oil content, and damaged/broken seed. In East Africa, the East African Community (EAC) standard EAS 762:2019 sets the regional benchmark. In Southern Africa, SANS 551 applies. Buyers almost always apply stricter internal specs.

Typical buyer specs (crushers and aggregators):

  • Moisture: 10% (premium), 12% (acceptable with discount)
  • Foreign matter: 2% (premium), 4% (acceptable)
  • Oil content: 3845% (higher = higher price; variety-dependent)
  • Broken/damaged seed: 5%
  • FFA: 2% (higher FFA = rejected or heavily discounted)
  • Aflatoxin: 10 ppb (EAC/EU export standard)

Business tip: Always take a 500 g sample before loading and test moisture with a calibrated meter. A single rejected 30-tonne truck costs more than a moisture meter. Several cooperatives in Tanzania and Zambia now offer shared moisture testing at collection centres.

SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND DUST

Sunflower seed stored above 12% moisture can develop aflatoxin, a carcinogen that causes liver damage and is strictly regulated in export markets. Never blend mouldy seed with good seed this contaminates the entire lot and can lead to total rejection and loss of buyer relationship.

Sunflower dust and the fine hairs on the seed coat are respiratory irritants. When threshing, cleaning, or bagging, always wear a dust mask (N95 or equivalent), eye protection, and long sleeves. In confined stores, ensure ventilation and never enter a fumigated store without a respirator and a second person outside.

6. Post-Harvest Loss Prevention

Post-harvest losses in African sunflower typically run 1530%, but with disciplined practice they can be held under 8%. The loss points are predictable:

  • Field shattering (25%): harvest at R8, not R9. Avoid harvesting in the heat of midday when heads are dry and brittle.
  • Bird damage (515% in some zones): bird scarers, reflective tape, and early harvesting of border rows.
  • Threshing loss (510% manual): thresh on tarpaulins, not bare ground. Use a motorised thresher where available.
  • Drying and storage (515%): target 9% moisture. Use hermetic bags or well-ventilated stores. Never store on the floor.
  • Transport and handling (13%): use clean, dry bags; avoid overloading trucks; cover loads in rain.

Each percentage point of loss on a 2-tonne crop at US$500/tonne is US$10 of lost revenue. A 15% loss is US$150 often more than the cost of a moisture meter and a set of hermetic bags. Loss prevention is the highest-return investment in sunflower farming.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test seed moisture before harvest target 912% for direct sale, up to 14% if you can dry.
  2. Harvest at R8 (yellow-brown back of head), not R9 (shatter risk).
  3. Cut heads with 2030 cm stalk; dry on tarpaulins, never on bare soil.
  4. Set combine cylinder to 250400 rpm and check grain tank every 100 m.
  5. Dry to 9% moisture before storage; use hermetic bags where available.
  6. Store on pallets in a clean, ventilated, rodent-free room never on the floor.
  7. Take a 500 g sample and test moisture, foreign matter, and FFA before every sale.
  8. Wear dust mask, eye protection, and long sleeves during threshing and bagging.
