Lessons

1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Health Shops, Cafes, Individuals

Start by looking at who already buys herbal products in your area. Walk around your neighbourhood or local market. Note health shops, pharmacies with natural sections, cafes, and juice bars. Also consider individuals: busy mothers, office workers, students, and elderly people. In many African cities, health-conscious middle-class buyers frequent organic shops and farmers' markets. For example, in Nairobi, shops like Healthy U or Zucchini Greengrocers stock herbal teas. In Lagos, try health stores in Lekki or Ikeja. In Accra, check out shops in Osu or East Legon. But don't ignore the mass market: many people buy from open-air markets or street vendors. Talk to 10 potential customers. Ask what they drink now, what health issues they care about (e.g., digestion, immunity, stress), and how much they pay for similar products. For health shops, they want consistent supply, proper labelling, and wholesale prices. Cafes want unique flavours and story behind the tea. Individuals want small packs, clear brewing instructions, and affordable prices. Create a simple customer profile for each group: age, income, where they shop, what they value. Then decide which group to target first. A common mistake is trying to sell to everyone. Better to start with one group, like health shops, and build a relationship. For example, offer a free sample pack to a health shop owner and ask for feedback. Once you have 2-3 shops, you can expand to cafes. Use a notebook to track who you talked to, what they said, and follow up. This groundwork will save you time and money later.

2. Pricing Strategies for Different Markets

Pricing your herbal tea is not just about covering costs. You need to know your cost per unit. For a 100g pack, add up ingredients, packaging (e.g., zip-lock bag, label), labour, and transport. Say your total cost is 200 KES (about $1.50). For individual retail, you might sell at 400 KES â a 100% markup. For health shops, they expect wholesale prices. If they buy 20 packs, sell at 300 KES each, so they can retail at 500 KES. Cafes might buy in bulk for brewing; offer 500g at a discount, e.g., 1200 KES instead of 1500. At local markets, buyers haggle. Set a higher price like 500 KES and be willing to drop to 400 KES. Always calculate your break-even point. If you sell 50 packs at 400 KES, you make 20,000 KES revenue, 10,000 KES profit after costs. But don't underprice. Many new sellers charge too little and go out of business. Research competitors: visit shops and markets, see what similar teas cost. In South Africa, rooibos tea sells for R50 per 100g in health shops. In Nigeria, moringa tea goes for â¦1500 per 100g. Set your price based on value: if your tea is organic, hand-picked, or has a unique blend, you can charge more. Offer bundle deals: buy 3 packs for 1000 KES. For social media, use round numbers. Keep a simple price list for each customer type. Review prices every 3 months. As you grow, you can adjust. Remember, the goal is to make a profit so you can keep producing.

3. Selling at Local Markets and Trade Fairs

Local markets and trade fairs are great for testing your product and getting immediate feedback. Choose markets where your target customers go. For example, in Kampala, go to Nakasero Market; in Dar es Salaam, Kariakoo Market; in Johannesburg, Neighbourgoods Market. For trade fairs, look for health expos, agricultural shows, or women's entrepreneurship events. Book a stall early â costs range from 500 to 5000 KES per day depending on location. Your stall must look attractive. Use a bright tablecloth, arrange teas in neat rows, and have a banner with your brand name and a tagline like 'Natural Wellness in a Cup'. Offer free tastings â small cups of brewed tea. This is your best sales tool. Have a sign: 'Try before you buy'. Price clearly: '100g â 400 KES'. Bring a friend to help. Keep a notebook to record sales and customer contacts. At markets, people want to touch and smell. Put samples in open jars. Tell the story: where herbs come from, how they help. For example, 'This hibiscus tea lowers blood pressure.' Be ready to answer questions. Offer a small discount for buying 2 packs. Collect phone numbers for WhatsApp follow-up. After the event, send a thank-you message and offer delivery. Track what sells best â maybe ginger-lemon outsells mint. Use that info to adjust your next market. Also, network with other vendors; they might become stockists. Don't just sit; stand and engage. Smile and greet everyone. Even if you don't sell much, you gain experience and contacts. Aim to break even on stall fees the first time. With practice, you can make a profit.

4. Using Social Media and WhatsApp to Promote Your Teas

Social media is free and powerful. Start with WhatsApp Business â it's widely used across Africa. Set up a catalogue with photos, prices, and descriptions. Use a clear profile photo of your tea. Post status updates 2-3 times a day: show brewing, packing, or customer testimonials. For example, 'New batch of lemongrass tea ready! 100g for 400 KES. DM to order.' Create a broadcast list of customers and send weekly specials. On Facebook, join local buy-and-sell groups (e.g., 'Nairobi Buy Sell', 'Lagos Marketplace'). Post your teas with a call to action: 'Order now, delivery in 24 hours.' Use Instagram if you have a smartphone â take bright photos of your tea in a nice cup. Add hashtags like #HerbalTea #NaturalRemedies #KenyaTea. For TikTok, make short videos: 15 seconds showing how to brew, or benefits of ginger tea. Post consistently. Engage with comments. Respond to messages within an hour. Use WhatsApp to offer a free sample to influencers â local health bloggers or fitness coaches. Ask them to review. Run a simple promotion: 'Buy 2 packs, get 1 free' for first-time customers. Use Google Forms to collect orders. For payment, use mobile money like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN MoMo. Send a confirmation message. For delivery, use boda boda or local courier. Track which posts get the most likes and orders. Double down on what works. Don't ignore offline: put your WhatsApp number on business cards and flyers. With consistent effort, social media can bring 10-20 orders per week.

5. Building a Customer Base through Free Tastings

Free tastings are the fastest way to turn strangers into buyers. People trust what they taste. Organise tastings at strategic locations: outside a health shop, at a busy market entrance, or during a community event. Get permission first. Set up a small table with a thermos of hot water, small disposable cups, and your tea. Brew a batch â for example, 2 litres of hibiscus tea. Offer a cup to passersby with a smile: 'Would you like to try our natural hibiscus tea? It's good for blood pressure.' Have a sign: 'Free Tasting Today'. Keep it simple. Don't force; let them sip. Then ask: 'How does it taste?' If they like it, say: 'You can buy a 100g pack for 400 KES. It makes 40 cups.' Many will buy on the spot. Collect their phone number for follow-up. Offer a small discount if they buy now: 'Today only, 350 KES.' At the end of the day, count how many cups you gave and how many packs you sold. Aim for at least 20 tastings and 5 sales. Repeat weekly at different locations. Over a month, you can build a base of 50-100 loyal customers. Also, give free samples to shop owners and cafe managers. They can taste and then order. For example, give a 50g sample to a health shop owner. Follow up after 3 days: 'Did you enjoy the tea? Would you like to stock it?' Tastings also give instant feedback: if people don't like a flavour, you can adjust. Keep a record of who bought and what they liked. Send them a WhatsApp message a week later: 'Hope you enjoyed the tea. Here's a new blend you might like.' This personal touch builds loyalty.

6. Partnering with Local Health Practitioners and Stores

Partnering with health practitioners and stores can give you steady sales and credibility. Start with herbalists, naturopaths, nutritionists, and traditional healers. In many African communities, people trust these practitioners. Visit their offices or clinics. Bring a sample of your tea and a one-page info sheet: ingredients, benefits, and price. Explain that you'd like them to recommend your tea to clients. Offer a commission: for every pack sold, they get 20%. For example, if you sell at 400 KES, they earn 80 KES. This motivates them. Also, partner with health shops. Approach the owner with a wholesale offer: 'I'll supply you 20 packs at 300 KES each. You can sell at 500 KES.' Provide attractive display stands or shelf talkers. Offer to do a free tasting event at their shop to drive traffic. For cafes, propose a 'tea of the week' feature. You supply the tea, they brew and sell by the cup. Split profits 50/50. For example, a cup sells for 150 KES; you get 75 KES. This introduces your tea to new customers. Always have a simple written agreement: quantity, price, payment terms (e.g., cash on delivery), and duration. Follow up regularly. Ask for feedback. If a partner sells well, give them a bonus or exclusive flavour. Build long-term relationships. For example, a Nairobi nutritionist who recommends your moringa tea to 10 clients per week can generate 40 packs per month. That's 12,000 KES in revenue. Treat partners well â deliver on time, keep quality consistent. Word-of-mouth from trusted practitioners is more powerful than any advertisement.

Practical Reference: Marketing and Selling Herbal Teas Locally

This practical reference supports the module Marketing and Selling Herbal Teas Locally in the Rise AFRICA Herbal Teas & Infusions course. It is written for African micro and small enterprises producing herbal teas from local plants such as hibiscus (roselle), moringa, lemongrass, ginger, baobab, and mint. The focus is on practical, low-cost actions that generate sales quickly while building a loyal customer base.

Across Africa, the market for herbal teas is expanding. Urbanisation, rising health awareness, and demand for natural products are creating opportunities in health shops, cafÃ©s, supermarkets, pharmacies, and online channels. However, most small producers fail not because their tea is poor, but because they do not identify who will buy it, how to price it, or where to sell it repeatedly. This reference gives you a business-first framework to fix that.

1. Identifying Your Target Customers

Your target customer is not "everyone who drinks tea." It is a specific group you can reach with a specific message. In most African cities, four customer segments are most accessible to small herbal tea businesses:

  • Health shops and pharmacies: Buy in small wholesale quantities, want consistent quality, labels with health claims, and batch consistency. They often test with one or two units before reordering.
  • CafÃ©s, restaurants, and hotels: Want a story, attractive packaging, and reliable supply. They may use your tea as a signature drink and charge a premium.
  • Individual consumers: Buy for personal health, gifting, or family use. They respond to tastings, WhatsApp messages, and word of mouth.
  • Corporate and institutional buyers: Offices, wellness programmes, NGOs, and schools buy in bulk for staff or events. They need invoices and consistent volumes.

To identify which segment fits your capacity, ask three questions: How much can I produce per month? What packaging can I afford? Can I deliver reliably? A producer making 50 packs a month should focus on individuals and one or two health shops, not a supermarket chain.

3. Selling at Local Markets and Trade Fairs

Local markets remain the fastest route to first sales in most African towns. At a market or trade fair, your goal is not just to sell today's stock, but to collect contacts and build repeat customers.

Practical setup for a market stall:

  • Use a clean table with a bright cloth and clear signage in the local language.
  • Display teas in transparent jars so buyers can see the product.
  • Offer a free hot cup of one tea. Do not offer all varieties free â it reduces perceived value.
  • Keep a notebook or phone to record names and WhatsApp numbers of interested buyers.
  • Price clearly. Ambiguous pricing loses sales.

Trade fairs (agricultural shows, SME expos, food fairs) attract buyers from health shops, hotels, and supermarkets. Prepare a one-page product sheet with your contact details, production capacity, and price list. Follow up within 48 hours â most small producers never follow up, which is why they lose contracts.

4. Using Social Media and WhatsApp to Promote Your Teas

In Africa, WhatsApp is the most important business communication tool. Facebook and Instagram are useful for discovery, but WhatsApp converts. Use them together:

  • WhatsApp Business: Set up a catalogue with photos, prices, and descriptions. Use broadcast lists (not groups) to send weekly updates to customers who opt in.
  • Facebook: Post 3â4 times a week: product photos, health tips, customer testimonials, and behind-the-scenes production clips.
  • Instagram: Use hashtags like #HerbalTeaAfrica, #MadeInKenya, #NaturalRemedies.
  • TikTok: Short videos of tea preparation and health benefits reach younger buyers.

Content that sells: short videos of you preparing the tea, customer reviews, and educational posts about the health benefits of local herbs. Avoid making medical claims â see the safety warning below.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Health Claims and Food Safety

In most African countries, herbal teas are regulated as food, not medicine. You must not claim that your tea cures, treats, or prevents diseases such as diabetes, cancer, malaria, or hypertension unless your product is registered as a medicine with the national regulator (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, KEBS and the Pharmacy and Poisons Board in Kenya, FDA Ghana, SAHPRA in South Africa). Unproven medical claims can lead to fines, product seizure, or closure. Always:

  • Use safe wording such as "traditionally used to supportâ¦" or "a refreshing herbal infusion."
  • Follow national food labelling and hygiene standards.
  • Warn customers with allergies, pregnancy, or on medication to consult a health practitioner.
  • Keep production premises clean and train staff in basic food hygiene (HACCP principles).

6. Partnering with Local Health Practitioners and Stores

Partnerships multiply your reach without multiplying your costs. Target these partners:

  • Herbalists and traditional health practitioners: They already have trust and a customer base. Offer them wholesale prices or a commission on sales.
  • Nutritionists and dieticians: They can recommend your tea to clients. Provide free samples and a one-page product sheet.
  • Health shops and pharmacies: Offer a consignment arrangement for the first order â they pay you after selling. This lowers their risk and gets your product on shelves.
  • Yoga studios, gyms, and wellness centres: Sell your tea as a post-workout or relaxation drink.
  • Gift shops and hotels: Package your tea as a gift set or welcome amenity.

Always put partnership agreements in writing, even if simple. Include price, payment terms, delivery schedule, and minimum order quantity. This protects both sides and builds professionalism.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. I know my cost per pack and have set a price that covers cost plus margin.
  2. I have chosen one or two target segments (not "everyone") and tailored my packaging and message to them.
  3. My product labels comply with national food labelling rules and make no illegal medical claims.
  4. I have a WhatsApp Business catalogue with clear photos and prices.
  5. I run at least one free tasting event per month and collect customer contacts.
  6. I follow up with every new contact within 48 hours.
  7. I have at least two retail or practitioner partners stocking my tea.
  8. I keep records of sales, costs, and customer feedback to improve each month.
