1. Understanding Basic Contract Terms

A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties. In Spanish, it's called a contrato. When you scale your business, you'll sign contracts with suppliers, clients, landlords, and employees. Knowing key terms helps you avoid costly mistakes.

First, parties: las partes. The offeror is el oferente, and the offeree is el aceptante. Consideration (something of value exchanged) is la contraprestacin. For example, if you agree to buy 100 kg of cocoa from a farmer in Ghana for 500 cedis, that's a contract.

Key clauses:
- Objeto del contrato: purpose of the contract.
- Plazo: term or duration.
- Precio: price.
- Forma de pago: payment method.
- Clusula de rescisin: termination clause.
- Penalizacin por incumplimiento: penalty for breach.

In many African countries, oral contracts are valid, but written contracts are safer. For cross-border deals, specify jurisdiction (jurisdiccin) and arbitration (arbitraje). For instance, 'Las partes acuerdan resolver disputas mediante arbitraje en la Cmara de Comercio de Nairobi.'

Always check for a 'clusula de fuerza mayor' (force majeure) to protect against unforeseen events like floods or strikes. Also, ensure both parties sign and date (firma y fecha) the document. Keep copies. If you don't understand a term, ask a lawyer or use a translation tool like DeepL.

Practice: Write a simple contract in Spanish for a service you offer. Use at least five terms from above. This builds your vocabulary and confidence.

2. Discussing Terms and Conditions

Terms and conditions (trminos y condiciones) are the rules of your contract. When negotiating, you'll discuss these in Spanish. Start with 'Quisiera negociar los trminos' (I would like to negotiate the terms).

Common phrases:
- 'Est de acuerdo con estas condiciones?' (Do you agree with these conditions?)
- 'Propongo un pago del 50% por adelantado.' (I propose a 50% advance payment.)
- 'Podemos incluir una clusula de confidencialidad?' (Can we include a confidentiality clause?)
- 'El plazo de entrega ser de 30 das.' (The delivery term will be 30 days.)
- 'Si hay retraso, se aplicar una multa del 2% por semana.' (If there is a delay, a 2% penalty per week will apply.)

In Africa, business relationships often rely on trust, but clear terms prevent disputes. For example, a Kenyan supplier might agree to deliver goods in 45 days, but if you don't specify, delays can hurt your cash flow. Always clarify: 'Qu sucede si no se cumple el plazo?' (What happens if the deadline is not met?)

Use tools like Google Translate for quick checks, but for legal documents, hire a translator. In Spanish-speaking countries like Equatorial Guinea, contracts are in Spanish, so knowing these phrases is essential.

Practice role-play: One person is the buyer, the other the seller. Negotiate payment terms, delivery, and penalties. Record yourself and listen for accuracy. This will prepare you for real negotiations.

3. Registering a Business: Vocabulary and Phrases

To scale legally, you must register your business. In Spanish, registration is 'registro de empresa' or 'constitucin de sociedad'. Common business structures:
- Empresa unipersonal (sole proprietorship)
- Sociedad de responsabilidad limitada (S.R.L. or LLC)
- Sociedad annima (S.A. or corporation)

Steps to register in a Spanish-speaking country (e.g., Equatorial Guinea or Spain):
1. Reserva de nombre (name reservation) at the commercial registry.
2. Escritura pblica (public deed) before a notary.
3. Nmero de Identificacin Fiscal (NIF) or tax ID.
4. Registro Mercantil (Commercial Registry).
5. Licencia de apertura (opening license) from the municipality.

Key phrases:
- 'Quiero registrar una sociedad de responsabilidad limitada.' (I want to register a limited liability company.)
- 'Cules son los requisitos?' (What are the requirements?)
- 'Necesito el formulario de solicitud.' (I need the application form.)
- 'Cunto cuesta la inscripcin?' (How much does registration cost?)

In many African countries, you can register online. For example, in Nigeria, the CAC (Corporate Affairs Commission) allows online registration. In Spanish-speaking countries, the process may take 1-2 weeks and cost between $200 and $1000.

After registration, you'll get a Tax ID (NIF) and must file annual returns. Keep your registration documents safe. If you expand to another country, you may need to register a branch (sucursal).

Practice: Look up the registration process for a Spanish-speaking country you're interested in. Write a short paragraph in Spanish describing the steps.

4. Taxes and Licenses in Spanish-Speaking Countries

Taxes (impuestos) and licenses (licencias) are mandatory for scaling. In Spanish-speaking countries, common taxes include:
- IVA (Impuesto al Valor Agregado): VAT, typically 15-21%.
- Impuesto sobre la Renta (ISR): income tax, 25-35%.
- Impuesto de Sociedades: corporate tax.

You must also get licenses: licencia de funcionamiento (operating license), licencia sanitaria (health license) for food businesses, and licencia ambiental (environmental license).

Key phrases:
- 'Dnde puedo pagar los impuestos?' (Where can I pay taxes?)
- 'Necesito una licencia de funcionamiento.' (I need an operating license.)
- 'Cul es la fecha lmite para declarar impuestos?' (What is the deadline to file taxes?)
- 'Hay exenciones para pequeas empresas?' (Are there exemptions for small businesses?)

In Equatorial Guinea, the tax authority is 'Direccin General de Impuestos'. In Spain, it's 'Agencia Tributaria'. In many African countries, tax rates are lower for small businesses. For example, in Kenya, small businesses with turnover below 5 million KES pay a presumptive tax.

Always keep receipts (recibos) and invoices (facturas). Use accounting software like QuickBooks or Wave. If you're late, you may face multas (fines) and interest (intereses).

Consider hiring an accountant (contador) to handle taxes. They cost around $100-$300 per month but save you from penalties.

Practice: List three taxes your business would pay in a Spanish-speaking country. Write a sentence in Spanish about each.

5. Hiring Employees and Writing Job Descriptions

Hiring (contratacin) is key to scaling. In Spanish, an employee is 'empleado' and a job description is 'descripcin del puesto'.

A job description should include:
- Ttulo del puesto (job title)
- Funciones (duties)
- Requisitos (requirements)
- Salario (salary)
- Horario (schedule)
- Ubicacin (location)

Example: 'Buscamos un vendedor con experiencia en ventas de productos agrcolas. Debe hablar espaol e ingls. Salario: 300.000 CFA por mes.'

When interviewing, ask:
- 'Cul es su experiencia previa?' (What is your previous experience?)
- 'Por qu quiere trabajar aqu?' (Why do you want to work here?)
- 'Puede trabajar los fines de semana?' (Can you work weekends?)

In many African countries, labor laws require written contracts (contrato de trabajo), minimum wage (salario mnimo), and social security (seguridad social). For example, in Senegal, the minimum wage is about 333 CFA per hour. In Equatorial Guinea, it's 150,000 CFA per month.

You must also register employees for social security and pay contributions. Failure can lead to fines.

Use platforms like LinkedIn or local job boards to post openings. For Spanish-speaking roles, specify 'Se requiere espaol fluido'.

Practice: Write a job description in Spanish for a position in your business. Include at least five details.

6. Practice: Review a Simple Contract in Spanish

Now, let's review a simple contract in Spanish. Below is a short service contract. Read it and answer the questions.

Contrato de Servicios

Entre: Empresa ABC (el Cliente) y Juan Prez (el Proveedor)

1. Objeto: El Proveedor se compromete a disear un logotipo para el Cliente.
2. Precio: El Cliente pagar 200.000 CFA.
3. Plazo: El diseo se entregar en 15 das.
4. Pago: 50% por adelantado, 50% al finalizar.
5. Confidencialidad: El Proveedor no divulgar informacin del Cliente.
6. Rescisin: Cualquiera de las partes puede rescindir con 7 das de aviso.
7. Ley aplicable: Las leyes de Senegal.

Firmas:
_________________ (Cliente)
_________________ (Proveedor)
Fecha: 1 de enero de 2025

Questions:
1. Cul es el objeto del contrato? (What is the purpose?)
2. Cunto es el precio total? (What is the total price?)
3. Cul es el plazo de entrega? (What is the delivery term?)
4. Cmo se paga? (How is payment made?)
5. Qu pasa si hay incumplimiento? (What happens if there is a breach?)

Answers: 1. Disear un logotipo. 2. 200.000 CFA. 3. 15 das. 4. 50% adelantado, 50% al final. 5. Se puede rescindir con 7 das de aviso.

Now, practice with a contract from your own business. Identify key terms and translate them. Use a dictionary if needed. This exercise builds your confidence to handle real contracts in Spanish.

It is a commercial survival skill. This Practical Reference module consolidates the six lessons in this unit into one operational toolkit: contract vocabulary, terms-and-conditions negotiation phrases, business registration language, tax and licensing terminology, hiring and job-description wording, and a structured method for reviewing a simple contract in Spanish.</p>

<p>The approach is business-first. Every term below is framed around what it protects, what it costs, and what it commits your enterprise to. Where a phrase is legally loaded meaning it can shift liability, delay payment, or trigger penalties it is flagged.</p>

<h3>Lesson 1 Understanding Basic Contract Terms</h3>
<p>A Spanish-language contract for agricultural goods typically opens with <em>las partes</em> (the parties), <em>el objeto</em> (the subject matter), and <em>la contraprestacin</em> (the consideration). The most dangerous words for an African exporter are often the smallest: <em>solidaria</em> means joint and several liability, so if your buyer defaults, the guarantor can be pursued for the full amount. <em>Irrevocable</em> means you cannot withdraw. <em>Salvo buen fin</em> means "subject to final collection" a clause that lets a buyer's bank reverse a credit if the underlying documents fail.</p>

<p>Key contract vocabulary you will meet repeatedly: <em>el contrato de compraventa</em> (sales contract), <em>el proveedor</em> (supplier), <em>el comprador</em> (buyer), <em>la clusula</em> (clause), <em>el anexo</em> (annex), <em>la vigencia</em> (term/validity), <em>la penalidad</em> (penalty), and <em>la rescisin</em> (termination). In agribusiness, <em>la merma</em> (shrinkage/wastage) and <em>el calibre</em> (size grade) are commercially decisive a one-point change in <em>calibre</em> can swing the price per tonne by 812%.</p>

<h3>Lesson 2 Discussing Terms and Conditions</h3>
<p>Negotiation phrases that actually move a deal: <em>Quisiramos proponer una modificacin a la clusula de pago</em> (We would like to propose an amendment to the payment clause). <em>Podran ampliar el plazo de pago a 60 das?</em> (Could you extend the payment term to 60 days?). <em>Proponemos que la jurisdiccin sea la Cmara de Comercio de Lima</em> (We propose jurisdiction be the Lima Chamber of Commerce).</p>

<h3>Lesson 3 Registering a Business: Vocabulary and Phrases</h3>
<p>Registration terminology varies by country, but the core vocabulary is stable. In Mexico you register before the <em>Registro Pblico de Comercio</em> and obtain an <em>RFC</em>. In Colombia it is the <em>Cmara de Comercio</em> and <em>RUT</em>. In Peru it is <em>SUNARP</em> and <em>RUC</em>. In Spain it is the <em>Registro Mercantil</em> and <em>NIF/CIF</em>. Common phrases: <em>Constituir una sociedad</em> (to incorporate a company), <em>Escritura pblica</em> (notarised deed), <em>Objeto social</em> (corporate purpose keep it broad enough to cover future agri-processing), <em>Capital social</em> (share capital), and <em>Representante legal</em> (legal representative).</p>

<p>For an African agribusiness, the practical question is usually whether to register a local subsidiary or work through a local agent. A subsidiary gives you <em>personera jurdica</em> (legal personality) and access to local credit, but exposes you to local corporate tax. An agent is faster and cheaper but leaves you without standing to enforce contracts in your own name.</p>

<h3>Lesson 4 Taxes and Licenses in Spanish-Speaking Countries</h3>
<p>Tax acronyms differ across the region: <em>IVA</em> (Impuesto al Valor Agregado) in most countries, <em>IGV</em> in Peru, <em>ITBMS</em> in Panama. Corporate income tax is <em>Impuesto sobre la Renta</em> (ISR) or <em>Impuesto a la Renta</em>. Exporters frequently qualify for <em>devolucin de IVA</em> (VAT refund) or <em>drawback</em>, but only if the paperwork is exact. Licences you will encounter include <em>licencia de funcionamiento</em> (operating licence), <em>permiso sanitario</em> (health permit, critical for food exports), and <em>certificado fitosanitario</em> (phytosanitary certificate, issued by the national plant-protection authority).</p>

<h3>Lesson 5 Hiring Employees and Writing Job Descriptions</h3>
<p>Hiring in Spanish-speaking markets requires familiarity with <em>el contrato de trabajo</em> (employment contract), <em>el periodo de prueba</em> (probation period usually 13 months), <em>la jornada laboral</em> (working hours), <em>el salario mnimo</em> (minimum wage), and <em>las prestaciones sociales</em> (mandatory benefits: health, pension, severance). In many countries, <em>aguinaldo</em> (Christmas bonus) and <em>prima vacacional</em> (vacation premium) are legally required and must be budgeted before you sign.</p>

<p>A job description in Spanish should include: <em>ttulo del puesto</em> (job title), <em>objetivo del puesto</em> (role objective), <em>responsabilidades</em> (responsibilities), <em>requisitos</em> (requirements), <em>competencias</em> (skills), and <em>condiciones</em> (conditions). For agribusiness roles such as <em>tcnico de campo</em> (field technician), <em>supervisor de planta</em> (plant supervisor), or <em>gerente de exportaciones</em> (export manager), specify the crop, the region, and the reporting line vague descriptions invite disputes.</p>

<h3>Lesson 6 Practice: Review a Simple Contract in Spanish</h3>
<p>Take any short Spanish sales contract and run it through the five-stage workflow in the diagram above. For each stage, write one sentence in Spanish summarising what the contract says, and one sentence in English stating whether you accept it. This dual-language discipline forces precision and creates a record you can share with your lawyer.</p>

<p>Example: <em>"El vendedor entregar 20 toneladas de caf verde, calibre 15, en el puerto de Buenaventura dentro de 30 das."</em> Your review note: "Delivery: 20 t green coffee, grade 15, Buenaventura port, 30 days. Accept but confirm who bears port storage if the vessel is delayed."</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Identify all parties</strong> verify <em>razn social</em> and tax ID (<em>NIT/RUC/RFC</em>) are active.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm the object</strong> product, grade (<em>calibre</em>), quantity, and Incoterm are unambiguous.</li>
<li><strong>Check payment terms</strong> <em>anticipo</em>, <em>plazo de pago</em>, and <em>inters moratorio</em> are stated numerically.</li>
<li><strong>Review liability</strong> watch for <em>solidaria</em>, uncapped <em>penalidad</em>, and <em>salvo buen fin</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Agree jurisdiction</strong> <em>arbitraje</em> seat, language, and <em>ley aplicable</em> are specified.</li>
<li><strong>Verify licences</strong> <em>permiso sanitario</em> and <em>certificado fitosanitario</em> are valid and current.</li>
<li><strong>Budget employment costs</strong> <em>prestaciones sociales</em>, <em>aguinaldo</em>, and <em>prima vacacional</em> included.</li>
<li><strong>Archive everything</strong> signed copy, translations, and correspondence stored digitally for five years.</li>
</ol>
