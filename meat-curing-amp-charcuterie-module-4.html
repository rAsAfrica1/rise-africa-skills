1. Biltong Basics: Spices, Vinegar, and Air-Drying

Biltong is a traditional African cured meat made from beef, game, or ostrich. The key ingredients are coarse salt, brown sugar, coriander seeds, black pepper, and vinegar. For 1 kg of meat, use 20 g coarse salt, 10 g brown sugar, 10 g toasted coriander seeds (crushed), 5 g black pepper, and 100 ml brown vinegar (or apple cider vinegar). The vinegar creates an acidic environment that inhibits harmful bacteria while allowing beneficial enzymes to tenderise the meat. Salt draws out moisture and further protects against spoilage.

You will need a sharp knife, a mixing bowl, a drying rack (or hooks), and a well-ventilated space. Cut the meat into strips about 2 cm thick, 5 cm wide, and as long as you like. Trim off excess fat and sinew. Mix the dry spices together. Dip each strip into vinegar, then rub generously with the spice mix. Layer the spiced strips in a bowl, cover, and refrigerate for 12 to 24 hours. This curing step flavours the meat and starts the preservation process.

After curing, hang the strips to dry. In a dry, breezy climate, you can hang them outdoors under shade cloth or in a dedicated drying box. Air must circulate around each piece. Depending on thickness and humidity, drying takes 3 to 5 days. The biltong is ready when it is firm and dry on the outside but still slightly moist inside. If you prefer it drier, leave it another day. In humid climates, use a fan or a light bulb in a drying box to keep the air moving and prevent mould.

Store finished biltong in a paper bag or breathable container in a cool, dry place. It keeps for weeks. Never store in plastic, as trapped moisture causes mould. With practice, you can adjust spices to your tastesome add chilli flakes, garlic powder, or a pinch of bicarbonate of soda to soften the meat. Biltong is high in protein and perfect for snacks, hiking, or selling at local markets.

2. Making Beef Biltong: Step-by-Step from Raw to Dry

Start with the right cut. Beef silverside, topside, or rump works best. Choose meat that is fresh, lean, and at least 2 kg. Ask your butcher to cut it into long strips with the grain, about 2 cm thick. Fat should be trimmed but not entirely removeda little fat adds flavour. You will also need coarse salt, brown sugar, coriander seeds, black pepper, vinegar, and optional Worcestershire sauce. For 2 kg beef: 40 g salt, 20 g sugar, 20 g coriander, 10 g pepper, 200 ml vinegar.

Step 1: Toast coriander seeds in a dry pan until fragrant, then crush coarsely. Mix with salt, sugar, and pepper. Step 2: Pour vinegar into a shallow dish. Dip each meat strip into vinegar, ensuring full coverage. Step 3: Rub the spice mix into the meat on all sides. Place strips in a glass or plastic container, cover, and refrigerate for 24 hours. Turn the meat once after 12 hours. Step 4: Remove from fridge and pat off excess liquid with paper towel. Do not rinse.

Step 5: Hang the strips. Use stainless steel hooks or paper clips (sanitised) to hang meat in a drying box or a cool, dry room with a fan. Space them at least 5 cm apart. Ideal temperature is 1520C with 5060% humidity. If humidity is higher, use a fan or dehumidifier. Step 6: Dry for 3 to 5 days. Check daily. The biltong is done when the outside is hard and dark, but the inside is still slightly pink and moist. For wet biltong (more moist), dry 3 days; for dry, 5 days. Step 7: Cut a piece to test. It should be firm but not rock-hard. If mould appears (green or black spots), discard affected pieces and increase airflow.

Once dry, slice thinly against the grain. Store in paper bags or cotton sacks in a cool, dark place. It keeps for 24 weeks. For longer storage, vacuum-seal and freeze. This method works for beef, game, or ostrich. With practice, you can scale up for a small business selling biltong at markets or to local shops.

3. Drowors: Spiced Dry Sausage from South Africa

Drowors is a traditional South African dry sausage, similar to biltong but made with minced meat and spices stuffed into casings. It is a favourite snack and keeps well without refrigeration. The basic recipe for 2 kg of beef (or game) mince: 40 g coarse salt, 20 g brown sugar, 20 g toasted coriander seeds (crushed), 10 g black pepper, 5 g ground cloves, 5 g ground nutmeg, 100 ml brown vinegar, and 10 g baking soda (optional, for tenderness). You will also need natural sheep or hog casings, a sausage stuffer (or a funnel), and a drying rack.

Start by mixing the mince with all dry spices and vinegar. Knead well for 5 minutes until the mixture is sticky. Cover and refrigerate for 2 hours to let flavours blend. Soak casings in warm water for 30 minutes to soften. Rinse inside and out. Stuff the meat mixture into casings, making links about 30 cm long. Do not overstuff, or casings will burst. Tie ends with string. Prick small holes along the sausage with a sterilised needle to release air.

Hang the sausages in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place. Ideal conditions: 1520C, 5060% humidity, with a gentle breeze. If humid, use a fan or a drying box. Dry for 4 to 7 days. The drowors is ready when it is firm and dry but still slightly pliable. It should not be brittle. Cut a link to testinside should be dark red and dry. If mould appears, wipe with vinegar and increase airflow. In very dry climates, 4 days may be enough; in humid areas, up to 10 days.

Once dry, store in paper bags or hang in a cool pantry. It keeps for 34 weeks. For longer shelf life, vacuum-seal and freeze. Drowors can be made from beef, kudu, springbok, or ostrich. Adjust spices to tastesome add chilli or garlic. This is a popular product for informal traders because it is lightweight, nutritious, and has a long shelf life. Always maintain hygiene: clean hands, tools, and surfaces to prevent contamination.

4. Using Game Meats: Kudu, Springbok, and Impala

Game meats like kudu, springbok, and impala make excellent biltong and drowors. They are leaner than beef, with a distinct flavour. However, they require careful handling because game animals may carry bacteria. Only use meat from healthy animals, and process it quickly after hunting. If you buy game meat, ensure it comes from a licensed butcher. For 1 kg of game meat, use the same spice mix as beef: 20 g salt, 10 g sugar, 10 g coriander, 5 g pepper, 100 ml vinegar. Game meat benefits from a slightly longer curing timeup to 36 hoursto tenderise.

Kudu is a large antelope with dense, dark meat. It is best for biltong because its low fat content dries well. Cut strips 2 cm thick. Springbok is smaller and more tender; its meat is mild and slightly sweet. It makes excellent drowors. Impala is similar to springbok but slightly larger. For all game, trim off all fat and sinewgame fat turns rancid quickly. Use vinegar generously to kill surface bacteria. Some hunters add a tablespoon of lemon juice per kg for extra acidity.

Drying game meat takes 3 to 5 days, similar to beef. Because game is lean, it dries faster and can become too hard. Check daily. If the outside is hard but inside still moist, it is perfect. If it becomes brittle, reduce drying time next batch. In humid climates, use a drying box with a fan. Game biltong has a stronger, earthier flavour than beef. It is prized in South Africa and Namibia. You can sell it at a premium price.

For drowors, mix game mince with beef fat (about 20% fat) to prevent dryness. Otherwise, the sausage will be crumbly. Use the same spices as beef drowors. Stuff into casings and dry for 4 to 7 days. Game drowors is a delicacy. Always label your product clearly if selling, and follow local food safety regulations. With practice, you can create a niche market for game biltong and drowors, especially among tourists and health-conscious customers.

5. Building a Drying Box for Humid Climates

In humid climates, air-drying biltong and drowors is risky because mould grows quickly. A drying box solves this by controlling airflow, temperature, and humidity. You can build a simple box using plywood, plastic sheeting, or a large cardboard box. The box should be at least 60 cm wide, 60 cm deep, and 90 cm tall. You will need a small fan (computer fan or 12V fan), a light bulb (40W) for heat, a hygrometer (to measure humidity), and a thermostat (optional). Total cost: about R500R800 ($30$50).

Cut a hole at the bottom for the fan to blow air in, and another hole at the top for air to escape. Cover holes with mesh to keep insects out. Install the fan to circulate air upward. Place the light bulb near the bottom to provide gentle warmthaim for 2530C. Too much heat cooks the meat; too little slows drying. Use a hygrometer to monitor humidity. Ideal humidity is 5060%. If higher, add a small dehumidifier or increase airflow. If lower, place a bowl of water inside. Hang meat from a wooden dowel or stainless steel rod using hooks. Space pieces 5 cm apart.

Run the box in a dry, shaded area. Plug in the fan and light. Check temperature and humidity twice daily. Turn meat occasionally. Drying time in a box is 2 to 4 days, faster than outdoor drying. The box protects against rain, insects, and dust. It also allows you to dry year-round, even in rainy seasons. Clean the box after each batch with vinegar and water to prevent mould spores. Replace the light bulb if it burns out.

For a larger operation, you can build a walk-in drying room with a dehumidifier and exhaust fan. But for home or small business, a box is enough. Always test your first batch: if meat dries unevenly, adjust airflow. If mould appears, increase fan speed and reduce humidity. With a drying box, you can produce consistent biltong and drowors regardless of weather. This is essential for selling to customers who expect quality.

6. Packaging and Storing Biltong for Long Shelf Life

Proper packaging and storage are critical for biltong and drowors. Even fully dried meat can spoil if exposed to moisture, air, or pests. The goal is to keep it dry, cool, and dark. For short-term storage (up to 4 weeks), use paper bags, cotton sacks, or breathable containers. Never use plastic bags for storagetrapped moisture causes mould. If you must use plastic, ensure the biltong is bone-dry and add a desiccant packet. For longer storage (36 months), vacuum-seal portions and keep them in a cool, dark place or freezer. Vacuum sealing removes air and prevents mould.

For selling, packaging must be attractive and legal. Use food-grade paper bags with a clear window, or vacuum-sealed pouches. Label with product name, ingredients, weight, production date, and expiry date. In South Africa, you need a certificate of acceptability (COA) from your local municipality to sell processed meat. Check your country's regulations. For informal markets, simple paper bags with a sticker are often acceptable. Always wear gloves when handling finished biltong to avoid contamination.

Store biltong in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. Ideal temperature is below 20C. If you live in a hot, humid area, keep it in the fridge or freezer. Biltong can be frozen for up to 6 months without losing quality. Thaw in the fridge, not at room temperature. Drowors can be hung in a cool pantry if humidity is low. If it becomes too dry, it will harden; if too moist, it will mould. Check stored products weekly for signs of mould (green, black, or white fuzzy spots). If mould appears, discard the affected piece. Do not just cut it offmould can spread invisible spores.

For a small business, invest in a vacuum sealer (about R1000R2000) and a digital scale. Package in 50g, 100g, and 200g portions. Offer different flavours (original, chilli, garlic). Store in a cool room with a dehumidifier if possible. Keep records of production dates and batch numbers. This ensures you can trace any issues. With proper packaging, your biltong can reach customers far from your location, increasing sales and reducing waste.

<h2>Practical Reference: Biltong &amp; Drowors Production, Safety, and Shelf Life</h2>

<p>This practical reference distils the technical essentials of producing biltong and drowors at a semi-commercial scale. It is written for agribusiness operators, small meat processors, and farmer-entrepreneurs who need repeatable results, compliant processes, and a clear view of the cost and quality drivers that determine margin. Whether you are curing beef, game, or a blend, the same physics and microbiology govern the outcome: control water activity (a<sub>w</sub>), control temperature, control airflow, and control hygiene. Get these four right and you get consistent product, longer shelf life, and fewer losses.</p>

<h3>1. The Core Science in One Page</h3>

<p>Biltong and drowors are both preserved by a combination of salt, acid (vinegar), drying, andin the case of droworsfermentation. The target is to reduce water activity below the level at which spoilage and pathogenic bacteria can grow. For dried meat products, a<sub>w</sub> below 0.85 is generally regarded as shelf-stable at ambient temperature, while a<sub>w</sub> below 0.91 inhibits most pathogenic bacteria including <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>. Salt, sugar, and drying all lower a<sub>w</sub>; vinegar lowers pH, which adds a second hurdle.</p>

<h3>2. Process Parameters That Matter</h3>

<p>The single biggest cause of failed batches in humid African climates is insufficient airflow. Drying is a mass-transfer problem: water must move from the interior of the meat to the surface and then be carried away by air. If relative humidity is high and air speed is low, the surface stays wet, mould grows, and case-hardening traps moisture inside. Aim for air speed of 12 m/s across the meat surface, relative humidity below 65% where possible, and a temperature of 2027C. Higher temperatures speed drying but risk fat rancidity and surface hardening.</p>

<p>For drowors, the fermentation step adds complexity. Historically, drowors were made from spiced, salted beef or game offcuts stuffed into natural casings and dried. Modern food safety guidance treats them as a fermented dry sausage, which means you need a controlled fermentation to drop pH below 5.3 within 48 hours, followed by drying to a<sub>w</sub> below 0.85. Without a starter culture, you rely on the natural flora, which is unpredictable. For commercial production, a <em>Pediococcus pentosaceus</em> or <em>Lactobacillus sakei</em> starter is strongly recommended.</p>

<h3>3. Spice Blends and Regional Variation</h3>

<p>Biltong spice is not a single recipe. Across Southern Africa, the blend shifts with local availability and family tradition. The constant is a balance of salt, acid, and aromatics. A workable commercial baseline for 10 kg of raw beef strips is: 250 g coarse salt, 100 g brown sugar, 40 g cracked black pepper, 30 g toasted coriander seed (crushed), 15 g bicarbonate of soda (optional, for texture), and 250 ml brown spirit vinegar. For drowors, add 5 g ground cloves, 5 g ground nutmeg, and 10 g ground allspice, and reduce the vinegar.</p>

<p>Game meats change the equation. Kudu, springbok, and impala are leaner than beef, with less intramuscular fat. Leaner meat dries faster but can become brittle. Many producers add 510% beef fat or a small amount of pork fat to game biltong to improve mouthfeel and reduce breakage. Game also has a stronger, sometimes gamier flavour, so spice blends are often heavier on coriander and pepper to balance it.</p>

<h3>4. Building a Drying Box for Humid Climates</h3>

<p>In coastal or high-rainfall regionsLagos, Durban, Mombasa, Accraambient humidity can sit above 80% for weeks. A simple box with a fan is not enough. You need active dehumidification or a heat source that raises the air's moisture-holding capacity. A practical design for 2030 kg batches uses:</p>

<ul>
<li>An insulated cabinet (50 mm polystyrene or polyurethane panels) with a volume of 1.52 m.</li>
<li>A 100200 W axial fan moving air across the hanging meat at 12 m/s.</li>
<li>A 300500 W PTC heater with a thermostat set to 2528C.</li>
<li>A desiccant or compressor dehumidifier rated for at least 10 L/day.</li>
<li>An exhaust vent with a adjustable louvre to bleed moisture-laden air.</li>
<li>A digital hygrometer/thermometer and, ideally, a data logger.</li>
</ul>

<p>Run the box at 25C and 5560% RH for beef biltong. For drowors, start at 22C and 8085% RH for 48 hours to allow fermentation, then drop to 18C and 70% RH for drying. Never let the surface dry faster than the interiorif you see a hard shell forming, lower the temperature and raise the humidity slightly.</p>

<strong style="color:#b3261e;"> SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE PRODUCING FOR SALE</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Biltong and drowors are ready-to-eat (RTE) products. They are not cooked. Any pathogen present on the raw meat<em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em> O157:H7, <em>Listeria monocytogenes</em>can survive if the salt, acid, and drying hurdles are insufficient. In South Africa, RTE meat products must comply with SANS 885 and be produced in facilities registered with the NRCS. In the EU and UK, biltong imports must meet EU Regulation 852/2004 and 2073/2005 microbiological criteria. Do not sell product made in a home kitchen without a food safety management system based on HACCP. If you are producing for export, verify the destination country's requirements for RTE dried meat before investing in equipment. When in doubt, test every batch for a<sub>w</sub>, pH, and <em>Listeria</em>.</p>

<h3>5. Packaging and Shelf Life</h3>

<p>Properly dried biltong with a<sub>w</sub> below 0.85 and a pH below 5.5 can achieve 612 months shelf life at ambient temperature if packaged correctly. The main enemies after drying are moisture migration, oxygen (rancidity), and light. Use vacuum packaging or high-barrier pouches with an oxygen absorber for premium retail. For whole pieces sold in bulk, breathable paper bags work for short-term sale but not for long storage. Always label with production date, batch number, and best-before date. Store below 25C and away from direct sunlight.</p>

<p>For drowors, the lower moisture and fermentation acidity give a longer potential shelf life, but fat rancidity is the limiting factor. Keep storage temperatures low and consider nitrogen flushing for export markets. In humid climates, a sachet of silica gel inside bulk cartons helps, but it is not a substitute for correct drying.</p>

<h3>6. Business-First Considerations</h3>

<p>Biltong is a high-value, low-weight product, which makes it attractive for export and e-commerce. A 1 kg raw beef input at US$57 yields 400500 g of biltong that retails for US$2540/kg in premium markets. The margin is real, but so are the risks: inconsistent drying, mould losses, and regulatory rejection. Invest first in a reliable drying box and a water activity meter (US$300600). This single instrument will save you more money than any marketing spend. Second, standardise your spice blend by weight, not by eye. Third, build a simple batch record: raw weight, spice weight, drying time, temperature, RH, final weight, final a<sub>w</sub>, and sensory notes. After 20 batches you will have a proprietary process that is repeatable, trainable, and financeable.</p>
