1. Module 11 Lesson 1: Advanced Surface Preparation for African Climates

Welcome to Module 11, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on advanced surface preparation, which is the foundation of every durable paint or decoration job in Africa. Whether you are working in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or a rural district, the quality of your preparation determines whether your finish lasts two years or ten. Many painters lose repeat business because they skip steps in preparation. This lesson will help you build a reputation for work that survives heat, humidity, harmattan dust, and heavy rain. First, understand the enemies of a good finish in Africa. These include high humidity, intense UV radiation, dust from unpaved roads, salt air along the coast, and sudden temperature changes. Each enemy attacks different surfaces. On exterior walls, moisture pushes out through masonry and causes blistering and peeling. On metal, rust begins within hours of exposure if the surface is not properly treated. On wood, termites, borers, and moisture cause swelling and decay. Your job as a professional is to neutralize these threats before you open a tin of paint. Start with a proper assessment. Walk the site with the client and note every surface. Use a moisture meter if you have one. If not, tape a square of clear plastic to the wall, seal the edges, and leave it for 24 hours. If condensation appears, the wall is not ready for paint. Moisture readings above 12 to 15 percent mean you must delay or treat. In coastal cities like Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, or Dakar, assume high humidity and plan extra drying time. In dry regions like northern Nigeria or the Sahel, dust and heat are your main concerns. Next, clean the surface thoroughly. Remove all loose paint, chalk, mould, and dirt. Use a stiff brush, scraper, and where necessary a wire brush. For mould, mix one part household bleach to three parts water, apply, scrub, and rinse. Allow to dry completely. In dusty areas, wash walls the day before painting and cover them overnight to prevent new dust settling. For masonry, fill cracks and holes with a suitable filler or cement mortar. Use a flexible exterior filler for hairline cracks and a cement-based patching compound for larger holes. Allow to cure fully. Sand the patched areas smooth. For previously painted surfaces, sand glossy areas to create a key. This is called abrading. It helps the new paint bond. For metal surfaces, remove rust by wire brushing, sanding, or using a rust converter. Apply a zinc-rich primer or red oxide primer within four hours of cleaning. In humid coastal areas, use a two-part epoxy primer for maximum protection. For wood, sand with the grain, fill holes with wood filler, and apply a wood preservative or primer. Check for termite damage and treat if necessary. In many African markets, local hardware stores sell preservatives that are effective and affordable. Now consider the role of primers. A primer is not optional. It seals the surface, improves adhesion, and blocks stains. For new plaster, use an alkali-resistant primer because fresh cement is highly alkaline. For old painted walls, use a bonding primer. For metal, use a rust-inhibiting primer. For wood, use an oil-based or shellac primer to block knots. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions for drying times. In hot weather, primers dry faster, so work in smaller sections. In humid weather, allow more time. A common mistake is to apply primer too thickly. Thin coats are better. Two thin coats beat one thick coat. Also, do not paint over a primer that is still tacky. Test with the back of your hand. If it feels sticky, wait. Surface preparation also includes masking and protection. Cover floors, furniture, and plants. Use drop cloths, plastic sheeting, and masking tape. In Africa, where many jobs are in occupied homes or businesses, protecting the client's property builds trust. Remove or cover electrical outlets and light switches. Turn off power at the mains before working near wiring. Use ladders safely. On uneven ground, use leg levellers or a helper to foot the ladder. Never overreach. Now let us talk about tools. Invest in good quality scrapers, sanding blocks, wire brushes, and a power sander if you can afford one. A random orbital sander saves hours on large jobs. For masonry, a grinding cup wheel removes thick coatings. For metal, a wire cup brush on an angle grinder is fast but must be used with care. Always wear personal protective equipment: dust mask, safety glasses, gloves, and ear protection when using power tools. In many African cities, you can buy these items at affordable prices in local markets. Do not skip them. Your health is your business. Finally, document your preparation. Take before photos. Note the products you used and the drying times. This protects you if a client complains later. It also helps you quote future jobs accurately. A well-prepared surface is your best advertisement. When a client sees a finish that still looks good after two rainy seasons, they will call you again. That is how you build a painting and decorating business that lasts. In summary, advanced surface preparation means assessing moisture, cleaning thoroughly, repairing defects, sanding, priming correctly, protecting the site, using the right tools, and documenting your work. Do these things and your reputation will grow across your region. Next lesson, we will look at advanced paint application techniques for large and difficult surfaces.

2. Module 11 Lesson 2: Advanced Paint Application Techniques for African Buildings

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 11. Now that you understand advanced surface preparation, we move to advanced paint application. This lesson is about technique, efficiency, and quality. In Africa, buildings range from simple mud-brick homes to modern concrete high-rises. Each requires a different approach. Your ability to adapt will set you apart. We will cover brushes, rollers, sprayers, and specialty finishes. We will also discuss how to work in heat, wind, and rain. First, choose the right tool for the job. Brushes are best for cutting in, edges, trim, and small areas. Use a angled sash brush for windows and corners. Use a wide brush for large flat areas if you are not spraying. Rollers are faster for walls and ceilings. Use a short nap roller for smooth surfaces and a medium nap for lightly textured walls. For rough masonry, use a long nap roller. Sprayers are fastest for large jobs, but they require masking and skill. Airless sprayers are common in professional work. HVLP sprayers are good for fine finishes. In many African markets, you can rent sprayers by the day. This is often better than buying if you only need it occasionally. Before you start, mix your paint thoroughly. In hot climates, paint can separate. Use a mixing paddle on a drill or stir by hand for five minutes. Do not shake cans because that introduces air bubbles. For two-part paints, mix according to the manufacturer's ratio and use within the pot life. In high heat, pot life shortens. Mix only what you can use in 30 to 60 minutes. Now let us talk about cutting in. This is the art of painting a straight line where two surfaces meet, such as a wall and ceiling or a wall and trim. Load your brush about one third of the way up the bristles. Tap off excess. Start slightly away from the corner and brush into it. Use a steady hand. If you are not confident, use masking tape. In Africa, where many walls are not perfectly straight, masking tape can save time. Press the tape firmly to prevent bleed. Remove it while the paint is still slightly wet for a clean edge. For rolling, work in a W pattern. Start at the top left, roll a W shape, then fill in without lifting the roller. This spreads the paint evenly. Do not over-roll. Over-rolling causes roller marks and uneven sheen. Maintain a wet edge. This means you should not let the paint dry before you blend the next section. In hot, dry conditions, the wet edge disappears quickly. Work in smaller sections and add a paint extender if the manufacturer allows. For spraying, set the pressure according to the paint thickness. Test on a piece of cardboard. Hold the gun 12 to 18 inches from the surface. Move at a steady speed. Overlap each pass by 50 percent. Keep the gun perpendicular to the surface. Do not arc your wrist. In windy conditions, do not spray outdoors. The wind will carry the paint onto cars, plants, and neighbours. If you must spray outside, use a windbreak or spray early in the morning when the air is calm. Now consider specialty finishes. In Africa, there is growing demand for textured finishes, metallic paints, and decorative effects. Textured finishes hide imperfections in masonry. They are popular for exterior walls. Apply with a roller, trowel, or sprayer, then knock down with a trowel or brush for a pattern. Metallic paints require a smooth base and a topcoat of clear sealer to prevent oxidation. Decorative effects include sponging, ragging, and stippling. These are popular in hotels, restaurants, and upscale homes. Practice on sample boards before you offer these services. Charge more for specialty work because it takes more time and skill. Next, let us discuss working in African weather. Heat: paint dries too fast, causing brush marks and lap lines. Work early morning or late afternoon. Add a retarder if allowed. Keep paint in the shade. Humidity: paint dries slowly and may sag. Allow more time between coats. Use a dehumidifier if available. In coastal areas, avoid painting when the relative humidity is above 85 percent. Rain: never paint exterior surfaces when rain is expected within 24 hours. Water-based paints need time to cure. If rain hits fresh paint, it will wash off or leave streaks. Wind: wind blows dust and debris onto wet paint. It also causes spray drift. Protect the work area with screens or plastic sheeting. Now let us talk about efficiency. Time is money. Plan your work so that you are not waiting for paint to dry. While one area dries, move to another. Use a helper to cut in while you roll, or vice versa. Keep your tools clean and ready. At the end of the day, clean brushes and rollers thoroughly. In Africa, where good tools are expensive, proper cleaning extends their life. Use the correct solvent for the paint type. For water-based, use warm soapy water. For oil-based, use mineral spirits or turpentine. Never leave brushes standing in solvent. Shape them and hang them to dry. Quality control is essential. Check your work in good light. Look for missed spots, drips, and uneven coverage. Fix them before the client sees them. Use a flashlight at a low angle to reveal imperfections. If you are working with a team, appoint one person to inspect. Keep a touch-up kit with a small brush and the same paint. Finally, think about the business side. Advanced application allows you to charge higher prices. Offer packages: basic, premium, and luxury. Basic uses one coat of primer and two coats of standard paint. Premium uses two coats of primer and two coats of premium paint. Luxury includes specialty finishes and a warranty. Document your process with photos and a written scope. This protects you and educates the client. In summary, advanced paint application requires the right tools, proper mixing, good technique, and adaptation to weather. Master these skills and you will win larger contracts. Next lesson, we will cover colour theory and custom colour mixing for African markets.

3. Module 11 Lesson 3: Colour Theory and Custom Colour Mixing for African Markets

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 11. Colour is one of the most powerful tools in painting and decorating. It affects mood, perceived space, and even temperature. In Africa, colour carries cultural meaning and personal identity. As a professional, you must understand colour theory and be able to mix custom colours. This lesson will help you advise clients, create unique finishes, and increase your income. First, the basics of colour theory. The colour wheel has three primary colours: red, blue, and yellow. Mixing two primaries gives secondary colours: purple, green, and orange. Mixing a primary and a secondary gives tertiary colours. Complementary colours are opposite each other on the wheel. They create strong contrast. Analogous colours are next to each other. They create harmony. Warm colours include red, orange, and yellow. They make a room feel cosy and energetic. Cool colours include blue, green, and purple. They make a room feel calm and spacious. Neutral colours include white, black, grey, and brown. They are versatile and popular in modern African interiors. Now consider how light affects colour. In Africa, we have intense sunlight and long shadows. A colour that looks soft in the shop may look washed out on a sunlit wall. A dark colour may look almost black in shadow. Always test colours on the actual surface. Paint a sample board and view it at different times of day. Morning light is cooler. Afternoon light is warmer. Artificial light changes colour too. Incandescent bulbs make colours warmer. LED bulbs can make colours cooler or more neutral. Fluorescent bulbs can make colours look green. Advise clients to choose colours under the same lighting they will have in the room. Next, cultural considerations. In many African cultures, colours have meanings. In some West African traditions, white represents purity and peace. Red represents strength and danger. Green represents fertility and growth. Blue represents peace and harmony. Yellow represents wealth and royalty. In East Africa, earth tones like terracotta, ochre, and olive are popular for exteriors because they blend with the landscape. In Southern Africa, bright colours are often used in Ndebele art and modern urban design. As a professional, ask the client about their preferences and any cultural associations. Do not assume. A colour that is lucky in one culture may be inappropriate in another. Now let us talk about custom colour mixing. This is a valuable service. Many clients want a colour that is not available in the shop. You can mix it on site. You will need a colour wheel, measuring cups, mixing sticks, and empty containers. Start with a base paint, usually white. Add small amounts of tinting colourant. Mix thoroughly. Test on a sample board. Let it dry because wet paint looks lighter than dry paint. Adjust as needed. Keep a record of your formula. Use parts or millilitres. For example, 1000 ml white base plus 10 ml red oxide plus 5 ml black gives a certain terracotta. Write it down. If the client wants more later, you can reproduce it. There are two types of colourants: universal and proprietary. Universal colourants work with both water-based and oil-based paints. Proprietary colourants are made for a specific brand. Always check compatibility. In many African markets, you can buy universal colourants in hardware stores. They come in tubes or bottles. Use them sparingly. A little goes a long way. For large jobs, use a mechanical shaker if available. If not, mix with a paddle on a drill for at least five minutes. Now consider popular African colour palettes. Earth tones: terracotta, ochre, burnt sienna, olive green, and warm brown. These are great for exteriors and rustic interiors. Coastal tones: turquoise, aqua, sandy beige, and white. These work well in beach houses and hotels. Urban modern: charcoal grey, white, black, and a pop of bright colour like orange or lime. These are popular in apartments and offices. Traditional: deep reds, royal blues, and gold. These are used in ceremonial buildings and upscale homes. Heritage: muted greens, dusty pinks, and cream. These suit older buildings and colonial-style architecture. When you advise a client, consider the function of the room. Kitchens and bathrooms benefit from bright, clean colours. Bedrooms benefit from calm, cool colours. Living rooms can handle warmer, social colours. Offices need neutral, professional colours. Exteriors should reflect heat. Light colours reflect sunlight and keep buildings cooler. Dark colours absorb heat and can cause paint to fail faster. In hot African climates, light exteriors are practical. Now let us discuss colour matching. Sometimes a client wants to match an existing colour. You can do this by eye or with a colour matching tool. Some paint shops have spectrophotometers. If not, take a sample of the existing paint to the shop. They can tint a new batch to match. Be aware that old paint fades. A perfect match may look different on the wall. In that case, it is better to repaint the whole wall or room. Another skill is creating a feature wall. This is a wall painted in a different colour or finish to create interest. It is popular in African homes and businesses. Choose a colour that complements the room. Use a level and pencil to mark the area. Mask the edges. Paint the feature wall first, then the surrounding walls. This gives a clean line. You can also use wallpaper, stencils, or murals. These are advanced decorative techniques. If you have artistic talent, offer them as premium services. Now let us talk about selling colour. Many clients are afraid of colour. They choose white or cream because it is safe. Your job is to guide them. Show them sample boards. Use a colour visualizer app on your phone. Explain how colour affects mood and space. Offer a colour consultation as a paid service. This adds value and increases your income. You can also partner with interior designers. They need reliable painters who understand colour. Attend local trade shows and network with architects and decorators. Finally, keep learning. Colour trends change. Follow design magazines, social media, and paint manufacturer websites. Experiment with new palettes. Create a portfolio of your colour work. Take before and after photos. Show clients how you transformed a space. In summary, colour theory and custom mixing are essential skills for advanced painting and decorating. Understand the wheel, light, culture, and function. Mix colours accurately and record your formulas. Advise clients with confidence. Next lesson, we will cover special effects and decorative finishes for high-end African projects.

4. Module 11 Lesson 4: Special Effects and Decorative Finishes for High-End African Projects

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 11. In this lesson we explore special effects and decorative finishes. These are the techniques that turn a simple paint job into a work of art. They are in high demand in hotels, lodges, restaurants, offices, and luxury homes across Africa. If you master these skills, you can charge premium prices and stand out from ordinary painters. First, let us define decorative finishes. They are techniques that create texture, pattern, or depth on walls, ceilings, furniture, and floors. They include sponging, ragging, stippling, colour washing, marbling, wood graining, metallic effects, and textured plasters. Some are subtle. Others are dramatic. The key is to match the technique to the space and the client's taste. Before you begin any decorative finish, ensure the surface is perfectly prepared. This means clean, smooth, and primed. Any defect will show through. Use a high-quality primer that is compatible with your topcoat. For most decorative finishes, you will need a base coat. This is usually a solid colour that complements the effect. Apply two coats and allow to dry completely. Now let us go through the most popular techniques. Sponging: This creates a soft, mottled effect. You will need natural sea sponges, a base coat, and a glaze or topcoat in a contrasting colour. Dampen the sponge, dip it in the glaze, and dab off excess on a palette. Then dab lightly on the wall. Work in small sections. Vary the pressure and angle. Build up the effect gradually. Sponging is popular in African lodges because it mimics natural textures. Ragging: Similar to sponging but uses a rag or plastic bag. Crumple the rag, dip in glaze, and dab or roll on the wall. This creates a softer, more random pattern. Ragging is faster than sponging and works well in large areas. Stippling: Use a stippling brush to dab paint onto the wall. This creates a fine, textured finish. It is often used as a background for other effects. Colour washing: Dilute your paint or glaze with water or a medium. Apply it in broad strokes with a brush or roller. Then wipe it back with a rag to create a translucent, washed effect. This is popular for a rustic or Mediterranean look. Marbling: This mimics natural marble. You will need a base coat, a glaze, and a feather or sponge. Apply the glaze, then use the feather to draw veins. Use a sponge to soften the edges. Practice on a board first. Marbling is common in upscale African hotels and banks. Wood graining: This makes metal or plain wood look like expensive wood. You will need a base coat, a graining tool, and a glaze. Apply the glaze, then drag the graining tool through it to create grain lines. Use a brush to soften. This is a valuable skill for doors, furniture, and trim. Metallic effects: Use metallic paints or glazes. Apply over a dark base coat for depth. You can use a brush, roller, or sprayer. For a hammered metal look, use a sponge or stipple brush. For a smooth metal look, spray and buff. Seal with a clear coat to prevent oxidation. Textured plasters: These are thick coatings that create a raised texture. They are popular for exteriors in Africa because they hide imperfections and resist weather. Apply with a trowel, roller, or sprayer. Then knock down with a trowel for a stucco or travertine look. Some textured plasters are tinted. Others are painted after application. Now let us talk about tools and materials. You will need high-quality brushes, rollers, sponges, rags, feathers, graining tools, trowels, and palette knives. You will also need glazes, extenders, and retarders. In many African cities, you can find these at specialist paint shops or order online. Do not substitute cheap tools. They will ruin the effect. Practice on sample boards before you work on a client's wall. This is non-negotiable. Create a portfolio of samples. Show them to clients. Let them touch the textures. This sells the service. Now consider pricing. Decorative finishes take more time and skill than plain painting. You should charge at least double or triple your normal rate. Some effects, like marbling or wood graining, can be charged by the square metre at a premium. Always quote after you have seen the surface and discussed the design. Never guess. Include the cost of sample boards and practice time in your quote. Also, consider the drying time. Some glazes need to dry overnight. Plan your schedule accordingly. Now let us discuss safety and ventilation. Many decorative products contain solvents. Work in a well-ventilated area. Wear a respirator, gloves, and eye protection. In Africa, where many rooms do not have air conditioning, open windows and use fans. But be careful with fans because they can blow dust onto wet finishes. If possible, work when the room is empty. Cover furniture and floors. Now let us talk about marketing. High-end clients are found in hotels, lodges, restaurants, and wealthy neighbourhoods. Build relationships with interior designers, architects, and property developers. Show them your portfolio. Offer to do a free sample wall in a hotel lobby or showroom. This is a powerful marketing tool. Also, use social media. Post before and after photos. Show videos of you creating a finish. Use hashtags like #AfricanDecor, #PaintingAfrica, and #DecorativeFinishes. Many clients find painters on Instagram and Facebook. Finally, keep learning. Decorative finishes are constantly evolving. Attend workshops. Watch online tutorials. Experiment with new products. Some African manufacturers now produce excellent glazes and metallic paints. Support local businesses when you can. In summary, special effects and decorative finishes are a profitable niche. They require preparation, practice, and the right tools. Master a few techniques first, then expand. Your reputation as an artist will grow. Next lesson, we will cover business growth, pricing, and client management for painting and decorating professionals in Africa.

5. Module 11 Lesson 5: Business Growth, Pricing, and Client Management for African Painters

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 11. This final lesson is about turning your painting and decorating skills into a profitable, sustainable business. Many talented painters in Africa struggle because they do not understand pricing, contracts, or client management. This lesson will give you practical tools to grow. First, let us talk about pricing. Many painters undercharge. They calculate only the cost of paint and a small labour fee. This is a mistake. Your price must cover materials, labour, transport, tools, insurance, taxes, and profit. Start by calculating your hourly rate. Add up your monthly business expenses: rent, transport, phone, internet, tools, insurance, and taxes. Add your desired monthly salary. Divide by the number of billable hours per month. In Africa, a common mistake is to assume you will work 40 hours per week. In reality, you may only bill 20 to 25 hours because of quoting, travel, and waiting for paint to dry. So your hourly rate must be higher. For example, if your monthly expenses are 500,000 shillings and you want to earn 700,000 shillings, you need 1,200,000 shillings per month. If you bill 100 hours, your hourly rate is 12,000 shillings. Now add materials. For each job, estimate paint, primer, filler, sandpaper, masking tape, and other consumables. Add 10 to 15 percent for waste and touch-ups. Add transport. If the job is far, charge for fuel and time. Add a profit margin of 20 to 30 percent. This is not greed. It is what allows you to reinvest, buy better tools, and survive slow months. Now let us talk about quoting. Never give a verbal quote. Write it down. Use a simple quotation form with your business name, client name, date, scope of work, materials, labour, total price, payment terms, and validity period. Include what is not included, such as moving furniture or repairing structural damage. This protects you from disputes. In many African countries, clients expect to negotiate. That is fine. But do not lower your price without reducing the scope. For example, if the client wants a lower price, offer one coat of primer instead of two, or use a standard paint instead of premium. Never reduce your labour rate. Your time is valuable. Now let us discuss contracts. For large jobs, use a simple contract. It should include the scope, price, payment schedule, start date, completion date, and warranty. A typical payment schedule is 30 percent deposit, 40 percent at midpoint, and 30 percent on completion. The deposit covers materials. Never start a job without a deposit. If the client refuses, walk away. They are not serious. For small jobs, a written quotation and a deposit receipt are enough. Always give a receipt for every payment. Use a receipt book with duplicate copies. This builds trust and helps you track income. Now let us talk about client management. The first rule is communication. Respond to calls and messages within 24 hours. If you are busy, send a quick message to say when you will reply. Be on time. If you are delayed, call ahead. Clients hate waiting. The second rule is to manage expectations. Explain the process. Tell them how long it will take. Tell them about drying times, odours, and mess. Tell them what they need to do, such as moving furniture or keeping children away. The third rule is to protect their property. Cover floors and furniture. Clean up every day. Leave the site tidy. The fourth rule is to handle complaints professionally. If the client is unhappy, listen. Do not argue. Inspect the work together. If it is your fault, fix it. If it is not, explain politely. Offer a solution. A satisfied client will refer you to others. A dissatisfied client will tell everyone. Now let us talk about marketing. You do not need a big budget. Start with word of mouth. Ask every satisfied client for a referral. Offer a small discount for referrals. Create a simple business card. Include your name, phone number, and services. Leave cards at hardware stores, real estate offices, and furniture shops. Use social media. Create a Facebook page or Instagram account. Post photos of your work. Use before and after shots. Ask clients for reviews. Join local business groups. Attend trade shows. Partner with interior designers, architects, and property managers. They are a steady source of high-value work. Now let us discuss record keeping. You must track income and expenses. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet. Record every job, every payment, and every expense. This helps you know if you are making a profit. It also helps with taxes. In many African countries, small businesses must pay taxes. Do not ignore them. Register your business if required. It may seem like a burden, but it opens doors to larger contracts and government work. Now let us talk about growth. As your business grows, you will need to hire. Start with one or two helpers. Train them in your methods. Pay them fairly and on time. Treat them well. A loyal team is your greatest asset. You can also expand your services. Add wallpaper installation, floor coating, or waterproofing. Add decorative finishes. Add colour consultation. Each new service increases your average job value. Consider buying a vehicle. It makes transport easier and allows you to take larger jobs. Consider renting a small shop or storage unit. It gives you a place to keep tools and mix paint. Now let us discuss risk management. Painting and decorating has risks: falls, chemical exposure, and damage to client property. Get insurance if you can. At minimum, use safety equipment and follow safe practices. Have a first aid kit on site. Know the emergency numbers. Keep a fire extinguisher in your vehicle. Finally, let us talk about your reputation. In Africa, reputation is everything. It is built on quality, honesty, and reliability. Do what you say you will do. Charge what you quoted. Finish on time. If you make a mistake, fix it. Treat every client with respect, whether they are rich or poor. Your character is your brand. In summary, business growth requires proper pricing, written quotes, contracts, client management, marketing, record keeping, and risk management. These are not optional. They are the foundation of a professional painting and decorating business. You have now completed Module 11. You have learned advanced surface preparation, application techniques, colour theory, decorative finishes, and business skills. Use them. Practice them. Teach them to others. Africa needs skilled, professional painters. You can be one of the best. Thank you for your attention. Now go and build your business.

6. Practical Reference

Module 11 Lesson 6: Practical Reference Guide for African Painting and Decorating Contractors

This practical reference lesson consolidates the technical, commercial, and safety knowledge covered across Module 11 into a single field-ready resource. It is designed for established painters, decorating contractors, and workshop owners operating in African markets who need quick access to verified figures, substrate-specific guidance, and business benchmarks. Unlike the preceding lessons, this module prioritises decision-making speed: the tables, diagram, and checklist below are formatted so they can be printed, laminated, and used on site or during client quotations.

African construction environments present conditions rarely covered in European or North American training material: high ultraviolet (UV) loads, harmattan dust, seasonal monsoon humidity, laterite and cement-rich substrates, and rapidly fluctuating temperatures. Paint failures in these markets are more often caused by substrate and climate mismatch than by poor brushwork. Getting the preparation and product selection right is therefore the single highest-leverage commercial decision a decorating business can make, because rework destroys margin faster than any other cost line.

â  SAFETY WARNING: Chemical and Respiratory Hazards

Solvent-borne paints, thinners, and two-pack epoxy or polyurethane systems release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that can cause acute respiratory irritation, headache, and long-term neurological and organ damage. In hot African conditions, evaporation rates increase and confined-space concentrations rise rapidly.

  • Always work in ventilated spaces; use forced-air extraction in enclosed rooms.
  • Wear a fitted respirator with organic vapour cartridges when spraying or using two-pack systems â a dust mask is not sufficient.
  • Never eat, drink, or smoke in the work area; wash hands before breaks.
  • Store solvents away from direct sunlight and ignition sources; African ambient temperatures can exceed flash points in unventilated stores.
  • Lead-based paints may still be present in older buildings. Refer to WHO guidance on lead paint and test before sanding or burning off.
  • Pregnant workers and apprentices under 18 should not handle solvent-borne or two-pack systems without supervision and controls.

Source: WHO (2020) Lead in Paint; US OSHA Hazard Communication Standard 29 CFR 1910.1200.

Quick Reference Checklist: Site and Business Readiness

  1. Substrate verified: Confirm surface type, moisture content (below 16% for masonry), pH (below 10 for cement), and presence of salts or efflorescence before specifying any coating.
  2. Climate window checked: Avoid exterior application when rain is forecast within the recoat interval, when relative humidity exceeds 85%, or during harmattan dust peaks.
  3. Product data sheets on file: Keep the TDS for every product used; it protects you in disputes and ensures correct thinning, recoat, and overcoating practice.
  4. Quantity measured, not estimated: Calculate litres from measured square metres and the manufacturer's spreading rate, then add 10% waste allowance.
  5. Labour priced separately: Quote preparation, priming, topcoating, and decorative work as distinct line items so clients see value and you protect margin.
  6. Safety equipment present: Respirators, gloves, eye protection, ladders, and harnesses for roof work must be on site before work begins, not promised later.
  7. Payment schedule agreed: Minimum 40% mobilisation deposit, 40% at completion of preparation and priming, 20% on final inspection and sign-off.
  8. Snag list and warranty defined: Issue a written 12-month workmanship warranty covering adhesion and application, excluding substrate movement and physical damage.
