🐔 Practical Poultry Feed Formulation

The arithmetic of mixing your own poultry feed - building an ingredient table you trust, using Pearson's Square to hit a protein target, checking energy, calcium and phosphorus afterwards, and producing broiler and layer rations you can mix this week from maize, cakes, fish meal, limestone, DCP and premix.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Build a working ingredient table with protein, energy, calcium and phosphorus for local feedstuffs
  • Use Pearson's Square to hit a crude protein target on a two-ingredient basis
  • Formulate a layer ration to a daily nutrient intake rather than a diet percentage
  • Read a premix label against published vitamin and trace mineral specifications
  • Set particle size and mixing time so every bird eats the ration you formulated
  • Substitute ingredients on a nutrient basis when a price moves against you

Lesson 6.1~12 min

Poultry Feed Ingredient Database

In this lesson
  • Build a working ingredient table with protein, energy, calcium and phosphorus for local feedstuffs
  • Assign each ingredient a maximum safe inclusion level based on its anti-nutritional factor
  • Update your table with real supplier prices so every formulation is costed as it is built

You cannot formulate without a table. Every ration you will ever mix is arithmetic performed on a list of ingredients and what each one contains. If the list is wrong, the arithmetic is wasted. So the first job is to build a table you trust, in your own notebook, for the ingredients you can actually buy in your own town.

Each ingredient needs six columns: crude protein percentage, metabolisable energy in kcal/kg, calcium percentage, phosphorus percentage, maximum safe inclusion, and delivered price per kilogram. The first four decide whether the ration works. The fifth stops you poisoning the birds. The sixth decides whether you make money.

Start with published energy values, which are the most reliable figures available. Yellow dent maize sits at 3,350 kcal AMEn/kg with low variability of about plus or minus 100, and roughly 88% digestibility - it is the reference against which everything else is judged. Wheat is 2,800 kcal/kg at around 75% digestibility with moderate variability of about plus or minus 150. Barley is 2,600-2,700 kcal/kg at 73%, and oats are 2,400-2,500 kcal/kg at 67% with high variability. On the protein side, soybean meal at 48% crude protein carries 2,150 kcal/kg with about 75% of protein and energy digestible. Fish meal at 60% crude protein carries 2,700-2,900 kcal/kg at 75-80% digestibility. Meat and bone meal is 2,200-2,400 kcal/kg at about 70% digestibility but with high variability of plus or minus 200 because processing and fat content differ so much between renderers. Low-glucosinolate canola meal is 1,900-2,000 kcal/kg at 65-70%, and traditional rapeseed meal lower again at 1,800-1,900 kcal/kg at 60-65%.

Amino acid quality separates ingredients that look identical on crude protein. Standardised ileal digestibility of lysine runs 90-98% in fish meal, 85-95% in soybean meal, 75-85% in meat meal, 70-80% in maize and canola meal, and 65-75% in wheat. Methionine plus cysteine follows the same ranking: 85-95% in fish meal, 80-90% in soybean meal, down to 60-70% in wheat. Threonine likewise. Young broilers aged 0-7 days digest less well than birds of 14-21 days, so a starter built on marginal ingredients underperforms more than the table suggests.

Now the ceiling column, which is where small producers get hurt.

  1. Cottonseed cake: 5-10% of the diet, set so total gossypol stays under 0.1% of the finished feed. Lower for chicks, which are more sensitive than older birds, and lower for broilers than layers.
  2. Traditional rapeseed meal: 2-5% because of glucosinolates and goitrogenic effects. Low-glucosinolate canola meal: 5-15%.
  3. Groundnut cake: governed by aflatoxin, not by an amino acid. Chicks tolerate only 0.005 ppm, broilers and layers 0.02 ppm. Untested groundnut cake has no place in starter feed.
  4. Maize and other cereals: watch fumonisins, with limits of 10 ppm for chicks, 20 ppm for broilers and 30 ppm for layers and breeders - and remember that 18.6 ppm, below the broiler limit, still altered gut microflora and predisposed birds to necrotic enteritis.

Mineral ingredients need their own lines. Limestone carries roughly 38% calcium and almost no phosphorus. Dicalcium phosphate carries roughly 24% calcium and 18% phosphorus, most of it available. Common salt supplies sodium and chloride, and Ross 2022 asks for 0.18-0.23% of each. Bone meal and fish meal both contribute calcium and phosphorus you must count - fish meal at around 5% calcium is enough to change a starter formulation, and forgetting it is the commonest cause of over-calcified starter feed and leg problems.

Finally, phytate. Maize holds 0.7-1.0% phytic acid, soybean meal 1.0-1.3%, barley 0.8-1.2%, and fish meal under 0.1%. Phytate chelates minerals, cutting phosphorus availability by 20-50% and zinc by 30-60%. This is why the phosphorus column in your table should say total P and available P separately, and why plant phosphorus should never be counted at face value.

A Zimbabwean example: a producer near Marondera keeps his table on a single laminated A4 sheet nailed above the mixer, with prices pencilled in and rewritten every month. When maize jumped 22% in one season he could see instantly, at the mixer, that adding 5 kg of maize bran and 1 kg of oil per 100 kg held energy while cutting cost - a decision he would have got wrong from memory.

Lesson 6.2~12 min

Broiler Ration Formulation

In this lesson
  • Use Pearson's Square to hit a crude protein target on a two-ingredient basis
  • Run the four post-square checks for energy, calcium, available phosphorus and salt
  • Produce a costed 100 kg broiler starter mix sheet you can take to the mixer

Pearson's Square is the simplest way to blend two feedstuffs to a protein target, and once you add a reserved-space step and four checks, it produces rations good enough to run a commercial flock on. Here is the whole method on a real broiler starter.

Step 1. Fix the target. NRC 1994 puts broiler starter at 23.0% crude protein for days 0-21. Ross 2022 asks for 2,975 kcal/kg, 0.95% calcium and 0.50% available phosphorus, with sodium and chloride each at 0.18-0.23%.

Step 2. Reserve space for the ingredients that are not about protein. In a 100 kg batch, set aside roughly 6 kg for oil, limestone, DCP, salt, methionine, premix and enzyme. That leaves 94 kg of grain and protein cake, and those 94 kg must still carry all 23 kg of protein. So the blend you are about to build needs 23 divided by 0.94, which is 24.5% crude protein.

Step 3. Choose your two sides. Energy side: maize at 8.5% crude protein. Protein side: a blend of soya cake at 44% and fish meal at 55% mixed four parts to one, giving 0.8 x 44 + 0.2 x 55 = 46.2% crude protein.

Step 4. Draw the square. Put 24.5 in the middle, maize 8.5 top left, protein blend 46.2 bottom left. Subtract diagonally, always taking the smaller from the larger. 46.2 - 24.5 = 21.7 parts of maize. 24.5 - 8.5 = 16.0 parts of protein blend. Total 37.7 parts.

Step 5. Convert parts to kilograms. Maize = 21.7 / 37.7 x 94 = 54.1 kg. Protein blend = 16.0 / 37.7 x 94 = 39.9 kg, which splits into 31.9 kg soya cake and 8.0 kg fish meal.

Step 6. Check crude protein. (54.1 x 0.085) + (31.9 x 0.44) + (8.0 x 0.55) = 4.60 + 14.04 + 4.40 = 23.04 kg of protein in 100 kg. That is 23.0%. The square worked.

Now the four checks that turn a protein calculation into a ration.

Check 1, energy. 54.1 x 3,350 = 181,235; 31.9 x 2,150 = 68,585; 8.0 x 2,800 = 22,400. Total 272,220 kcal, which is 2,722 kcal/kg - well short of 2,975. Add 2 kg of vegetable oil at about 8,800 kcal/kg and take 2 kg out of the reserved space: that adds 17,600 kcal and lifts you to 2,898 kcal/kg. A hand-mixed smallholder mash realistically lands at 2,850-2,950, and you should note the gap rather than pretend it away, because a bird on lower energy simply eats more feed to reach the same weight.

Check 2, calcium. Fish meal carries roughly 5% calcium, so 8.0 kg contributes 0.40 kg. DCP at 24% calcium contributes 1.6 x 0.24 = 0.38 kg. Limestone at 38% calcium: use only 0.4 kg, contributing 0.15 kg. Total 0.93 kg, so 0.93% calcium against the 0.95% target. This is the check most home mixers fail - they add the standard 1.5 kg of limestone, forget the fish meal, and land near 1.3% calcium, which exceeds the 1.0% level above which phosphorus metabolism is disturbed and leg problems appear.

Check 3, available phosphorus. DCP at 18% phosphorus gives 1.6 x 0.18 = 0.29 kg, essentially all available. Fish meal at about 2.8% phosphorus gives 0.22 kg, also highly available. Total 0.51%, against the 0.50% target. Plant phosphorus is ignored deliberately because phytate cuts availability by 20-50%; add phytase at 100 g of a 10,000 FTU/g product per tonne and you can trim DCP later.

Check 4, salt. 0.30 kg of salt per 100 kg puts sodium near 0.12% from salt alone, with fish meal adding more. Ross asks for 0.18-0.23% sodium, so 0.35-0.40 kg is usually right; in hot weather you will raise the dietary electrolyte balance deliberately, which is covered in Module 7 Lesson 1.

The finished starter, per 100 kg: maize 55.6, soya cake 31.9, fish meal 8.0, vegetable oil 2.0, DCP 1.6, limestone 0.4, salt 0.35, DL-methionine 0.25, premix 0.30 - adjust maize last so the total reads exactly 100.0 kg. At illustrative prices of maize $0.32, soya cake $0.62, fish meal $1.10, oil $1.40, DCP $0.85, limestone $0.09, salt $0.25, methionine $4.20 and premix $2.60, the batch costs about $52 per 100 kg, or $520 per tonne.

A Malawian example: a Lilongwe producer running 600 birds per cycle formulates exactly this way on a school exercise book, then reformulates the grower by dropping fish meal to 5 kg, soya to 27 kg and pushing maize to 62 kg to reach the 20% NRC grower figure - the same square, a new target number.

Layer Ration Formulation

In this lesson
  • Formulate a layer ration to a daily nutrient intake rather than a diet percentage
  • Calculate limestone inclusion and split it into fine and coarse fractions
  • Cost a layer ration per kilogram and per dozen eggs produced

Layer formulation is different from broiler formulation in one decisive way: you formulate to a daily intake, not to a percentage. A hen eating 90 g a day and a hen eating 110 g a day need different concentrations of the same nutrients to receive the same grams per day. Get this backwards and you either starve a good hen or overfeed a poor one.

Start from the published intake-linked figures. Hy-Line Brown at peak production on 90 g of feed a day needs 19.83% crude protein, 0.92% SID lysine and 0.83% SID Met+Cys. The same strain in the Layer 5 phase, below 85% production and eating 110 g a day, needs only 16.23% crude protein, 0.75% lysine and 0.68% Met+Cys - because the extra 20 g of feed carries the difference. Lohmann Brown-Classic at 110 g asks for 0.78% lysine, 0.39% methionine and 0.70% Met+Cys. On energy, a white-egg layer eating 100 g a day needs about 2,800 kcal/kg, while one eating only 80 g a day needs about 3,000 kcal/kg. Lohmann's layer phase runs at 11.6 MJ/kg falling to 11.4 MJ/kg, which is roughly 2,770 down to 2,725 kcal/kg.

Calcium is the other difference, and it is large. Hy-Line Brown needs 4.0 g of calcium per bird per day at peak, giving a dietary concentration of 3.8-4.1% depending on intake, with a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of 8.4-11.0:1 that would be dangerous in a broiler. At week 77 and beyond, daily calcium rises to 4.7 g, giving 3.92-4.70% of diet. Each egg takes 2.2-2.5 g of calcium out of the hen.

Here is a worked layer ration for a hen eating 110 g a day, targeting 16.2% crude protein and 3.9% calcium.

Step 1. Reserve the non-protein space: limestone 9.5 kg, DCP 1.2 kg, salt 0.35 kg, DL-methionine 0.15 kg, premix 0.30 kg, oil 1.0 kg - 12.5 kg in total, leaving 87.5 kg. Those 87.5 kg must carry all 16.2 kg of protein, so blend to 16.2 / 0.875 = 18.5%.

Step 2. Sides of the square: maize at 8.5%, and a protein blend of soya cake and sunflower cake at three parts to one, giving 0.75 x 44 + 0.25 x 30 = 40.5%.

Step 3. Diagonals: 40.5 - 18.5 = 22.0 parts maize; 18.5 - 8.5 = 10.0 parts protein blend; 32.0 parts total. Maize = 22.0 / 32.0 x 87.5 = 60.2 kg. Protein blend = 10.0 / 32.0 x 87.5 = 27.3 kg, splitting into 20.5 kg soya cake and 6.8 kg sunflower cake.

Step 4. Check protein: (60.2 x 0.085) + (20.5 x 0.44) + (6.8 x 0.30) = 5.12 + 9.02 + 2.04 = 16.18 kg, so 16.2%. That is close to the Hy-Line Layer 5 figure of 16.23% for a hen on 110 g.

Step 5. Check energy: 60.2 x 3,350 = 201,670; 20.5 x 2,150 = 44,075; sunflower cake at roughly 2,000 kcal/kg gives 13,600; oil 1.0 x 8,800 = 8,800. Total 268,145 kcal, or 2,681 kcal/kg - about 11.2 MJ/kg, just under the Lohmann layer figure. On 110 g a day the hen still receives about 295 kcal, which is workable, but if your birds eat less than 105 g you must raise oil and lower bran.

Step 6. Check calcium: limestone at 38% calcium gives 9.5 x 0.38 = 3.61 kg; DCP at 24% gives 1.2 x 0.24 = 0.29 kg. Total 3.90 kg, so 3.90% calcium. At 110 g of intake that delivers 4.29 g of calcium per bird per day, comfortably above the 4.0 g peak requirement.

Step 7. Split the limestone. Coarse particles of 2-4 mm sit in the gizzard and release calcium slowly through the night when the shell is being formed; fine particles are absorbed quickly and serve bone maintenance. At peak production use 40% fine and 60% coarse, so of your 9.5 kg, 3.8 kg is fine and 5.7 kg is coarse. In late production, from about week 77, shift to 25% fine and 75% coarse - 2.4 kg fine and 7.1 kg coarse - which is the single cheapest fix for thin shells in an ageing flock.

Cost it. At maize $0.32, soya cake $0.62, sunflower cake $0.30, oil $1.40, limestone $0.09, DCP $0.85, salt $0.25, methionine $4.20 and premix $2.60, the 100 kg batch comes to about $39, or $390 per tonne. Using a working figure of 1.5 kg of feed per dozen eggs for a small flock - Hy-Line reports 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen at peak and 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen across a full cycle - feed cost is about $0.59 per dozen.

A Kenyan example: a Nakuru producer with 500 Lohmann Browns weighed her feed hopper for a week and found birds were eating 118 g, not the 110 g she assumed. Her calcium delivery was fine, but her protein delivery was 8% higher than needed and she was paying for it. She dropped soya cake by 1.5 kg per 100 kg and saved about $9 per tonne with no drop in lay.

Vitamin and Mineral Premix Preparation

In this lesson
  • Read a premix label against published vitamin and trace mineral specifications
  • Calculate the inclusion rate needed to deliver a target level in the finished feed
  • Pre-blend micro-ingredients safely using stepwise dilution before mixing

The premix is the smallest line in your feed budget and the largest lever on health. It is also the ingredient most small producers understand least, because the quantities are grams and milligrams rather than kilograms, and because bad premix looks exactly like good premix.

Start with what should be in it. Ross 2022 for broilers specifies vitamin A at 13,000 IU/kg in starter, 11,000 in grower and 10,000 in finisher; vitamin D3 at 5,000, 4,500 and 4,000 IU/kg; and vitamin E at 80, 65 and 55 IU/kg. Trace minerals run zinc 120 mg/kg, manganese 120 mg/kg, copper 16 mg/kg and selenium 0.30 mg/kg, with iodine around 0.30 mg/kg and iron typically 40-60 mg/kg. Note how far above the older NRC 1994 minimums these sit - NRC gives vitamin A at 1,500-5,000 IU/kg, vitamin D3 at 200-1,100 IU/kg, vitamin E at 5-25 IU/kg, zinc at 35-45 mg/kg and selenium at 0.06-0.2 mg/kg. NRC describes the level below which deficiency appears. Ross describes the level at which a modern fast-growing bird performs. Formulate to the breeder figures for commercial stock. B vitamins sit in narrower bands: riboflavin 1.8-4.0 mg/kg, niacin 10-70 mg/kg, pantothenic acid 2-16 mg/kg, pyridoxine 2.0-3.0 mg/kg, biotin 0.04-0.15 mg/kg, folate 0.5-1.0 mg/kg and B12 at just 0.002-0.004 mg/kg. Vitamin K runs 0.5-1.75 mg/kg, and layer choline 500-1,600 mg/kg.

Now the arithmetic that turns a label into an inclusion rate. Suppose a premix label states 4,000,000 IU of vitamin A per kilogram of premix and recommends 2.5 kg per tonne. Feed vitamin A = 4,000,000 x 2.5 / 1,000 kg of feed = 10,000 IU/kg. That meets the Ross finisher figure but falls short of the 13,000 IU/kg starter figure. To reach 13,000 you would need 13,000 x 1,000 / 4,000,000 = 3.25 kg per tonne. Run the same division for vitamin E and selenium before you accept a supplier's recommended rate, because premixes are formulated for different markets and a rate that suits a temperate broiler may not suit yours.

Selenium deserves its own check. At 0.30 mg/kg of feed and a 2.5 kg per tonne inclusion, the premix must contain 0.30 x 1,000 / 2.5 = 120 mg of selenium per kilogram of premix. Selenium is toxic at excess and essential at trace, so it is also the ingredient that punishes bad mixing hardest. Pair it with vitamin E - the published ratio guidance is roughly 10-20 IU of vitamin E per 0.3 mg of selenium, and the two work together in the glutathione peroxidase antioxidant system.

Should you make your own vitamin premix? No. Weighing 2 mg of B12 per tonne on a farm scale is not possible, and vitamin potency depends on stabilised, coated forms you cannot buy loose. Buy the vitamin-trace mineral premix from a supplier who prints the levels on the label. What you can and should do yourself is the pre-blend.

Stepwise dilution is the technique. To put 300 g of premix into a 100 kg batch evenly:

  1. Weigh the 300 g of premix into a bucket.
  2. Add roughly 1 kg of maize meal or fine bran and mix thoroughly by hand for two minutes. You now have about 1.3 kg of first dilution.
  3. Add about 4 kg more maize meal and mix again. You now have about 5.3 kg of second dilution.
  4. Spread that 5.3 kg across the main batch during mixing, not dumped in one corner.

Do the same for salt, DL-methionine and any phytase or probiotic. A 250 g addition of methionine dumped straight onto a heap of maize will never distribute, and the birds at the wrong end of the trough get none.

Storage rules are simple and non-negotiable. Keep premix out of sunlight, sealed, off the floor, and buy in quantities you will use within roughly three months. Vitamins A, D3 and E are the first casualties of heat and oxidation, and a premix stored in an open sack in a tin-roofed store in an African dry season is losing potency every week. Rancid fat in the feed itself also destroys vitamins A and E, so do not store premix beside old oily cake.

A Tanzanian example: a Morogoro producer with 900 broilers was seeing pale shanks and poor feathering on a premix bought loose by the scoop from a market trader, with no label at all. He switched to a sealed, labelled 25 kg premix, stored it in a lidded plastic drum in the coolest corner of his store, and pre-blended it in two dilution steps. The premix cost roughly $0.008 per kilogram of feed - under 2% of feed cost - and the feathering problem disappeared in a month.

Lesson 6.5~12 min

Feed Milling and Quality Control

In this lesson
  • Set particle size and mixing time so every bird eats the ration you formulated
  • Run a mixer uniformity test with materials you already own
  • Keep a batch record that lets you trace a problem back to an ingredient

A perfect formulation badly mixed is a bad ration. The bird does not eat your mix sheet; it eats whatever lands in front of it in the trough. Milling and mixing decide whether those two things are the same. This lesson covers the mechanics on the mill floor; storage, rodent control and the biosecurity of incoming ingredients are dealt with in Module 10.

Particle size comes first. Grinding maize too finely wastes fuel, produces dust that birds refuse and that separates in the bag, and can worsen gizzard development. Grinding too coarsely leaves particles birds selectively pick out, so the ration they eat is not the ration you made. For mash feed a medium grind - roughly the texture of coarse cornmeal, with visible particles but no large chips - is the practical target on a hammer mill with a 3-4 mm screen. The important exception is limestone. Coarse limestone of 2-4 mm must stay coarse; if it goes through the hammer mill with the maize you have destroyed the slow-release calcium your layers need for overnight shell formation. Add it after grinding, never before.

Mixing time is the second control. Under-mixing leaves pockets; over-mixing on a horizontal mixer can cause the fine, dense ingredients to separate again. As a working rule on a small ribbon or drum mixer, load the bulk ingredients first, add the pre-blended micro-ingredients partway through, and run for three to five minutes after the last addition. Then stop. Record the time.

Order of addition matters more than most people expect. A workable sequence is:

  1. Half the maize.
  2. The protein cakes and fish meal.
  3. The pre-blended premix, salt, methionine and enzyme, spread across the surface rather than dumped.
  4. The mineral sources - DCP and fine limestone.
  5. The remaining maize.
  6. Oil last, added slowly while mixing, so it coats rather than clumps.

Test whether your mixer actually mixes. You do not need a laboratory. Take about 200 g of cracked coloured material - dyed salt, crushed charcoal, or simply whole maize grains in a mash batch - and add it with the micro-ingredients. After the mixing time is up, take ten samples from different points in the batch: top, bottom, both ends, the middle, and inside the discharge. Count the marker particles in each sample. If the counts are broadly similar, your mixer works. If one sample has thirty particles and another has three, your mixing time is too short or your mixer is overloaded. Most small mixers fail this test when filled beyond about two-thirds of drum volume.

Sampling for quality control is the third habit. Take a small handful from every incoming bag of a new consignment, combine them in a bucket, mix, and keep about 500 g in a labelled sealed bag with the date and supplier name. If a flock goes wrong six weeks later, that retained sample is the only evidence you will have. Keep retained samples for at least the length of one full production cycle plus a month.

The batch record is the fourth. One line per batch in a hard-backed book, with: date, batch number, ration type, every ingredient and its weight, the supplier and lot of any ingredient that changed since last time, the mixing time, the operator's name, and the house or flock it was fed to. This takes two minutes and is the difference between diagnosing a problem and guessing at one.

Simple daily checks catch most disasters before they reach the birds. Smell every consignment of cake - rancid or musty means reject. Break open maize grains at random and look for discoloured or shrivelled kernels, which flag mould and therefore mycotoxin risk. Check that fish meal has not been diluted or salted; salted fish meal will wreck your sodium balance, which Ross sets at only 0.18-0.23%. Weigh your scale against a known weight monthly - a 5% scale error on limestone is invisible and expensive.

A Nigerian example: an Ibadan producer mixing 2 tonnes a week found birds in one house growing 8% slower than the other on identical feed. His batch record showed nothing unusual, but a marker test revealed his drum mixer, which he had been filling to the brim to save time, was leaving the premix concentrated in the last third of every batch. Filling to two-thirds and mixing four minutes rather than two fixed it. The equipment was fine. The loading was not.

Feed Optimization and Troubleshooting

In this lesson
  • Substitute ingredients on a nutrient basis when a price moves against you
  • Diagnose common flock symptoms back to a specific formulation error
  • Recalculate a ration to hold performance while cutting cost per tonne

Formulation is not something you do once. Prices move, suppliers change, seasons change intake, and birds tell you when something is wrong. This lesson is about reading those signals and reacting with arithmetic rather than panic.

Start with substitution. When maize jumps 25% overnight, the instinct is to add maize bran. Bran is cheap, but it carries fibre and much less energy than whole maize, so you dilute the ration. If you replace 8 kg of maize with 8 kg of bran per 100 kg you lose roughly 8 x (3,350 - 1,700) = 13,200 kcal from the batch, or 132 kcal/kg of feed. To put it back you need about 1.5 kg of oil per 100 kg. Do that arithmetic before you change the bag, not after the growth check.

The same rule applies on protein. Moving 5 kg from soya cake to sunflower cake keeps crude protein roughly level but drops lysine, because sunflower is lysine-poor and methionine-adequate. Either add 1-1.5 kg of fish meal to restore lysine or accept slower growth. Crude protein is a nitrogen figure; the bird eats amino acids.

Now troubleshooting. Read symptoms as arithmetic problems.

  1. Slow growth on a ration that looks correct on paper. Check energy first, then intake. A diluted or over-fibrous ration means the bird fills its crop before meeting its energy requirement. In hot weather intake is already suppressed, so the same ration underperforms in January and works in July.
  2. Lameness, splayed legs, twisted hocks in young broilers. Check calcium in the starter. Anything above 1.0% interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes to tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformities. Check the Ca to available P ratio too: the target is around 2:1, with ratios below 1.5:1 or above 2.5:1 raising skeletal problems and malabsorption. Ross puts starter at 0.95% Ca and 0.50% available P, a 1.9:1 ratio.
  3. Wet litter and dirty eggs. Check salt. Above roughly 0.5% sodium or chloride, birds drink far more and litter goes wet. Check your fish meal for salting and your salt weighing.
  4. Thin or pale shells in layers. Check the limestone particle split before you add more total calcium. Moving from 40:60 to 25:75 fine to coarse is usually the answer in an older flock. Also check total daily calcium: at week 77 and beyond, hens need 4.7 g per bird per day, not the 4.0 g they needed at peak.
  5. Pale combs, ragged feathering, poor pigmentation. Check the premix - its age, its storage, its label, and whether it was pre-blended properly. Suspect rancid cake in the same store.
  6. A sudden production or growth crash across a whole house. Check the feed against the mycotoxin limits. Aflatoxin at 0.005 ppm for chicks and 0.02 ppm for broilers and layers, ochratoxin A at 0.10 ppm for broilers and layers but only 0.01 ppm for breeders, T-2 and HT-2 at 0.25 ppm for all poultry, fumonisins at 10 ppm for chicks and 20 ppm for broilers. Ochratoxin at 0.5 ppm has been associated with 50-75% production decline in layers. Chronic low-level multi-toxin exposure is normal in commercial operations, and effects can be additive or synergistic, so testing for one toxin is not enough.

Now optimisation for cost. Three moves reliably cut cost per tonne without cutting performance.

First, add phytase at 500-1,500 FTU/kg and reduce DCP. At 1,000 FTU/kg you recover roughly 0.15-0.25% available phosphorus, which is about 10-12 kg of DCP per tonne. At $0.85/kg that is $8.50-$10.20 saved against an enzyme cost of $1.20-$2.00. Do not remove all mineral supplementation.

Second, add an NSP enzyme if you have moved to wheat or barley. Energy gains run 96-265 kcal/kg with 3-7% feed efficiency improvement, with the biggest responses on wheat because of arabinoxylans and barley because of beta-glucans. On a maize-based ration expect only modest gains and check the price before buying.

Third, stop overshooting protein. Excess nitrogen above the amino acid requirement is money burned and ammonia produced. If your grower is running at 22% crude protein when NRC says 20%, you are buying nitrogen the bird excretes.

A Ugandan example: a Jinja producer with 1,000 broilers faced a maize price spike and simply pushed bran from 5 kg to 15 kg per 100 kg. FCR moved from 1.78 to 2.05, and the extra 0.5 kg of feed per bird cost more than the maize he saved. Recalculating with 8 kg bran plus 1.5 kg oil brought FCR back to 1.82 at a lower cost per tonne than his original ration. The arithmetic, not the instinct, was what saved him.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which ingredient has the highest lysine digestibility?

Fish meal leads on standardised ileal digestibility of lysine at 90-98%, ahead of soybean meal at 85-95%. This is why small fish meal inclusions carry a starter ration.

2. Why must plant phosphorus not be counted at face value?

Phytic acid binds phosphorus and other minerals. Bioavailability falls 20-50% for phosphorus and 30-60% for zinc, so your table needs separate total and available phosphorus columns.

3. Forgetting which ingredient's calcium most often causes over-calcified broiler starter?

Fish meal carries meaningful calcium. Ignoring it while adding a standard limestone level pushes starter calcium past 1.0%, which interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes to leg problems.

4. Meat and bone meal is listed with high variability of plus or minus 200 kcal/kg because of what?

Rendered products vary enormously with processing temperature, bone-to-soft-tissue ratio and residual fat, which is why book values for meat and bone meal are the least reliable in the table.

5. What sets the maximum inclusion of groundnut cake?

Groundnut cake is limited by aflatoxin contamination risk rather than an inherent anti-nutritional factor. Chicks tolerate only 0.005 ppm, so untested cake must stay out of starter.

6. Why must you reserve space before drawing Pearson's Square?

If 6 kg of a 100 kg batch is oil, limestone, DCP, salt and premix, the other 94 kg must supply all 23 kg of protein. That means blending to 23/0.94 = 24.5%, not to 23%.

7. A blend must reach 24.5% CP from maize at 8.5% and a cake blend at 46.2%. How many parts of maize?

Subtract diagonally: 46.2 - 24.5 = 21.7 parts maize, and 24.5 - 8.5 = 16.0 parts protein blend, for 37.7 parts in total.

8. Which post-square check do home mixers most often fail?

Fish meal carries around 5% calcium. Adding a standard limestone level on top pushes starter calcium past 1.0%, disturbing phosphorus metabolism and causing leg problems.

9. Why is plant phosphorus deliberately excluded from the available phosphorus check?

Counting bound phytate phosphorus as available is a common and expensive error. Ignore it, supply available P from DCP and fish meal, then use phytase to recover part of the plant fraction.

10. The square gives 2,722 kcal/kg against a 2,975 target. What is the standard correction?

Oil is the densest energy source available. Two kilograms per 100 kg adds around 17,600 kcal to the batch, lifting the ration by roughly 176 kcal/kg.

11. A hen eating 90 g a day needs 19.83% crude protein. Why does a hen eating 110 g need only 16.23%?

Layer requirements are daily intakes, not percentages. More feed at a lower concentration delivers the same grams per bird per day, which is what the hen actually needs.

12. Why is coarse limestone used alongside fine limestone in layer feed?

Shell formation happens largely at night. Coarse particles held in the gizzard give a slow calcium release, while fine particles are absorbed quickly and serve bone maintenance.

13. A ration contains 3.90% calcium and the hen eats 110 g a day. What is her daily calcium intake?

110 g x 3.90% = 4.29 g per bird per day, comfortably above the 4.0 g peak requirement in the Hy-Line specification.

14. What should an ageing flock with thin shells get first?

From around week 77 the published guidance moves to 25% fine and 75% coarse limestone. It is the cheapest single intervention for shell quality in an old flock.

15. Why did the Nakuru producer save money by measuring intake?

Formulating to an assumed intake rather than a measured one either shortchanges or overfeeds the flock. Measuring let her cut soya cake by 1.5 kg per 100 kg with no drop in lay.

16. A premix contains 4,000,000 IU vitamin A per kg and is used at 2.5 kg per tonne. What does the feed contain?

4,000,000 x 2.5 = 10,000,000 IU spread over 1,000 kg of feed, giving 10,000 IU/kg. That meets the Ross finisher figure but falls short of 13,000 IU/kg for starter.

17. Why should a farmer not blend his own vitamin premix?

Micro-quantities at milligram and microgram level need laboratory weighing and coated, stabilised vitamin forms. Buy premix; do the pre-blending yourself.

18. What is stepwise dilution used for?

Mixing 300 g into 100 kg directly cannot give even distribution. Diluting into 1 kg, then 5 kg, then spreading that across the batch does.

19. Why are Ross vitamin levels so much higher than NRC 1994 levels?

NRC 1994 ranges mark the point below which clinical deficiency appears. Breeder specifications describe what a high-yield modern bird needs to grow and defend itself, which is considerably higher.

20. Zinc above which level begins to antagonise copper, iron and manganese absorption?

High dietary zinc above roughly 150 mg/kg antagonises the absorption of copper, iron and manganese, so more is not better in a trace mineral premix.

21. Why must coarse limestone be added after grinding?

The value of coarse limestone lies entirely in its particle size, which lets the gizzard hold and slowly release it during night-time shell formation. Milling it turns it into fine limestone.

22. How do you test whether a farm mixer actually mixes?

A marker test is free and definitive. Similar counts across samples mean good uniformity; wildly different counts mean the mixing time is too short or the mixer is overloaded.

23. Why keep a 500 g retained sample from every new ingredient consignment?

Feed-related problems usually appear well after mixing. A dated, labelled retained sample lets you test the actual ingredient rather than guess.

24. When should oil be added during mixing?

Oil added last and slowly coats the dry particles evenly. Added early or dumped in, it forms clumps that never distribute.

25. What does a musty smell in a consignment of cake indicate?

Musty or rancid smells signal mould or oxidised fat. Both damage the feed's vitamin content and raise mycotoxin risk, and no formulation adjustment can correct for them.

26. Replacing 8 kg of maize with 8 kg of maize bran per 100 kg does what to the ration?

Bran carries far less energy than whole maize. Eight kilograms of substitution removes around 13,200 kcal from a 100 kg batch, or 132 kcal per kilogram of feed.

27. Splayed legs and twisted hocks in young broilers should send you first to which figure?

Starter calcium above 1.0% interferes with phosphorus metabolism and predisposes birds to tibial dyschondroplasia and angular limb deformities. Ross specifies 0.95% Ca with 0.50% available P.

28. Wet litter and dirty eggs most often trace back to what formulation error?

Excess salt drives water consumption and wet litter. Check both your salt weighing and whether your fish meal has been salted or adulterated.

29. Why is testing a feed for a single mycotoxin insufficient?

Commercial feeds commonly carry several mycotoxins at low levels. Their combined effect can equal or exceed the sum of the individual effects, so a comprehensive panel is recommended.

30. Which cost-cutting move reliably works on a maize-based ration?

Phytase at around 1,000 FTU/kg recovers 0.15-0.25% available phosphorus for roughly $1.20-$2.00 against a DCP saving of $8.50-$10.20. NSP enzymes give only modest responses on maize.

Module 6 capstone

Formulate, mix and prove one complete ration. Week 1: build your own ingredient table with at least ten locally available ingredients, recording for each one the delivered price per kilogram, the crude protein you believe it carries and where that figure came from - a supplier analysis, a published table, or a guess you have marked as a guess. Week 2: use Pearson's Square to formulate a 100 kg batch for the bird you actually keep, then run the four checks in Lesson 2 - crude protein, metabolisable energy, calcium and available phosphorus - and adjust until each one is inside the published band. Week 3: mix one 100 kg batch by hand or machine, timing the mix, recording the order of addition and the pre-blending steps for premix, salt and methionine, then run a simple mixer uniformity check using cracked maize or coloured salt. Week 4: feed the batch, weigh 20 birds before and after, record feed used and calculate the actual feed conversion and cost per kilogram of gain. Submit the mix sheet, the four nutrient checks with your working shown, the batch record and the measured result, and state one change you will make to the next batch and why.
