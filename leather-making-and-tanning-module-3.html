1. Chemistry of Tanning

Tanning is the chemical process that turns raw animal skin into stable leather. Without tanning, skin would rot, harden, and crack. The key is collagen, a protein in the skin. Tanning agents bind to collagen, making it resistant to bacteria, heat, and water. In Africa, you'll work with two main types: vegetable tannins from plants like acacia (wattle) or mimosa, and chrome salts (basic chromium sulfate). Vegetable tannins are large molecules that hydrogen-bond with collagen. Chrome tanning uses chromium ions that form cross-links between collagen fibers. Both stabilize the skin, but chrome is faster and gives softer leather; vegetable tanning is slower and yields firmer, more breathable leather.

The chemistry matters because it affects your process. For vegetable tanning, you need a liquor with tannin concentration measured in degrees Barkometer (Â°Bk). Start at 2-4 Â°Bk and gradually increase to 20-30 Â°Bk over several days. For chrome tanning, you control pH and temperature. Chrome works best at pH 2.8-3.2 and 30-40Â°C. You'll use a drum to agitate the skins in the tanning solution. The drum's mechanical action helps the tanning agent penetrate. Without proper chemistry, you get uneven tanning, which leads to stiff or rotten spots.

In Africa, many small tanneries use vegetable tanning because tannins are locally available. For example, in Nigeria, you can extract tannins from Acacia nilotica pods. In South Africa, wattle bark is common. Chrome tanning requires imported chemicals, which can be expensive, but it's faster and often used for shoe upper leather. Understanding the chemistry helps you choose the right method and troubleshoot problems. If tanning fails, the leather may feel hard or have a raw smell. Always test pH with litmus paper or a pH meter. Temperature control is crucial: too hot, and the skin may shrink; too cold, and tanning slows down.

Remember, tanning is not just soaking. It's a chemical reaction that needs time, correct concentration, and proper conditions. Start with clean, delimed skins. For vegetable tanning, begin with a weak liquor and strengthen it gradually. For chrome, use a proper recipe: 8-10% chrome powder based on skin weight, plus salt and acid. Always wear gloves and gogglesâchrome is toxic. With practice, you'll learn to read the leather: when it's tanned, it turns a uniform color and feels firm but flexible. That's the chemistry working.

2. Vegetable Tanning Process

Vegetable tanning is the oldest method, using natural tannins from tree bark, leaves, or pods. In Africa, common sources include wattle (Acacia mearnsii) bark, mimosa (Acacia dealbata), and tara pods. This process takes days to weeks but produces leather that is firm, durable, and ideal for saddles, belts, and bags. You'll need a tanning pit or drum, tannin liquor, and clean water. Start with delimed and bated skinsâthey should be soft and swollen. If you're using pits, you'll suspend skins in a series of pits with increasing tannin concentration. If using a drum, you'll rotate skins in the liquor.

First, prepare the tannin liquor. For bark, grind it into small pieces and soak in water. The ratio is about 1 kg of bark to 10 liters of water. Let it steep for 24 hours, then strain. Measure concentration with a Barkometer. Start with a weak liquor of 2-4 Â°Bk. Place skins in the liquor and agitate regularly. Every day, add more tannin extract to raise concentration by 2-3 Â°Bk. After 7-10 days, you should reach 20-30 Â°Bk. The skins will gradually change color from white to tan to brown. They should feel firmer. Total tanning time: 2-4 weeks for thin skins, up to 3 months for thick hides.

In Africa, many tanneries use a combination of pits and drums. For example, in Ethiopia, sheepskins are tanned in pits with wattle bark. In Nigeria, goat skins are tanned in drums with mimosa extract. The key is patience: rushing leads to uneven tanning. Always keep skins submerged and move them to avoid dark spots. Temperature should be 20-30Â°C. If it's too hot, tannins may precipitate; too cold, tanning slows. You can add a little acetic acid to keep tannins soluble. After tanning, the leather is washed and dried slowly in shade to prevent cracking.

Vegetable-tanned leather is biodegradable and develops a rich patina over time. It's perfect for crafts and tools. However, it can be stiff, so you may need to fatliquor it with oils like neatsfoot or cod liver oil. To test if tanning is complete, cut a small piece and boil itâif it curls but doesn't dissolve, it's tanned. Also, check the color: it should be uniform all the way through. If the center is still white, it needs more time. With practice, you'll produce high-quality leather using local materials. This method is sustainable and supports local farmers who harvest bark and pods.

3. Chrome Tanning Process

Chrome tanning is the most common method worldwide, producing soft, durable leather for shoes, garments, and upholstery. It uses basic chromium sulfate, a blue-green powder. Chrome tanning is fastâoften completed in 8-24 hoursâbut requires careful control of pH, temperature, and chemical ratios. In Africa, many commercial tanneries use chrome because it's efficient and yields consistent results. However, chrome is toxic, so you must wear protective gear and dispose of waste properly. The process starts with pickled skins: after deliming and bating, skins are treated with acid and salt to lower pH to about 2.5-3.0. This prepares them for chrome uptake.

You'll need a drum, chrome powder (8-10% of skin weight), salt (5-8%), and a basifying agent like sodium bicarbonate. First, dissolve chrome powder in water at 30-35Â°C. Add salt to prevent acid swelling. Place skins in the drum and run for 30 minutes. Then add the chrome solution and drum for 1-2 hours. The pH should be around 2.8-3.2. Next, slowly add sodium bicarbonate to raise pH to 3.8-4.0 over 2-3 hours. This fixes the chrome to the collagen. Drum for another 2-4 hours, then let skins rest overnight. Total drum time: 6-12 hours. Check penetration by cutting a small piece and testing with a chrome indicatorâit should be green throughout.

Temperature is critical: keep it between 30-40Â°C. If too hot, chrome binds too fast on the surface, leaving the center untanned. If too cold, penetration is slow. The drum speed should be 8-12 rpm. After tanning, skins are piled to drain, then sammed (stretched) and dried. Chrome-tanned leather is light blue (wet blue) and can be shaved to uniform thickness. It's then neutralized, dyed, and fatliquored. In Africa, chrome tanning is used for export leather, but small tanneries can also do it with proper training. Always test the float (liquor) for chrome exhaustionâif the float is clear, the chrome is exhausted. If colored, add more chrome or adjust pH.

Safety first: chrome dust is harmful. Wear a mask, gloves, and goggles. Work in a ventilated area. Dispose of chrome waste according to local regulationsânever pour it into rivers. Despite the risks, chrome tanning is valuable because it produces leather that is soft, water-resistant, and takes dyes well. With practice, you'll master the timing and chemical additions. Remember, chrome tanning is a balance: too much acid, and chrome won't bind; too much alkali, and it binds too fast. Keep records of your recipe and adjust based on results.

4. Alternative Tanning Methods

Not all tanneries use chrome or vegetable tannins. Alternative methods include brain tanning, alum tanning, and synthetic tanning (syntans). These are useful in Africa where resources may be limited or where you want to avoid chemicals. Brain tanning uses animal brainsâyes, brains! The lecithin in brains emulsifies fats and conditions the skin. It's a traditional method used by many indigenous cultures. To brain tan, you need one brain per skin (deer, goat, or sheep). Mash the brain in warm water to make a paste. After softening the skin by working it, apply the brain paste and work it in. Then smoke the skin over a smoldering fire to fix the tannins. The result is soft, breathable leather, but it's not water-resistant. This method is labor-intensive but uses local materials.

Alum tanning uses aluminum salts (potash alum) and salt. It's a quick method, often used for fur tanning. Mix 1 part alum to 1 part salt in water. Soak skins for 24-48 hours. Alum-tanned leather is white, soft, and easy to dye, but it's not as durable as chrome or vegetable-tanned. It can be used for gloves, garments, and linings. In Africa, alum is available in markets. Another alternative is syntan tanning, which uses synthetic tanning agents (like phenolic syntans). These are often used in combination with chrome or vegetable tannins to fill and soften leather. Syntans are powders that dissolve in water. Use 5-10% based on skin weight. They work at pH 4-5 and 40Â°C. Syntans are good for making light-colored leather.

For small-scale tanneries, combination tanning is common: e.g., vegetable then chrome (semi-chrome) to get the best of both. Or vegetable then syntan. In Africa, many artisans use vegetable tanning for crafts and chrome for shoe upper. Alternative methods are also good for sustainability. For example, tanning with cassava starch or tannins from local plants like moringa or baobab. Research your local plants: many contain tannins. You can test by soaking plant material and seeing if it turns hide brown. Always test small pieces first.

Alternative tanning can be less toxic and more affordable. But they have limitations: brain-tanned leather can stiffen if washed; alum-tanned may not be washable. So choose based on the final product. For a belt, vegetable-tanned is best. For a soft bag, chrome or alum. For a traditional shield, brain tan. With practice, you can master these methods and even invent your own combinations. The key is to understand the chemistry: tannins bind to collagen, and each method has its own binding mechanism. Keep notes and experiment. In Africa, innovation in tanning is growing, with tanneries using local materials like cassava peel or sorghum bran to reduce costs and waste.

5. Tanning Drum Operation

A tanning drum is a rotating cylinder that mixes skins with tanning liquor. It's essential for even tanning and faster processing. In Africa, drums range from small 1-meter diameter units for artisans to large 3-meter industrial drums. The drum has a door for loading and unloading, and internal paddles or shelves to lift and turn the skins. Drum speed is critical: too fast, and skins tangle; too slow, and mixing is poor. Typical speed is 8-12 revolutions per minute (rpm). For chrome tanning, use 10-12 rpm; for vegetable tanning, 6-8 rpm. The drum should be loaded to about 30-40% of its volume with skins and liquor. The liquor ratio (water to skin weight) is usually 1:1 to 2:1 for chrome, and 3:1 to 5:1 for vegetable tanning.

Before loading, check the drum for cleanlinessâresidue from previous batches can ruin the leather. Load skins carefully, avoiding folds. Add the tanning solution and close the door tightly. Start the drum and run for the required time. For chrome tanning, you'll run for 1-2 hours, then add basifying agents and run for another 2-4 hours. For vegetable tanning, you'll run for several hours per day, then let skins rest in the liquor overnight. Always monitor temperature: use a thermometer inside the drum or check the liquor. If it gets too hot, add cool water. If too cold, warm the liquor. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can be high, you may need to cool the drum or work in the early morning.

Safety is paramount. Never reach into a moving drum. Use a lockout/tagout system. Wear gloves and goggles when handling chemicals. After tanning, drain the liquor and unload skins. The drum should be cleaned with water and a mild detergent. Check the drum's bearings and motor regularlyâmaintenance prevents breakdowns. In small tanneries, drums are often homemade from plastic barrels or steel tanks. Ensure they are sturdy and have a reliable motor. A well-operated drum can process 50-100 skins per batch. Keep records of drum times, temperatures, and chemical additions. This helps you replicate success.

Troubleshooting: if skins are unevenly tanned, check drum speed and load size. If skins are tangled, reduce speed or load. If the drum leaks, replace seals. In Africa, many tanneries use second-hand drums from Europe or Asia. Inspect them for cracks. With proper operation, a drum can last for years. Remember, the drum is not just a mixerâit's a reactor. The mechanical action helps chemicals penetrate. So treat it with care and maintain it well. A good drum operator can produce consistent, high-quality leather.

6. Testing for Tannage Completion

How do you know when tanning is done? Testing is crucial to avoid under-tanned leather, which will rot, or over-tanned leather, which may be brittle. The simplest test is a visual and tactile check. For vegetable-tanned leather, cut a small piece from the thickest part (e.g., neck or butt). The color should be uniform throughoutâno white center. If you see a white line in the middle, tanning is incomplete. The leather should feel firm but flexible, not spongy. For chrome-tanned leather, the color should be a uniform light blue-green. You can also do a boil test: place a small piece in boiling water for 5 minutes. If it curls but doesn't dissolve, it's tanned. If it dissolves or turns to jelly, it's not tanned. This test works for both vegetable and chrome, but chrome-tanned may shrink more.

Another test is the shrinkage temperature. Tanned leather shrinks at a higher temperature than raw skin. For vegetable-tanned, shrinkage occurs at 70-80Â°C; for chrome-tanned, at 100-120Â°C. You can test by heating a small piece in water and noting when it shrinks. But this requires a thermometer. A simpler field test: soak a piece in water for 30 minutes. If it becomes slippery or slimy, it's not fully tanned. If it remains firm, it's tanned. Also, check the pH of the leather. For chrome-tanned, the pH should be 3.5-4.0. For vegetable-tanned, 4.0-5.0. Use a pH meter or litmus paper. If pH is too low, the leather may be acidic and deteriorate; if too high, it may be unstable.

For chrome tanning, you can use a chrome indicator solution (diphenylcarbazide) to check penetration. Cut a piece and apply a dropâif it turns purple, chrome is present. If the center is not purple, chrome hasn't penetrated. For vegetable tanning, you can use a ferric chloride test: a drop turns blue-black if tannins are present. These tests are quick and can be done in the tannery. In Africa, many tanneries use these simple methods. Always test a representative sample from each batch. If tanning is incomplete, return the skins to the drum or pit for more time. For chrome, add more chrome and basify; for vegetable, increase tannin concentration.

Remember, testing saves money and reputation. Under-tanned leather will smell bad and crack. Over-tanned leather is stiff and hard to work. So test early and often. Keep a log of your tests and results. With experience, you'll develop a feel for when leather is done. But never skip the tests. In small-scale tanneries, a common mistake is to assume tanning is complete after a fixed time. Instead, test each batch. The extra 10 minutes can save a whole batch. Also, test after drying: sometimes leather looks tanned when wet but shows white spots when dry. So test both wet and dry. Finally, get feedback from your customersâthey will tell you if the leather wears well. Use that to improve your process.

Practical Reference: Tanning Fundamentals for African Leather Enterprises

It is written for small and medium leather businesses â from Kano and Fez to Nairobi, Addis Ababa, and Bulawayo â that need reliable, repeatable tannage on locally available hides and skins. The goal is simple: produce leather that is stable, sellable, and consistent enough to win contracts with shoemakers, bag manufacturers, upholstery buyers, and export agents.

Tanning converts raw hide protein (collagen) into a stable material resistant to microbial attack, heat, and moisture. Whether you use vegetable tannins, chrome salts, or alternative agents, the commercial outcome depends on control: pH, float ratio, drum speed, time, and testing. This lesson gives you the operating parameters and verification steps that separate a profitable tannery from one that loses money to rejected lots.

Chemistry You Can Control on the Floor

Collagen fibres are held together by hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions. Tanning agents cross-link these fibres, increasing hydrothermal stability. Vegetable tannins (polyphenols) form hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions; chrome (basic chromium sulphate) forms coordinate covalent bonds. The measurable outcome is shrinkage temperature (Ts): untanned hide ~60Â°C, vegetable-tanned ~70â85Â°C, chrome-tanned ~100â120Â°C.

  • Float ratio: 0.5â1.0 for chrome (high exhaust), 1.0â2.0 for vegetable.
  • Temperature: 30â40Â°C accelerates chrome; vegetable tannage often 25â35Â°C.
  • Mechanical action: Drum speed 8â12 rpm; too fast causes grain damage, too slow causes uneven penetration.

Vegetable Tanning: The African Advantage

Africa has abundant tannin sources: Acacia mearnsii (wattle) bark in Kenya, Tanzania, and South Africa; Acacia nilotica pods across the Sahel; mangrove bark in West Africa; and sumac in North Africa. Vegetable tanning suits sole leather, saddlery, belts, and traditional crafts, and commands premium prices in niche markets.

Chrome Tanning: Speed and Global Market Access

About 85% of global leather is chrome-tanned because it is fast (8â24 hours), produces soft, heat-resistant leather, and is compatible with modern finishing. For African tanneries supplying footwear and automotive markets, chrome remains the default. Basic chromium sulphate (BCS) at 6â8% on pelt weight, with 1â2% sodium formate and 0.5â1% sodium bicarbonate for basification, gives reliable results.

Alternative Tanning Methods

Alternatives reduce chrome use or serve specific markets:

  • Aluminium/zirconium (wet-white): Low chrome or chrome-free; good for automotive and children's leather.
  • Synthetic tannins (synektan): Often blended with vegetable or chrome to improve fullness and lightfastness.
  • Oxazolidine and organic tanning: Emerging chrome-free routes; higher cost but growing demand in the EU.
  • Smoke and brain tanning: Traditional methods still used for cultural products; not scalable but valuable for heritage markets.

Drum Operation Essentials

The drum is your reactor. Keep a log for every batch: hide weight, float, chemicals, pH at start/middle/end, temperature, drum speed, and time. Clean drums between incompatible processes (chrome residue contaminates vegetable tanning). Use stainless steel or plastic drums where possible; mild steel corrodes and stains leather.

Testing for Tannage Completion

Do not ship untested leather. Use these field tests:

  • Boil test: A small sample boiled in water for 2 minutes should not shrink or curl for chrome-tanned leather.
  • Shrinkage temperature: Raise water temperature slowly; note when sample shrinks. Chrome >100Â°C, vegetable 70â85Â°C.
  • Dye penetration: Cut cross-section; dye should penetrate uniformly.
  • Feel and plumpness: Fully tanned leather feels firm, plump, and uniform.
  • pH of leather: 3.5â4.5 for chrome, 4.0â5.0 for vegetable.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh accurately. All chemical doses are based on pelt weight, not raw hide weight. Re-weigh after deliming.
  2. Check pH at three points: start, middle, and end of tannage. Log every reading.
  3. Control temperature. Keep drums within 25â40Â°C; use warm water in cold seasons.
  4. Run the boil test on every batch before unloading. If it fails, extend tannage or add tannin.
  5. Clean drums between incompatible processes. Chrome residue ruins vegetable leather.
  6. Wear PPE always. Gloves, goggles, respirator for chrome; face shield for lime.
  7. Treat spent floats. Recover chrome; neutralise lime before discharge.
  8. Record everything. Batch log enables traceability, which buyers increasingly demand.
