1. Types of Salt and Their Uses in Preservation

Salt preserves food by drawing out moisture, which bacteria and mold need to grow. Not all salt is the same. In Africa, you will find three main types: fine table salt, coarse sea salt, and rock salt. Fine table salt often has iodine and anti-caking agents. Iodine is good for health but can darken cured meat over time. Anti-caking agents can make brine cloudy but do not affect safety. Coarse sea salt is ideal for dry salting because its large crystals dissolve slowly and draw moisture evenly. Rock salt is the cheapest and works well for long brining, but it may contain dirtrinse it first. Avoid using salt substitutes or low-sodium salt; they do not preserve properly. For dry salting fish, use 1 part salt to 3 parts fish by weight. For wet brining meat, use 1 cup of salt per 4 liters of water. If you are unsure, taste the brine: it should be as salty as seawater. Always store salt in a dry container; moisture makes it clump and lose effectiveness. In coastal areas, you can make sea salt by evaporating seawater, but it must be clean and free of oil. Never use salt that has been mixed with spices or herbs for long-term preservation unless you know the exact salt content. Start with plain salt, then add spices later if you want. Remember: salt does not kill bacteria; it only stops them from growing. So keep your tools, hands, and containers very clean.

2. Dry Salting Fish: Step-by-Step

Dry salting is the oldest and cheapest way to preserve fish. It works best with small to medium fish like tilapia, mackerel, or catfish. First, clean the fish: remove scales, guts, and gills. Do not wash with fresh water after cleaningjust wipe with a clean cloth. Fresh water can introduce bacteria. Next, make a salt mix: 1 kilogram of coarse salt for every 3 kilograms of fish. You can add 1 teaspoon of ground pepper or dried chili per kilogram to keep flies away. Choose a clean container: a plastic bucket, a wooden box, or a clay pot. Put a layer of salt at the bottom. Then place a layer of fish, skin side down. Cover completely with salt. Repeat layers until the container is full, finishing with a thick salt layer. Put a clean weight (a heavy stone wrapped in plastic) on top to press the fish. Keep the container in a cool, shaded place. After 24 hours, liquid will start to come out. Drain this liquid every day for 3 days. After 3 days, remove the fish and rub off excess salt. You can now sun-dry the fish on a rack for 2-3 days until it is hard and brittle. If it bends without breaking, it needs more drying. Store the dried fish in airtight bags or containers. In humid areas, add a small bag of silica gel or dry rice to absorb moisture. Properly dry-salted fish can last 6 months or more. Always check for mold or bad smell before eating.

3. Wet Brining for Meat and Poultry

Wet brining uses a saltwater solution to preserve and flavor meat and poultry. It is faster than dry salting and keeps meat moist. For 5 kilograms of meat or chicken, make a brine with 4 liters of clean water and 1 kilogram of coarse salt. You can add 500 grams of sugar to balance the saltiness and help browning. Optional: 10 cloves of crushed garlic, 2 tablespoons of black pepper, and 5 bay leaves. Stir until salt and sugar dissolve completely. Use a food-grade plastic bucket or a large clay pot. Place the meat in the brine. Make sure it is fully covered; weigh it down with a clean plate. Keep the container in a refrigerator or a very cool place (below 4C). If you have no fridge, use a cooler with ice packs and change them twice a day. Brining times: chicken pieces 4-6 hours, whole chicken 12-24 hours, pork or beef 2-3 days. After brining, remove the meat and rinse it with clean water. You can now cook it immediately, or smoke it for longer storage. For smoking, hang the meat in a smokehouse for 8-12 hours using hardwood (not softwood like pine). The brine adds about 2-3% salt to the meat, which slows spoilage. Do not reuse brine; it contains bacteria from the raw meat. If you want to store brined meat without cooking, keep it in the fridge for up to 5 days. For longer storage, you must dry or smoke it. Always test the brine: a raw egg should float in a strong brine. If it sinks, add more salt.

4. Salting Vegetables for Long Storage

Salting vegetables is a great way to store surplus harvests without a refrigerator. It works best with cabbage, carrots, green beans, and leafy greens like kale or spinach. First, wash and chop the vegetables into small pieces. For every 1 kilogram of vegetables, use 200 grams of coarse salt (about 1 cup). In a large bowl, mix the vegetables and salt thoroughly. Let them sit for 2 hours. The salt will draw out water. After 2 hours, pack the mixture tightly into clean glass jars or clay pots. Press down hard with a wooden spoon to remove air bubbles. The vegetables should be covered by their own salty juice. If not, add a little extra brine (1 tablespoon salt per cup of water). Leave 2 centimeters of space at the top. Seal the jar loosely and keep it in a cool, dark place. After 3 days, check for bubblesthis is normal fermentation. Skim off any white film that forms on top. After 2 weeks, the salted vegetables are ready to eat. They will keep for 6 months or more. For longer storage, you can dry the salted vegetables in the sun for 2 days, then store in airtight bags. Always use non-iodized salt for fermenting; iodine can stop the good bacteria. If you see fuzzy mold (not white film), throw the batch away. Salted vegetables are rich in probiotics and vitamin C. Use them in soups, stews, or as a side dish. Start with small batches to learn the process.

5. Controlling Salt Levels for Taste and Safety

Salt is a preservative, but too much makes food inedible and too little allows spoilage. The safe range for preservation is 15-20% salt by weight of the food. For dry salting, that means 150-200 grams of salt per kilogram of fish or meat. For brining, a 20% brine means 200 grams of salt per liter of water. You can measure salt accurately using a kitchen scale or a measuring cup: 1 cup of coarse salt weighs about 250 grams. If you do not have a scale, use the egg test: a fresh egg floats in a 10% brine (100 grams per liter). For preservation, you need a stronger brinethe egg should float with a coin-sized area above water. Taste is personal, but safety comes first. After salting, you can reduce saltiness by soaking the product in fresh water for a few hours before cooking. For fish, soak for 2-3 hours; for meat, 4-6 hours. Change the water every hour. Never reduce salt below 10% for long-term storage. In hot, humid climates, use the higher end (20%) because bacteria grow faster. If you are selling, label the salt content clearly. Some customers may prefer less salt, but you must warn them that it will not keep as long. Always store salted products in a cool, dry place. If you see mold, slime, or smell ammonia, discard the product. Salt does not kill bacteria; it only slows them. So keep everything clean. Test your salt levels with a salinometer if you can afford one; otherwise, use the egg test and your taste buds.

6. Packaging and Selling Salted Products

Packaging protects your salted fish, meat, or vegetables from moisture, insects, and dirt. It also makes your product look professional and helps you sell more. For dried salted fish, use clear plastic bags or vacuum-sealed bags. If you do not have a vacuum sealer, squeeze out as much air as possible before tying the bag. You can also wrap fish in clean paper and then in a plastic bag. For salted meat, use breathable cotton bags or paper bagsplastic can trap moisture and cause mold. For salted vegetables, use glass jars with tight lids. Always label your products with the name, date, and salt level. For example: 'Salted Tilapia 18% salt Best before Dec 2025'. This builds trust with customers. Pricing: calculate your costsfish, salt, packaging, and your time. A good rule is to sell at 2-3 times your cost. In local markets, salted fish sells by the piece or by weight. A 500-gram bag of dried fish might sell for 5-10 USD depending on the region. For online sales, take clear photos in natural light. Show the texture and color. Use social media like WhatsApp, Facebook, or Instagram to reach urban customers. Offer samples at markets. Store your products in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. If you are selling in bulk, use food-grade buckets with lids. Always check for mold or insects before selling. Return or refund any product that spoils within the promised time. Good packaging and honest labeling will make customers come back. Start small, then scale up as you learn what sells best.

<h2>Practical Reference: Salting and Brining for Fish and Meat</h2>

<p>Salt is the oldest and cheapest food preservative available to African agribusinesses. It draws moisture out of fish, meat, poultry and vegetables, creating an environment where spoilage bacteria and moulds cannot multiply. For small and medium enterprises (SMEs) along the coast, around lakes and in inland livestock areas, salting and brining turn perishable surpluses into products that can be stored for months, transported to distant markets and sold at a higher margin than fresh produce.</p>

<p>This practical reference lesson gives you the working knowledge to salt and brine safely, control quality, and package products for sale. It complements the earlier lessons in this module: Types of Salt and Their Uses in Preservation, Dry Salting Fish: Step-by-Step, Wet Brining for Meat and Poultry, Salting Vegetables for Long Storage, Controlling Salt Levels for Taste and Safety, and Packaging and Selling Salted Products.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING NITRITE CURING SALTS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Nitrite curing salt (Prague powder, pink salt) is <strong>toxic if used like ordinary salt</strong>. Never use it as table salt or in unmeasured quantities. Store it in a clearly labelled container, away from food preparation areas, and keep it out of reach of children and untrained staff. In Africa, incidents of nitrite poisoning from home curing are rare but serious where they occur. If you are not trained in meat curing, use ordinary salt and refrigeration instead. For any cured meat product, follow the exact nitrite concentration allowed by your national standards body (e.g. KEBS, SON, NAFDAC, Ghana Standards Authority).</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Source clean salt:</strong> buy food-grade salt from a licensed supplier and check for anti-caking agents that slow curing.</li>
<li><strong>Use clean water and containers:</strong> brine must be made with potable water; use food-grade plastic, glass or stainless steel, never rusty metal.</li>
<li><strong>Measure brine strength:</strong> use a salometer or a hydrometer; do not guess. Record the reading for every batch.</li>
<li><strong>Keep temperatures low:</strong> brine and cure below 5C where possible, or use high-salt dry curing in hot climates.</li>
<li><strong>Control time:</strong> under-salting or over-salting both lose money. Follow the times in Table 2 and adjust for fish or cut size.</li>
<li><strong>Dry and package correctly:</strong> dry salted products to 2025% moisture loss, then vacuum-seal or pack in clean, labelled bags.</li>
<li><strong>Label and date every batch:</strong> include product name, salt level, production date, best-before date and your business contact.</li>
<li><strong>Test your market:</strong> run a small taste panel with local customers before scaling up; saltiness preferences differ across regions.</li>
</ol>
