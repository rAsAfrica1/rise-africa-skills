Lessons

1. How to read a buyer's specification sheet

A buyer's specification sheet is a document that lists all the requirements your product must meet. It often looks like a table with columns for parameter, unit, and limit (e.g., moisture content max 8%). First, identify the product name and the buyer's reference code. Then, scan for mandatory parameters like moisture, foreign matter, and defect percentage. For example, a European coffee buyer might require moisture 12%, defects 5%, and no live insects. Next, check for testing methods: they might specify ISO 6673 for moisture. You must use a lab that follows that method. Also, look for packaging and labeling details: bag size (e.g., 60 kg jute bags), labeling language (English/French), and batch numbering. If something is unclear, don't guessemail the buyer for clarification. Many African exporters lose deals because they assume they know the spec. A good practice is to create a checklist from the spec sheet and tick each item before shipment. For shea butter, a French buyer might require free fatty acid 0.5%, peroxide value 5 meq/kg, and no hexane residues. You can test at labs like SGS or Bureau Veritas in Accra or Nairobi. Always keep a copy of the spec sheet and your test reports. If you cannot meet a parameter, tell the buyer early and propose an alternative. Remember, the spec sheet is a contract: if you ship non-compliant goods, they can reject the whole container. So read it line by line, and ask questions. That is how you build trust and get repeat orders.

2. Private standards vs. public regulations: what buyers demand

Public regulations are laws set by governments, like the EU's maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides. Private standards are rules set by buyers, industry groups, or NGOs. Examples include GlobalG.A.P. for fruits and vegetables, Fairtrade for cocoa, and Rainforest Alliance for coffee. Buyers often demand private standards even if public regulations are met. For instance, a German coffee buyer may require Rainforest Alliance certification, which covers environmental and social criteria. Public regulations are mandatory; private standards are voluntary but become mandatory if you want that buyer. In Africa, many exporters struggle because they only focus on public rules. To find out what buyers want, ask for their supplier manual or code of conduct. These documents list requirements like no child labor, traceability, and pesticide lists. For shea butter, a French buyer might demand organic certification (EU organic) and fair trade. For coffee, a German buyer might require 4C certification (Common Code for the Coffee Community). The cost of certification can be high: GlobalG.A.P. inspection can cost $1,000$3,000. But it opens doors to premium prices. Some buyers offer to share costs or provide training. Always check if the standard is recognized in your country. For example, Kenya has a national organic standard. You can also join cooperatives to share certification costs. Remember, private standards often exceed public ones. If you meet them, you can negotiate better prices. But don't over-promise: if you claim to be organic, you need annual inspections. Start with one standard at a time. Use free online tools like the ITC Standards Map to compare requirements. That helps you decide which standard is worth the investment.

3. Using trade portals (ITC, AfCFTA) to find standards for your product

Trade portals are free websites that help you find market requirements. The International Trade Centre (ITC) has two key tools: the Standards Map and the Market Access Map. Go to standardsmap.org. Type your product, e.g., 'shea butter', and select target market 'France'. The tool shows public and private standards you must meet. It lists mandatory regulations like EU cosmetics regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 and private standards like Ecocert. You can also see which labs are recognized. The Market Access Map (macmap.org) shows tariffs and rules of origin. For African exporters, the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) portal (afcfta.au.int) provides information on standards across African countries. For example, if you want to export coffee to Ghana, you can check Ghana's standards. The ITC also has a 'Trade Map' for market trends. To use these portals, you need a computer or smartphone and internet. Many African trade promotion organizations (TPOs) offer free training on these tools. For instance, the Kenya Export Promotion and Branding Agency runs workshops. Start by creating a free account on ITC's tools. Then, search for your product and destination. Download the standards report as PDF. Look for 'mandatory' vs 'voluntary' standards. Also, check the 'quality infrastructure' section to find local labs and certification bodies. If you cannot find your product, use broader terms like 'vegetable oil' or 'coffee, green'. The portals also list contact points for technical barriers to trade (TBT). If a standard seems impossible, you can ask for help from the TBT enquiry point in your country. Use these portals before you produce, not after. That saves money and time. Many African exporters have used ITC tools to win contracts. For example, a Ghanaian shea butter exporter used Standards Map to get Ecocert certification and now sells to France. So make it a habit: check the portal first.

4. Case study: exporting shea butter to France

Awa is a shea butter producer in Burkina Faso. She wants to export to France. She contacts a French buyer who sends a specification sheet. The buyer requires: free fatty acid (FFA) 0.5%, peroxide value 5 meq/kg, moisture 0.2%, and no hexane. Also, the buyer demands organic certification (EU organic) and fair trade (Fair for Life). Awa's current butter has FFA of 1.2% and moisture 0.5%. She cannot meet the spec. She asks the buyer for time and advice. The buyer suggests she work with a local lab, like Laboratoire National de Sant Publique in Ouagadougou, to test and adjust. Awa invests in a better roaster and filter. She also joins a cooperative to share the cost of organic certification. The certification process takes 12 months and costs about $2,000. She gets a loan from a microfinance bank. After 8 months, her FFA drops to 0.4% and moisture to 0.15%. She sends samples to the buyer. The buyer tests and approves. Awa then negotiates a price: $4.50 per kg for organic fair trade shea butter, compared to $2.80 for conventional. She ships 5 tons in 25 kg cartons. The buyer pays 30% advance. Awa learns that meeting standards is an investment but pays off. She also uses ITC Standards Map to check for any new French regulations. Now she exports 20 tons per year. Her advice: start small, test often, and communicate with your buyer. Don't hide problems. French buyers value transparency. Also, she got help from the Burkina Faso Export Promotion Agency. They provided a grant for half the certification cost. So, if you want to export shea butter to France, check the spec, test your product, and get certified. It is possible.

5. Case study: exporting coffee to Germany

Kofi is a coffee farmer in Ethiopia. He wants to export green coffee to Germany. A German buyer requires: moisture 12%, defects 5%, and no primary defects (black beans, sour beans). Also, the buyer demands 4C certification (Common Code for the Coffee Community) and EUDR compliance (EU Deforestation Regulation). Kofi's coffee has 13% moisture and 8% defects. He cannot meet the spec. He asks the buyer for guidance. The buyer sends a supplier manual and offers to connect him with a local exporter who can help. Kofi joins a cooperative that has a wet mill. They improve drying beds and sorting. After 3 months, moisture is 11.5% and defects 4%. He applies for 4C certification through a local body. The cost is $500, shared by the cooperative. For EUDR, he must provide geolocation of his farm. He uses a smartphone app to map his plot. The buyer sends a field officer to verify. After 6 months, Kofi is ready. He negotiates a price: $3.20 per kg for 4C coffee, compared to $2.50 for non-certified. He ships 10 tons in 60 kg jute bags. The buyer pays 50% advance. Kofi learns that German buyers care about traceability and sustainability. He also uses the ITC Standards Map to check for any new German rules. Now he exports 30 tons per year. His advice: work with a cooperative to share costs, use free mapping tools, and keep records. Also, he got help from the Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority. They provided training on EUDR. So, if you want to export coffee to Germany, check the spec, improve quality, and get certified. It is hard but doable.

6. Negotiating standards with buyers without losing the deal

Sometimes a buyer's standard is too expensive or impossible for you right now. You don't have to say yes or no immediately. You can negotiate. First, understand the buyer's real need. Ask: 'Is this standard mandatory for your market, or can we phase it in?' For example, a French buyer might require organic certification, but you can propose to start with conventional and transition over 12 months. Second, offer alternatives. If they want a specific pesticide limit, show them test results from a local lab that meet the limit using a different method. Third, be transparent about costs and timelines. Say: 'I can meet this standard in 6 months if you pay 20% advance.' Many buyers will share costs if you show commitment. Fourth, use a phased approach. Propose a trial shipment of 1 ton that meets most standards, with a plan to meet the rest. Fifth, get help from trade support institutions. In Africa, organizations like the African Export-Import Bank (Afreximbank) or national TPOs can provide grants for certification. Sixth, never promise what you cannot deliver. If you fail, you lose the buyer and your reputation. Seventh, put the agreement in writing. Email is fine. State what you will do and by when. Eighth, if the buyer insists on an impossible standard, walk away politely. There are other buyers. For example, a Kenyan exporter of green beans was asked for GlobalG.A.P. but couldn't afford it. She negotiated with a UK buyer to accept a local standard for the first year, then GlobalG.A.P. in year two. The buyer agreed. She got the deal. So, negotiation is about finding a win-win. Be honest, be flexible, and know your limits. That is how you keep the deal and build a long-term relationship.

<h2>Practical Reference: Negotiating Standards with Buyers Without Losing the Deal</h2>

<p>For most African exporters, the moment of truth is not the harvest it is the email from a buyer in Rotterdam, Paris, or Hamburg that says: "Please confirm compliance with our supplier standard before we can issue the purchase order." At that point, the deal is not won or lost on price. It is won or lost on how well you understand, negotiate, and document standards.</p>

<p>This lesson is a working reference. It assumes you already know the difference between public regulations (mandatory, set by governments) and private standards (voluntary, set by buyers, retailers, or schemes). Here we focus on the negotiation table: what to accept, what to push back on, what to price in, and what to walk away from.</p>

<h3>Why Negotiation Is a Business Skill, Not a Compliance Task</h3>

<p>Many African exporters treat buyer standards as a fixed gate: either you pass or you don't. That framing is expensive. In practice, most private standards are negotiated documents. Retailers in the EU have supplier manuals running to 80 pages; large coffee roasters have sustainability codes with hundreds of indicators. No smallholder cooperative meets every clause on day one. The question is which clauses are non-negotiable, which can be phased, and which can be met through group or third-party arrangements.</p>

<p>The exporters who win are the ones who arrive at the negotiation with a costed gap analysis. "We comply with 78% of your clauses today. Clauses 4.2, 5.1, and 7.3 require an investment of USD 18,000 over two seasons. If you can commit to a 12-month trial purchase at USD 2.40/kg, we can finance the upgrade." That is a business conversation. It is very different from silence followed by a failed audit.</p>

<h3>Case Snapshot: Shea Butter to France</h3>

<p>A women's cooperative in Ghana exporting unrefined shea butter to a French cosmetics buyer faced three requirements: (1) EU cosmetic ingredient regulation compliance (public, mandatory), (2) the buyer's own <em>charte fournisseur</em> requiring documentary traceability from collection point to drum, and (3) an organic certificate for a premium line. The cooperative could not afford organic certification for the entire volume. The negotiated outcome: 60% of volume under the buyer's standard with group traceability, 40% earmarked for organic certification over 18 months, with the buyer co-financing the certification body's inspection costs through a 3% price premium. The deal closed. Without negotiation, the cooperative would have lost the contract to a competitor willing to absorb the cost.</p>

<h3>Case Snapshot: Coffee to Germany</h3>

<p>A Ugandan coffee exporter selling to a German roaster was told to comply with the roaster's sustainability code, which referenced Rainforest Alliance and additional in-house indicators on living income and agroforestry. The exporter's gap analysis showed full Rainforest Alliance compliance but only partial coverage of the in-house indicators. The roaster accepted a two-year improvement plan with annual verification by an approved auditor, and agreed to a fixed price floor for the first year. The lesson: private codes often reference public or scheme standards as a baseline, then add buyer-specific layers that are themselves negotiable.</p>

<h3>Using Trade Portals to Prepare Your Negotiation</h3>

<p>Two free portals do most of the heavy lifting for African exporters:</p>

<p><strong>ITC Standards Map</strong> (standardsmap.org) lets you search by product and destination market to see which public and private standards apply. It summarises each standard's requirements and shows overlaps, which is exactly what you need to build a gap analysis before a buyer meeting.</p>

<p><strong>AfCFTA and national trade portals</strong> including the African Trade Observatory and national single windows help you understand intra-African standards and rules of origin, which matter if you are sourcing from multiple African countries or selling into African markets before re-exporting.</p>

<p>For EU-bound exports, the European Commission's Access2Markets portal gives the definitive list of public requirements: tariffs, rules of origin, SPS measures, and product-specific regulations. This is the baseline you must meet regardless of what any buyer asks for.</p>

<h3>How to Negotiate: A Practical Sequence</h3>

<p><strong>1. Ask for the standard in writing before quoting.</strong> Never quote a price before you have the buyer's supplier manual or code of conduct. Standards change cost structure. A price quoted without standards is a price you will regret.</p>

<p><strong>2. Map every clause to one of the four categories.</strong> Use Table 1. This tells you instantly what is negotiable.</p>

<p><strong>3. Cost the gap.</strong> For each unmet clause, estimate the investment, the timeline, and the recurring cost. This becomes your negotiation document.</p>

<p><strong>4. Propose a phased plan with a price.</strong> "We can meet clauses 16 now. Clauses 79 require USD X and Y months. We propose a trial order at price Z, with a review at month six."</p>

<p><strong>5. Offer alternatives, not refusals.</strong> If the buyer demands Rainforest Alliance and you have Fairtrade, show equivalence where it exists. If the buyer demands a specific packaging format, offer a costed alternative.</p>

<p><strong>6. Get commitments in writing.</strong> Volume, price, duration, and who pays for audits. Verbal assurances are not bankable.</p>

<p><strong>7. Know your walk-away point.</strong> If the standard requirement destroys your margin and the buyer will not share cost or commit volume, the deal is not a deal. It is a subsidy you are paying to the buyer.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Obtain the buyer's full specification sheet and supplier code in writing before quoting any price.</li>
<li> Sort every requirement into: public mandatory, private mandatory, private voluntary, or buyer preference.</li>
<li> Cross-check public requirements on Access2Markets and ITC Standards Map for your specific product and HS code.</li>
<li> Build a costed gap analysis: investment required, timeline, and recurring cost per unmet clause.</li>
<li> Propose a phased compliance plan with a trial order, price, and review date.</li>
<li> Confirm who pays for audits, testing, certification, and any corrective action.</li>
<li> Never accept a clause you cannot document or test test before you commit.</li>
<li> Define your walk-away point in advance and stick to it.</li>
</ul>
