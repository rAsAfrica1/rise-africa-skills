1. Farrowing Pens: 3 in Use

In a batch farrowing system, you need three farrowing pens in use at all times. This is because you have three groups of sows at different stages: one group farrowing, one group nursing, and one group weaning. Each group stays in the farrowing house for about 5 weeks (1 week before farrowing, 4 weeks nursing). With a batch interval of 3 weeks, you need three pens per group. So if you have 6 sows per batch, you need 18 farrowing pens total. But the lesson title says '3 in Use' â that means at any given time, you have 3 pens occupied by sows that are farrowing or nursing. The other pens are empty, being cleaned, or waiting for the next batch.

In Africa, farrowing pens are often built with locally available materials. A good pen is 2.5 meters by 2.5 meters, with a 0.6-meter-high wall. Use concrete for the floor if possible â it's easy to wash and prevents worms. If concrete is too expensive, use packed earth with wood shavings. The pen needs a creep area for piglets: a corner with a heat lamp or a simple wooden box lined with dry grass. In hot climates, ensure good ventilation with open sides and a roof of corrugated iron or thatch.

Each pen should have a feeder and a water nipple. For a sow and her litter, use a 1.5-meter-long trough that is 20 cm deep. Water nipples are better than open bowls because they keep the water clean. Install a nipple at 30 cm height for the sow and a lower one at 15 cm for piglets. The pen must have a gate that opens inward so the sow cannot push it open.

Clean the pen thoroughly between batches. Remove all bedding, scrub with soap and water, then disinfect with a 2% bleach solution. Let it dry for at least 2 days before the next sow enters. This prevents disease like coccidiosis and scours. Keep a record of which pen is used for which batch â paint numbers on the wall. With 3 pens in use, you can manage a steady flow of piglets to the nursery every 3 weeks.

2. Nursery Space Per Batch

After weaning at 4 weeks, piglets move to the nursery. In a batch system, you need enough nursery space for one batch of weaners. A batch might be 6 sows x 10 piglets = 60 piglets. Each weaner needs 0.3 square meters of floor space. So for 60 piglets, you need 18 square meters. If you build pens of 3 meters by 3 meters (9 square meters), you need 2 pens per batch. But because you have batches every 3 weeks, you need to keep the nursery empty for cleaning between batches. So you need 2 pens for the current batch and 2 pens for the next batch â total 4 pens, or 36 square meters.

In Africa, nursery pens can be simple. Use a raised floor of wooden slats or wire mesh to keep piglets off the wet ground. This reduces diarrhoea. The slats should be 2 cm wide with 1 cm gaps. Build walls 1 meter high, with a solid lower half (concrete or mud) and wire mesh above for ventilation. The roof should be insulated â use grass thatch or a double layer of iron sheets with a gap. Piglets need a warm environment: 28Â°C in the first week, then reduce by 2Â°C each week. In hot climates, use fans or open sides. In cold areas, use a heat lamp or a charcoal stove (careful with fumes).

Each pen needs a small feeder and a water nipple. Feeders should be 1 meter long for 30 piglets. Water nipples at 20 cm height. Keep the pens dry â wet floors cause pneumonia. Use wood shavings or sawdust as bedding, changed every 2 days.

A good nursery design in Africa is the 'open-sided' pen with a concrete floor and a slatted area. Many farmers use old shipping pallets for slats. The key is to have easy access for cleaning. Make sure the pen is secure against rats and wild birds that carry disease. With 4 pens, you can rotate: use 2, clean 2. This gives 2 days of drying time. Always keep a record of piglet weights â weigh a sample every week to check growth. If piglets are not gaining 300 grams per day, check feed and water.

3. Grower Space Per Batch

Growers are pigs from 10 weeks to 20 weeks old, weighing 30 kg to 70 kg. They need 0.8 square meters per pig. In a batch system, you have one batch of growers at a time. If your batch is 60 pigs, you need 48 square meters. If you build pens of 4 meters by 6 meters (24 square meters), you need 2 pens. But because batches overlap every 3 weeks, you need extra pens for the next batch. So total 4 pens, or 96 square meters.

In Africa, grower pens are often built with mud bricks or concrete blocks. The floor should be concrete for easy cleaning, but if not, use packed earth with a slope for drainage. Walls 1.2 meters high. The pen should have a covered area (3 meters by 4 meters) and an open area for exercise. Pigs need space to move â overcrowding causes tail biting and fighting. Provide a wallow in hot weather: a shallow concrete pit with water. This helps them cool down.

Feeders: use a 2-meter-long trough for 30 pigs. Water: use nipple drinkers, 1 nipple per 10 pigs. Install at 40 cm height. Growers are messy â they waste feed. Use a feeder with a lip to prevent spilling. Clean the pen daily, removing wet bedding and manure. Use a wheelbarrow and shovel. Disinfect weekly with a 1% bleach solution.

A good layout is to have pens in a row with a central alley for feeding. This saves labour. In many African farms, growers are kept on deep litter â a 30 cm layer of wood shavings or rice husks. This absorbs urine and creates heat. But you must turn it weekly and replace every 3 months. If you use concrete, you can wash it down. Always provide shade â a simple roof of palm fronds works. With 4 pens, you can rotate: 2 for current batch, 2 for next. This allows 1 week of cleaning and drying between batches. Keep records of feed intake â a grower should eat 2 kg of feed per day. If they eat less, check for worms and treat with ivermectin.

4. Finisher Space Per Batch

Finishers are pigs from 20 weeks to market weight (around 90-100 kg). They need 1.2 square meters per pig. For a batch of 60 pigs, you need 72 square meters. If you build pens of 5 meters by 8 meters (40 square meters), you need 2 pens. With batch overlap, you need 4 pens total, or 160 square meters.

In Africa, finisher pens are often the simplest. Many farmers use open yards with a concrete feeding floor and a shaded resting area. The floor should be concrete to prevent mud and foot problems. If concrete is too costly, use a raised slatted floor over a pit. Walls can be 1 meter high with wire mesh above. Provide a self-feeder â a 1.5-meter-long hopper that holds 50 kg of feed. This saves labour. Water: use a large trough or nipple drinkers. A finisher drinks 6-10 litres per day. In hot weather, ensure water is always available.

Finishers need space to lie down. Provide a dry, clean area with bedding. In humid areas, use raised platforms to keep them off the wet ground. Clean the pen twice a day â remove manure to a compost pit. Disinfect weekly. Watch for respiratory diseases â good ventilation is key. Open sides with a high roof allow air movement. In cold areas, use curtains to block wind.

A common mistake is to overcrowd finishers. This slows growth and increases disease. Stick to 1.2 square meters per pig. If you have 60 pigs, 72 square meters is minimum. You can also use outdoor pens with electric fencing, but in Africa, predators like jackals can be a problem. So secure fencing with posts and wire mesh is better. With 4 pens, you can market one batch every 3 weeks. This gives you daily pig sales â the goal of this course. Keep a weight chart â finishers should gain 700 grams per day. If not, check feed quality and deworm. Sell at 90 kg live weight for best price.

5. Dry Sow and Boar Housing

Dry sows are pregnant sows that are not nursing. They need 2 square meters per sow. Boars need 6 square meters each. In a batch system, you keep dry sows in groups. For 6 sows per batch, you need 12 square meters. If you have 3 batches, you need space for 18 sows â 36 square meters. Boars: if you have 1 boar per 20 sows, you need 1 boar pen of 6 square meters. But if you use artificial insemination, you may not need a boar.

In Africa, dry sow housing can be a simple open shed with a concrete floor. Group housing is better than individual stalls â sows can exercise. But you must feed them separately to avoid fighting. Use a feeding stall system: a row of individual feeding spaces with a gate that locks the sow in while eating. This is called an 'electronic sow feeder' in large farms, but you can make a simple wooden version. Each sow gets 2 kg of feed per day. Provide plenty of water â 10 litres per sow per day.

Boar pens need strong walls â boars are powerful. Use concrete or heavy timber. The pen should have a concrete floor for hygiene. Boars need exercise â a 6 square meter pen is minimum, but 10 square meters is better. Provide a rubbing post â a strong wooden post for the boar to scratch against. Keep the boar cool with a wallow or sprinkler. In hot climates, boars can suffer from heat stress and become infertile. So shade and water are critical.

Clean dry sow pens daily. Remove manure and wet bedding. Disinfect weekly. Check sows for signs of return to heat â if a sow returns to heat, she was not pregnant. Keep records. A good dry sow house has a central alley for feeding and a manure pit at the end. In many African farms, dry sows are kept on deep litter with rice husks. This works if you add fresh litter weekly. But watch for mastitis â dirty bedding causes udder infections. So keep it clean. With proper housing, your sows will be healthy and produce large litters.

6. Calculating Your Total Space

To calculate total space for a batch farrowing system, you need to add up all the areas for each stage. Let's use a batch of 6 sows, each producing 10 piglets. That's 60 piglets per batch. You have 3 batches in the system at different stages. So total pigs: 18 sows (6 farrowing, 6 nursing, 6 dry) plus 1 boar, plus 60 piglets in nursery, 60 growers, 60 finishers. That's 18+1+60+60+60 = 199 pigs. But space is calculated per stage, not per pig total.

Farrowing: 18 pens of 2.5m x 2.5m = 6.25 sq m each. Total 112.5 sq m. But you only need 3 pens in use at a time â but you need 18 pens to rotate. So farrowing house: 18 pens x 6.25 = 112.5 sq m, plus alleyways (20% extra) = 135 sq m.

Nursery: 4 pens of 3m x 3m = 9 sq m each. Total 36 sq m. Plus alley 20% = 43 sq m.

Grower: 4 pens of 4m x 6m = 24 sq m each. Total 96 sq m. Plus alley 20% = 115 sq m.

Finisher: 4 pens of 5m x 8m = 40 sq m each. Total 160 sq m. Plus alley 20% = 192 sq m.

Dry sow and boar: 36 sq m for sows + 6 sq m for boar = 42 sq m. Plus alley 20% = 50 sq m.

Total space = 135 + 43 + 115 + 192 + 50 = 535 sq m. That's about 0.05 hectares. But you also need feed storage, a office, and a manure pit. Add 20% for those = 642 sq m. So roughly 650 sq m, or 0.065 hectares.

In Africa, you can build this on a small plot. Use local materials to save money. For example, farrowing pens can be made with mud bricks and thatch roof. Nursery pens with wooden slats. Grower and finisher pens with concrete floors and wire mesh walls. Always include a water source and a dipping tank for footbaths. Keep a tape measure and a notebook â measure your pens and write down the sizes. This helps you plan for expansion. If you want to increase to 10 sows per batch, multiply all areas by 10/6 = 1.67. So total space becomes 650 x 1.67 = 1085 sq m. Plan ahead â it's cheaper to build once than to renovate later.

Practical Reference: Housing for a Batch Farrowing System

In a batch farrowing system, housing is not just a welfare requirement â it is a scheduling tool. Every pen, creep area, and finishing floor must be sized so that a batch moves through the system without bottlenecks, without buying extra feed to cover overflow, and without turning away a buyer because pigs are not ready. For African pig producers running 10â50 sows, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to whether the housing plan matches the batch calendar.

This Practical Reference gives you the numbers, the diagrams, and the calculations to design or audit housing for a batch system. It assumes tropical and subtropical conditions common across West, East, and Southern Africa: high ambient temperatures, limited climate control, and a strong preference for natural ventilation and locally available materials.

1. Farrowing Pens: 3 in Use

In a small batch system, farrowing pens are the most expensive and most critical housing unit. A practical rule for a 3-sow batch is to have 3 farrowing pens in active use plus 1 spare pen for cleaning, repair, or an emergency. Each pen should provide:

  • A sow crate or farrowing rail to reduce piglet crushing (optional in low-input systems, but strongly recommended).
  • A creep area for piglets â warm, dry, and protected from the sow.
  • Non-slip flooring, ideally concrete with a slight slope for drainage.
  • A dunging area away from the lying area.

Typical dimensions for a pen with a farrowing crate are 2.2 m Ã 1.8 m (about 4 mÂ²), with a creep area of 0.6 m Ã 1.2 m. In hot climates, open-sided pens with a roof overhang of at least 0.5 m reduce heat stress and improve piglet survival.

5. Dry Sow and Boar Housing

Dry sows and boars are often overlooked in batch systems, yet poor housing here directly reduces conception rates and litter size. In tropical Africa, group housing with individual feeding stalls is the most practical compromise between welfare and cost.

  • Dry sows: 1.5â2.0 mÂ² per sow in group housing, with 0.6â0.7 m of feeding space per sow if not using stalls.
  • Boars: 6â8 mÂ² individual pen, with solid walls at least 1.2 m high and a strong gate. Boars are powerful and can injure handlers.
  • Shade and wallows: Essential for heat management. A simple wallow or misting line can improve conception rates by 10â20% in hot climates.

Ensure pens have secure gates, non-slip floors, and an escape route. In hot weather, pigs are more irritable â handle them early morning or late evening, and never corner an animal.
