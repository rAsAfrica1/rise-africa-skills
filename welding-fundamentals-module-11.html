Lessons

1. Vertical and Overhead Welding Practice

Vertical and overhead welding are the most challenging positions because gravity pulls the molten metal down. In vertical welding, you can go uphill or downhill. Uphill (vertical-up) gives deeper penetration and is used for thick steel, like 6mm and above. Set your machine to 90-110 amps for 3.2mm 6011 or 6010 electrodes. Use a short arc and a slight upward angle of 5-15 degrees. Move in a steady zig-zag or triangle pattern, pausing at the sides to let the metal freeze. Downhill is faster but less penetrating; use it for thin sheets (1.5-3mm) at 70-90 amps. For overhead, you need to fight gravity. Use 6011 or 6010 rods at 80-100 amps for 3.2mm. Keep the arc very short and push the rod into the joint at a slight angle. Use a back-and-forth motion to control the puddle. In Africa, many workshops use inverter welders like the ESAB Buddy or local brands. Practice on scrap steel plates first. Always wear a leather jacket, gloves, and a welding helmet with at least shade 10. In overhead, hot sparks fall on you, so don't look up. Start with short beads, 50-75mm long, then overlap them. Clean each bead with a chipping hammer and wire brush before the next pass. Remember, heat input matters: too much heat causes the metal to sag; too little causes lack of fusion. Listen to the arca steady crackling sound means good settings. If it pops, increase amps slightly. Practice 30 minutes daily for two weeks, and you'll see improvement.

2. Pipe Welding Basics

Pipe welding is a high-demand skill in Africa for water pipelines, oil and gas, and construction. You'll mostly weld carbon steel pipes using SMAW (stick) with 6010 for root and 7018 for fill and cap. For a 6-inch pipe with 6mm wall thickness, bevel the ends to 37.5 degrees, leaving a 1.5-2mm root gap and a 1.5mm land. Tack weld in four places. Use a 3.2mm 6010 rod at 70-90 amps for the root pass. The key is to push the rod into the gap and use a slight back-and-forth motion to keyhole the root. This ensures full penetration. For the hot pass, use 7018 at 90-110 amps, moving quickly to avoid burning through. Fill passes: use a slight weave, 3-4mm wide, at 100-120 amps. Cap with a smooth weave, 5-6mm wide, at 100-120 amps. Clean each pass with a wire brush. In Africa, many pipes are welded in the 5G position (horizontal fixed). You'll need to adjust your angle as you move around. For the bottom (6 o'clock), use a slight upward push; for the top (12 o'clock), use a slight drag. Practice on scrap pipes first. Use a pipe stand or chain clamp to hold the pipe. Always check for slag inclusions by grinding a test piece. With practice, you can pass a visual inspection and even radiographic testing. Remember, pipe welding is about consistencykeep your travel speed and arc length steady.

3. Aluminum Welding with GTAW

Aluminum is tricky because it conducts heat fast and forms an oxide layer that melts at a much higher temperature than the metal itself. GTAW (TIG) is the best process for thin aluminum (1-6mm). You need a AC/DC machine set to AC, with high frequency for arc starting. Use pure tungsten (green) or zirconiated (white) electrodes, 1.6mm or 2.4mm. For 3mm aluminum, set the machine to 120-150 amps. Use 4043 filler rod for general work, or 5356 for stronger joints. Clean the aluminum with a stainless steel brush dedicated to aluminum, then wipe with acetone. Don't use the same brush for steel. Set the AC balance to about 70% penetration (or 30% cleaning) to break the oxide. Use a gas lens and 100% argon at 10-12 liters per minute. Hold the torch at a 10-15 degree angle, almost vertical. Start the arc on a scrap piece to form a puddle, then move to the joint. Add filler rod with a dabbing motion. Aluminum welds should be bright and shiny; a dull gray weld means too much heat or dirty metal. In Africa, many workshops use inverter TIG welders like the Miller Dynasty or local brands. Practice on 3mm aluminum plates, butt joints and T-joints. Keep your travel speed fast to avoid burn-through. After welding, clean with a stainless brush. Remember, aluminum has no color change when hot, so you must watch the puddle. With practice, you can weld aluminum radiators, window frames, and boat parts.

4. Stainless Steel Welding Techniques

Stainless steel (like 304 or 316) is used in food processing, dairy, and chemical plants across Africa. It welds differently from carbon steel: it expands more, conducts heat poorly, and can warp easily. Use GTAW (TIG) for thin sheets (1-3mm) or SMAW for thicker sections. For TIG, use DCEN (electrode negative) with 2% thoriated (red) or lanthanated (gold) tungsten, 1.6mm or 2.4mm. Set amps: 60-80 for 1.5mm, 90-120 for 3mm. Use 308L filler for 304, and 316L for 316. Argon gas at 8-10 L/min. Keep the arc short and travel fast to minimize heat input. Use a chill bar or copper backing to prevent warping. For stick welding, use 308L-16 or 316L-16 electrodes at 70-90 amps for 3.2mm. Use a short arc and a slight back-and-forth motion. Stainless steel welds should be silver or light straw color. If they turn blue or black, you're too hot. Clean the weld with a stainless brush after each pass. In Africa, many fabricators weld stainless steel tanks and pipes. Always use a dedicated stainless brush and never use it on carbon steel. Preheating is not needed for thin sections, but for thick (above 10mm), preheat to 100-150C. After welding, passivate the weld with a citric acid gel to restore corrosion resistance. Practice on scrap pieces, then try butt welds and fillet welds. Remember, stainless steel is expensive, so measure twice and cut once.

5. Multi-Pass Welds and Weaving

Multi-pass welding is used for thick steel (above 6mm) where a single pass won't fill the joint. Each pass builds on the previous one, and you must clean thoroughly between passes. For a V-groove butt joint on 10mm steel, use 6010 for root at 80-90 amps, then 7018 for fill and cap. The root pass should penetrate fully. Then, increase amps to 110-130 for fill passes. Use a weaving motion: a slight side-to-side oscillation, about 2-3 times the electrode diameter. For 3.2mm rod, weave 6-10mm wide. Pause at the sides to let the metal freeze and avoid undercut. For the cap, use a wider weave (10-15mm) and a steady speed. Overlap each pass by 50% of the previous bead. Clean slag with a chipping hammer and wire brush after each pass. In Africa, multi-pass welding is common in structural steel, truck frames, and heavy machinery. Use a back-step technique to control distortion: weld from the center outward, or skip around. For vertical-up multi-pass, use a triangle pattern. Always check for slag inclusions between passes; grind them out. If you hear a crackling sound, the amps are too low. If the puddle is too fluid, reduce amps. Practice on 10mm plates, making a 3-pass weld. Then cut and etch the cross-section to check for fusion. With practice, you can weld 20mm steel with 5-6 passes. Remember, multi-pass welding is slower but stronger than single pass.

6. Welding Repair and Maintenance

Welding repair is a valuable skill for fixing broken machinery, tools, and structures. First, identify the base metal: use a magnet (sticks to carbon steel, not to stainless or aluminum) and a grinder spark test (carbon steel has long orange sparks; stainless has short red sparks; aluminum has no sparks). Clean the area with a grinder to bare metal, removing rust, paint, and oil. For cracks, grind a V-groove along the crack to ensure penetration. Drill a 3mm hole at each end of the crack to stop it from spreading. For cast iron repairs, preheat to 200-300C and use nickel rods (like ENi-Cl) at 80-100 amps, peening each bead. For aluminum repairs, use GTAW with 4043 filler. For steel, use 6011 for dirty or unknown steel, or 7018 for clean steel. Always clamp the parts to prevent movement. Weld in short passes, 25-50mm, and let cool between passes to avoid distortion. After welding, grind the weld smooth if needed. For maintenance, check your welder: clean the liner, replace contact tips, and check gas flow. Keep your helmet lens clean and replace if cracked. Store electrodes in a dry place; 7018 must be kept in a rod oven at 120C. In Africa, many repairs are done on-site with portable inverter welders. Always have a fire extinguisher nearby. Test the repair with a hammer or load before putting it back into service. Remember, a good repair is often stronger than the original part.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Advanced Welding Techniques and Practice

This practical reference distils the six lessons of the Advanced Welding Techniques and Practice module into a field-ready guide for African welding enterprises. Whether you run a fabrication shop in Kumasi, a pipeline crew in the Niger Delta, a repair workshop in Nairobi's Industrial Area, or a mobile welding service serving farms and mines across Southern Africa, the principles below protect your margins, your people, and your reputation. Advanced welding is not simply about passing a certification test it is about repeatable quality that wins contracts, reduces rework, and keeps skilled welders employed.

Why Advanced Technique Matters to Your Business

In most African markets, welding is a competitive, price-sensitive trade. The welder who can run a clean vertical-up root pass on a structural column, TIG a cracked aluminium gearbox housing, or repair a stainless steel milk line without contaminating it commands premium rates and repeat customers. Conversely, defects such as lack of fusion, porosity, and undercut cost money: rework hours, rejected components, and in pipeline or pressure work, catastrophic failure. The techniques in this module are the difference between a welding business that survives on small jobs and one that qualifies for tenders in construction, mining, food processing, water infrastructure, and oil and gas.

Vertical and Overhead Welding Practice

Vertical and overhead positions are where manual skill is tested most. For vertical-up (3G) welding with SMAW, use a slight weave, keep the arc short, and watch the puddle edges to prevent undercut. Vertical-down is faster and suits thin sheet and some pipe root passes, but it demands higher travel speed and lower amperage to avoid lack of penetration. Overhead (4G) welding requires lower current, a tight arc, and careful manipulation to fight gravity the molten pool must freeze quickly. In African fabrication shops, where much work involves structural steel for buildings, canopies, and agricultural equipment, mastering these positions opens doors to site work that flat-position-only welders cannot access.

Pipe Welding Basics

Pipe welding is a high-value skill across Africa water pipelines, oil and gas transmission, and process piping in mines and refineries all depend on it. The essential sequence is: joint preparation (bevel 3037.5, land 1.52 mm, root gap 23 mm), tacking, root pass (often TIG or cellulosic SMAW), hot pass, fill passes, and cap. For open-root welds, maintain a consistent keyhole and watch for burn-through. In the field, wind and humidity are enemies: use windbreaks and preheat or dry electrodes where necessary. Certification to recognised standards (e.g., AWS D10.10 or API 1104 for pipelines) is often a tender requirement, so investing in welder qualification pays commercial dividends.

Aluminum Welding with GTAW

Aluminium is increasingly common in African workshops vehicle bodies, irrigation fittings, food equipment, and boat repairs. GTAW (TIG) with AC is the standard for thin to medium aluminium because the alternating current provides cleaning action on the oxide layer. Key points: use pure tungsten or zirconiated tungsten (for AC), 100% argon shielding, and a stainless steel or dedicated aluminium wire brush to remove oxide before welding. Preheat thick sections to 100150C. Travel speed is higher than steel because aluminium conducts heat away rapidly; too slow and you get a wide, mushy bead. Filler selection matters: 4043 for general work, 5356 for higher strength and anodised parts.

Stainless Steel Welding Techniques

Stainless steel is vital in Africa's food, beverage, dairy, and pharmaceutical sectors. The main risks are sensitisation (chromium carbide precipitation) and distortion. Use low heat input, stringer beads rather than wide weaves, and keep interpass temperature below 150C. For GTAW, DCEN with 2% thoriated or lanthanated tungsten and argon shielding is standard; for SMAW, use E308L-16 or E316L-16 depending on the base grade. Back-purge with argon on pipe and vessel work to prevent sugaring on the inside surface. Cleanliness is non-negotiable: stainless must be kept away from carbon steel contamination use dedicated brushes, grinding discs, and work surfaces.

Multi-Pass Welds and Weaving

Multi-pass welding builds up thick sections with controlled heat input and better grain structure than a single huge pass. Each pass should be cleaned before the next: slag chipped, wire-brushed, and inspected. Weaving is useful for wider beads and for controlling heat distribution, but excessive weave width (generally beyond 3 electrode diameter) increases the risk of slag entrapment and lack of fusion. For vertical-up, a slight side-to-side weave with a pause at the edges helps wet in the toes and prevents undercut. On stainless and aluminium, keep weaves narrow to limit heat input.

Welding Repair and Maintenance

Repair welding is a major revenue stream for African workshops cracked machinery, worn excavator buckets, broken trailer frames, leaking tanks. The repair welder must first identify the base metal (mild steel, cast iron, aluminium, stainless) and the cause of failure. For cast iron, preheat and use nickel-based electrodes (e.g., ENi-Cl) with short, intermittent beads to avoid cracking. For worn surfaces, hardfacing electrodes build up abrasion resistance. Always clean the area to bare metal, remove cracks completely (dye penetrant helps), and control distortion. Documenting repairs and offering a warranty builds trust and repeat business.

SAFETY WARNING Welding Fumes and Ventilation

Welding fumes contain metal oxides, fluorides, and in some cases hexavalent chromium (from stainless steel) and manganese. In enclosed African workshops with poor ventilation, exposure can cause metal fume fever, lung damage, and long-term neurological effects. Always weld in a well-ventilated area or use local exhaust ventilation (LEV) and appropriate respiratory protection (P2/P3 or supplied air for stainless and galvanised steel). Never weld galvanised steel without removing the coating and using extraction zinc fumes are acutely toxic. Keep a fire extinguisher within reach, wear leather gloves, apron, and a welding helmet with correct shade (typically 1013 for SMAW).

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm base metal and joint prep identify material, clean to bare metal, and check bevel, gap, and land before striking an arc.
  • Set machine parameters match amperage, polarity, and gas flow to the process and position; record settings for repeatability.
  • Check PPE and ventilation helmet, gloves, apron, eye protection, and fume extraction or respiratory protection in enclosed spaces.
  • Control heat input especially on stainless and aluminium; keep interpass temperature low and avoid excessive weave.
  • Clean between passes chip slag, brush, and inspect before the next pass to prevent inclusions and lack of fusion.
  • Inspect and document visual check for undercut, porosity, and cracks; record parameters and welder ID for traceability.
  • Maintain equipment check leads, earth clamp, torch consumables, and gas hoses daily; replace worn parts before they cause defects.
  • Price for quality, not just speed factor in rework risk, consumables, and certification requirements when quoting jobs.

Business takeaway: Advanced welding technique is an investment in market access. Welders who can work vertical, overhead, pipe, aluminium, and stainless and who document quality can bid for higher-value work in construction, mining, food processing, and energy. Pair technical skill with safety discipline and equipment maintenance, and your workshop becomes a reliable supplier rather than a commodity competitor.
