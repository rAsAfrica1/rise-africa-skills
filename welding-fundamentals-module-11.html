WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 11 OF 12

Defects

Every weld defect has a cause, and every cause traces back to something in an earlier module. This module is the diagnostic reference.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Name the main weld defects and describe each
  • Recognise each defect on a real weld or a cut cross-section
  • Trace each defect back to its cause
  • State how serious each defect is, and which ones must be repaired
  • Repair or remove a defective weld correctly
  • Understand what destructive and non-destructive testing actually show
Lesson 11.1

Why defects happen

A defect is anything about a weld that makes it fail to do its job. Defects come from one of four sources:

  1. Preparation failures — dirty metal, wrong bevel, wrong gap, wrong alignment
  2. Consumable failures — damp electrodes, wrong electrode for the job, wrong gas
  3. Technique failures — wrong current, wrong arc length, wrong angle, wrong travel speed
  4. Design failures — the joint could not be welded as designed, given the access

Almost every defect you will meet traces back to one of those four. Finding the defect and asking "which of the four caused this?" is the diagnostic routine.

Lesson 11.2

Porosity

Gas trapped in the weld as it solidified. Porosity appears as small round holes — visible on the surface, or hidden inside the weld.

Where it comes from

  • Moisture — in electrodes (module 5), on the metal surface, or in the shielding gas
  • Oil, paint, rust, dirt — anything that vaporises under the arc produces gas
  • Shielding failure — arc too long, wind blowing shielding gas away (MIG and TIG), insufficient gas flow, blocked nozzle
  • Wrong polarity — particularly with flux-cored and some stick electrodes
  • Wet or damaged electrode coating — reduces shielding and adds moisture

What it does

Porosity reduces the cross-sectional area of the weld. A weld full of porosity is like a weld of a smaller size. A few small pores scattered through the weld are usually acceptable. A cluster of pores, or a continuous line of them, is a serious defect.

On pressure vessels, porosity means leaks. On structural work, porosity is a crack initiation point.

Prevention

  1. Clean the joint to bright metal — no oil, paint, rust, moisture
  2. Store electrodes correctly (module 5)
  3. Check shielding gas flow and nozzle (MIG, TIG)
  4. Screen the work from wind (MIG, TIG)
  5. Correct arc length
  6. Correct polarity
Lesson 11.3

Slag inclusion

Slag — the non-metallic residue from the electrode coating — trapped inside the weld. Appears as dark, irregular shapes in a cut cross-section, sometimes visible as dark lines or patches on the weld surface.

Where it comes from

  • Not cleaning between passes — the single most common cause. Each stick pass leaves slag; if you do not chip it off before the next pass, the next pass traps it
  • Welding over the slag in the joint itself during the pass
  • Wrong electrode angle or travel speed — if the pool runs ahead of the slag, the slag gets trapped before it can float to the surface
  • Interrupted arc — if the arc stops and restarts within a pass, slag can be trapped at the restart

What it does

Slag is not metal. A weld with slag inclusions has less metal where the slag is, and the slag itself is a stress concentration. It acts like a crack waiting to happen.

Any visible slag inclusion is a defect. Hidden ones are worse — you cannot see them from outside, and they show up on X-ray or when the weld fails.

Prevention

  1. Chip the slag off every pass before starting the next. Wire brush after chipping to remove the fine residues
  2. Watch the pool — the slag should float ahead of and on top of the pool. If it is being pushed under the pool, the angle or speed is wrong
  3. Keep the arc going steadily. Restarting mid-pass tends to trap slag

The step most often skipped

Chipping slag is slow. It is not a productive-looking activity. But every pass on a multi-pass weld must have its slag removed before the next pass. Skipping this is why multi-pass stick welds fail with slag inclusions.

Chipping hammer and wire brush — buy good ones, keep them by the bench, and use them every time.

Lesson 11.4

Lack of fusion and lack of penetration

Two related defects, both about the weld not joining to something it should.

Lack of fusion

The weld metal sits next to the parent metal but does not fuse to it. The two look joined from outside, but the boundary between them is not welded.

Two forms:

  • Lack of sidewall fusion — the weld does not fuse to the sides of the joint in a bevel or fillet
  • Lack of inter-run fusion — a subsequent pass does not fuse to the previous pass

Where it comes from

  • Current too low — the pool is not hot enough to melt the parent metal edges
  • Travel speed too fast — the pool moves on before fusion has time to happen
  • Arc directed at the wrong place — the arc needs to reach the joint edges, not just run down the middle
  • Dirty joint — contamination prevents fusion even at correct current
  • Wrong electrode angle — the arc goes to one side of the joint and misses the other
  • Not enough current for the thickness — thin arc, no penetration into the joint

Lack of penetration

The weld does not reach the root of the joint — the deepest point of the fusion zone is above where it should be.

Where it comes from

  • Current too low for the material thickness
  • Travel speed too fast
  • Root gap too small, or no gap at all in a joint that needs one
  • Root face too thick on a bevelled joint — the arc cannot get through the land
  • Wrong electrode type — some are designed for shallow penetration (iron-oxide types), some for deep (cellulosic)
  • Arc length too long — the arc is at the surface, not into the joint

What they do

Both are more serious than they look

Neither defect is usually visible from the outside. The weld looks fine. But the joint carries far less load than a sound weld of the same size, and it fails suddenly when loaded.

Lack of fusion and lack of penetration are the defects that give welding its dangerous reputation. A neat bead with no fusion is a decoration that looks like a weld. This is why coded welding exists, and why testing is done on critical joints.

Prevention

  1. Correct current for the material and electrode
  2. Correct travel speed — do not rush
  3. Correct fit-up — gap, bevel angle, root face
  4. Aim the arc so it reaches both sides of the joint
  5. Shorter arc length for more penetration into the joint
  6. Clean the joint — contamination is a common cause of both

The only honest test

Cut a test piece in half and look at the cross-section. The fused zone shows up as a distinct bright region between the parent metal and the weld. If the fusion is shallow, the section shows it.

Do this often while learning. Every welder should cut and inspect at least one test piece a week.

Lesson 11.5

Undercut, overlap, underfill

Undercut

A groove melted into the parent metal alongside the weld, where the arc has burned away metal that the weld did not fill. Visible as a small notch where the weld meets the metal.

  • Cause: current too high, arc too long, travel angle wrong, or travel speed too fast
  • Significance: a stress concentration — a notch in the load path. On a loaded joint, undercut can be the start of a crack. Any undercut deeper than about 0.5mm on structural work is a defect
  • Prevention: lower current, shorter arc, correct angle, slower travel. Watch the edges specifically while welding

Overlap

The weld metal sits on top of the parent metal without fusing to it. Visible as the weld extending over the parent metal at the toe, rather than blending in.

  • Cause: current too low, travel speed too slow, wrong work angle, or the pool running before it freezes
  • Significance: the parent metal underneath is not welded. The overlap is a physical edge with no fusion beneath it — a stress concentration and a place for corrosion to start
  • Prevention: correct current, correct travel speed, angle the electrode so the pool wets into both sides

Underfill

The weld is below the surface of the surrounding metal — the joint is not filled. Visible as a shallow channel along the joint line.

  • Cause: not enough filler added, travel speed too fast, or a joint wider than the weld could fill
  • Significance: a reduced cross-section — the joint carries less load than it should. Also traps moisture if the surface is exposed
  • Prevention: add more filler, slow the travel, use a wider weave on wide joints
Lesson 11.6

Cracking, repair and testing

Cracking

TypeWhen it appearsCause
Hot crackingAs the weld solidifies, while still warmContamination (sulphur, phosphorus), deep narrow weld shape, high restraint
Cold / hydrogen crackingHours to days after weldingHydrogen from moisture in the electrode or on the metal, combined with a hardened HAZ, high restraint and fast cooling
Crater crackingAt the end of a run, immediatelyBreaking the arc abruptly, leaving a hollow crater that shrinks and splits
Lamellar tearingSome hours after welding, in thick plateInclusions in the plate parallel to its surface — the weld's through-thickness stress tears the plate

Any crack is a serious defect

A crack propagates under load. A small crack in a weld becomes a large crack, and eventually a failure. Any visible crack in a weld, and any crack detected by testing, must be repaired.

Repair means grinding the crack out completely — back to sound metal, often past the depth of the crack — and re-welding. A crack that is welded over without being removed will re-open at the boundary between the new weld and the crack.

Repair of a defective weld

  1. Identify the defect and its extent. Grind the surface clean and look. If you cannot see the extent, the defect goes deeper than you think — grind until you find sound metal
  2. Remove the defect completely. Grind past it, past the edges of the crack if it is a crack, and into sound parent metal. A half-removed defect is still a defect
  3. Fix the cause. If the defect came from damp electrodes, dry your electrodes before re-welding. If it came from insufficient current, set it correctly. Re-welding without fixing the cause just produces the same defect
  4. Re-weld. Use the correct technique for the joint
  5. Re-inspect. The repaired area is now the most critical part of the joint. Check it thoroughly

Testing

Destructive testing

The weld is cut, bent, or broken to see how it behaves. This is what you do on test pieces.

  • Cut and etch — cut the weld across, polish the cut face, and inspect the fusion and structure
  • Bend test — the weld is bent, either root side or face side out, to a specified angle
  • Nick break — a notch is cut into the weld and the piece is broken. The fracture surface shows fusion, penetration and internal defects

Non-destructive testing (NDT)

The weld is inspected without destroying it. Done by specialist technicians with specialist equipment.

  • Visual inspection — the simplest, and catches surface defects. The welder should do this on every weld
  • Dye penetrant — a coloured liquid is applied, wiped off, and a developer draws out any that has entered cracks. Shows surface-breaking defects
  • Magnetic particle — a magnetic field is applied and iron particles are dusted onto the surface. Defects disturb the field and show as lines. For ferromagnetic materials only
  • Ultrasonic testing — sound waves are sent through the weld and reflections from defects are analysed. Detects internal defects
  • Radiographic testing (X-ray) — X-rays pass through the weld and expose a film. Shows internal defects. The most thorough, and the most expensive

None of these are DIY tests. They require equipment, training and experience to interpret.

Practical assignment — Module 11

Do the practical work if you have access to a machine and supervision. Otherwise complete the observation and analysis parts.

  1. Find five welds with visible defects in real structures — gates, frames, brackets, machinery. Photograph each close-up and identify the defect from the list in this module. State what caused it
  2. Produce three defects on purpose: weld a bead with arc too long (porosity), a multi-pass bead without chipping slag (slag inclusion), and a weld with too little current (lack of fusion). Photograph each. Cut one in half and photograph the cross-section
  3. The repair exercise: take one defective weld, grind out the defect completely, and re-weld it correctly. Photograph the ground-out joint and the re-welded result
  4. Your defect log. Keep a log for two weeks of every defective weld you produce: what the defect was, why you think it happened, and what you changed next time
  5. Research testing. Find out what non-destructive testing services are available in your area — ultrasonic, radiographic, dye penetrant. Write down names, services offered, and typical cost if you can get it
  6. The critical defect list. List the defects from this module that you consider must be repaired every time, and the ones that are sometimes acceptable. Justify each

Points 1, 3 and 4 carry the most marks. Point 1 tests whether you can recognise defects in real work. Point 3 tests whether you know how to repair one properly. Point 4 is the habit that turns a beginner into a competent welder.

Module 11 knowledge check

1. What is the most common cause of slag inclusion in a multi-pass stick weld?

  1. Too much current
  2. Not chipping the slag off between passes
  3. Wrong electrode type
  4. Too fast travel
Show answer

b. Each stick pass leaves a slag coating. If the next pass is made on top without chipping and brushing the slag off first, the next pass traps the slag beneath it. Multi-pass stick welding requires chip-brush between every pass.

2. Why are lack of fusion and lack of penetration particularly dangerous?

  1. They look terrible
  2. They are usually not visible from outside — the weld looks fine but carries far less load than it appears to
  3. They cause porosity
  4. They make the weld too hard
Show answer

b. Both defects are typically invisible on the surface. A neat bead with no fusion is a decoration that looks like a weld. This is why coded welding and testing exist, and why cutting test pieces is the only honest check.

3. What causes undercut?

  1. Current too low
  2. Current too high, arc too long, or travel speed too fast — the arc burns a groove into the parent metal that the weld does not fill
  3. Wrong electrode type
  4. Too little filler
Show answer

b. Undercut is a groove melted into the parent metal alongside the weld. It concentrates stress and can initiate cracking under load. Reduce current, shorten the arc, and slow down.

4. Why must a crack be completely ground out before re-welding?

  1. To make room for the new weld
  2. Because welding over a crack without removing it will re-open at the boundary between the new weld and the existing crack
  3. To reduce the cost
  4. It is not necessary — welding over a crack works
Show answer

b. A crack propagates under load. Welding over it does not remove it, and the new weld will crack along the same line. The crack must be ground out completely — often past the visible extent, into sound metal — before re-welding.

5. What does radiographic (X-ray) testing show that surface inspection does not?

  1. The colour of the weld
  2. Internal defects — porosity, slag inclusion, lack of fusion — that are hidden inside the weld and not visible on the surface
  3. The hardness of the weld
  4. The chemical composition
Show answer

b. Radiography passes X-rays through the weld and exposes a film, revealing the internal structure. It detects hidden defects that visual inspection cannot, but it requires specialist equipment and a trained technician to perform and interpret.
