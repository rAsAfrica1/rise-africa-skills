1. What Are Pellets and How They Differ from Briquettes

Pellets are small, cylindrical pieces of compressed biomass, typically 6-8 mm in diameter and 10-30 mm long. They are made by forcing ground biomass through a die under high pressure. The pressure and heat (from friction) cause lignin in the biomass to soften and bind the particles together. Unlike briquettes, which are larger (often 50-100 mm) and made in molds or extruders, pellets are uniform and flow like grain, making them suitable for automatic feeding in stoves and boilers.

In Africa, pellets are gaining popularity for use in gasifier stoves and small industrial boilers. They burn more efficiently than briquettes because their uniform size allows for controlled air flow and complete combustion. For example, a 10 kW pellet stove can run for 8-10 hours on 5 kg of pellets, while the same energy from briquettes might require 7-8 kg due to higher moisture and uneven burning.

Making pellets requires more processing than briquettes: you need to grind the material finely (to 3-5 mm), control moisture (10-15%), and use a pellet mill. Briquettes can be made with simpler presses and tolerate larger particle sizes. However, pellets command a higher price in urban markets because they are clean, consistent, and easy to handle. A 25 kg bag of pellets in Kenya or South Africa sells for $8-$12, while briquettes sell for $4-$7.

For a small producer, pellets offer a profitable niche if you can secure a consistent supply of dry biomass like sawdust, rice husks, or maize cobs. The key difference is that pellets are a premium product for automatic stoves, while briquettes are often used in manual stoves and open fires. Understanding this distinction helps you target the right customers and set up the right production line.

2. Small-Scale Pellet Mills: Manual and Motorized Options

A pellet mill compresses biomass into pellets. For small-scale production in Africa, you have two main options: manual and motorized. Manual pellet mills are hand-cranked or lever-operated. They cost $150-$400 and can produce 20-50 kg per hour with 2-3 people working. They are ideal for very small businesses or cooperatives that cannot afford electricity or fuel. However, they require significant physical effort and produce pellets of varying quality.

Motorized pellet mills are more common for commercial production. A small electric mill (3-5 kW) costs $800-$2,500 and produces 100-300 kg per hour. Diesel-powered mills (8-15 HP) cost $1,500-$4,000 and are suitable for areas without reliable electricity. These mills have a die and rollers that compress the biomass. The die size determines pellet diameter (usually 6 mm or 8 mm).

When choosing a mill, consider the power source, capacity, and spare parts availability. In many African countries, imported mills from China or India are common, but local fabricators in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa also make reliable machines. Check that the mill can handle your raw materialsome mills are designed for sawdust, others for agricultural residues. A hammer mill is also needed to grind material to the right size; a small hammer mill costs $500-$1,500.

Maintenance is critical. Dies and rollers wear out after 300-500 hours of use and cost $100-$300 to replace. Keep spare dies and rollers on hand. Lubricate bearings daily and check belt tension. For a start-up, a 100 kg/hour motorized mill with a 7.5 kW motor is a good balance of cost and output. You can produce 800 kg in an 8-hour day, enough to supply local stoves and boilers.

3. Preparing Material for Pelletizing: Grinding and Moisture Control

The quality of your pellets depends on proper material preparation. First, grind your biomass to a fine, consistent size. A hammer mill with a 3-5 mm screen is ideal. If particles are too large, the pellet mill will jam or produce crumbly pellets. If too fine, pellets may be dense but the mill may overheat. For rice husks, which are already small, you may only need to remove impurities. For maize cobs or branches, you must grind them first. A typical hammer mill processes 200-500 kg per hour, depending on the screen size and material.

Next, control moisture content. The ideal moisture for pelletizing is 10-15%. If biomass is too dry (below 10%), the pellets will not bind well and will be dusty. If too wet (above 15%), the mill will clog and pellets will be soft. Test moisture by squeezing a handful of ground material: it should clump together but not drip water. For accurate measurement, use a moisture meter ($50-$150). In humid African climates, you may need to dry material in the sun on a concrete slab for 1-2 days, or use a simple solar dryer.

If material is too dry, add water gradually while mixing. A concrete mixer or shovel works well. Add 1-2 liters of water per 100 kg of biomass, mix thoroughly, and let it sit for 30 minutes to equalize. If too wet, spread it out to dry. Never add oil or binders unless specifiednatural lignin in the biomass is sufficient. For materials like rice husks that are low in lignin, you may blend with 20-30% sawdust to improve binding.

Always prepare material in batches and test a small amount in the pellet mill before full production. Adjust moisture or grind size as needed. Consistent preparation is the key to strong, durable pellets that burn efficiently.

4. Operating a Pellet Mill: Step-by-Step Guide

Operating a pellet mill safely and efficiently requires following a sequence. Start by wearing safety gear: gloves, goggles, and ear protection. Ensure the mill is on a level surface and all guards are in place. Check that the die and rollers are clean and not worn. Lubricate bearings and check belt tension. For a motorized mill, start the motor and let it run for 1-2 minutes to warm up.

Next, feed a small amount of prepared biomass into the hopper. For a 100 kg/hour mill, start with 1-2 kg. Watch the ammeterif the motor draws too much current, reduce feed rate. The mill should run smoothly. If it shakes or makes loud noises, stop immediately and check for foreign objects or jam. Once pellets start coming out, gradually increase feed to full capacity. The die temperature will rise to 80-100C due to friction; this is normal and helps bind the pellets.

Adjust the feed rate to match the mill's capacity. For a 7.5 kW mill, feed about 100 kg per hour. If pellets are soft or crumbly, the moisture may be too high or the die may be worn. If pellets are too hard and shiny, moisture may be too low. Collect pellets in a container or directly into a cooling bin. Do not let pellets pile up more than 20 cm deep while hot, as they may stick together.

After production, run the mill empty for 2-3 minutes to clear remaining material. Turn off the motor and clean the die and rollers. Remove any stuck pellets with a wooden sticknever use metal tools that can damage the die. Record output and note any issues. With practice, you can produce 800 kg in an 8-hour day. Regular maintenance and consistent feed will keep your mill running for years.

5. Cooling, Sieving, and Bagging Pellets

Freshly made pellets are hot (70-90C) and soft. They must be cooled before bagging, or they will stick together and mold. Spread pellets in a thin layer (5-10 cm deep) on a clean concrete floor or in a cooling bin. In hot African climates, cooling takes 20-30 minutes. In humid conditions, use a fan to speed cooling and prevent moisture absorption. Do not cool pellets in direct sunlight, as they may dry too much and become brittle.

After cooling, sieve the pellets to remove fines (dust and broken pieces). A simple vibrating screen or a manual sieve with 4 mm openings works well. Fines can be recycled back into the pellet mill. For a 100 kg batch, you might get 5-10 kg of fines. Sieving improves pellet quality and reduces dust in stoves. You can sell fines separately as a low-cost fuel or use them for briquettes.

Bag pellets in 25 kg or 50 kg woven polypropylene bags. Use bags with a plastic liner to keep moisture out. For premium markets, use branded bags with handles. Fill bags to the desired weight, then sew or tie them shut. Store bags in a dry, well-ventilated area on pallets, away from walls. In humid regions, use silica gel packets or store in a dehumidified room. Properly stored pellets can last 6-12 months.

Label each bag with production date, pellet size, and moisture content. This builds trust with customers. For boiler users, you may offer 1-tonne bulk bags. Always keep a sample from each batch to test quality. Cooling, sieving, and bagging are simple but essential steps to ensure your pellets reach customers in good condition and command a higher price.

6. Quality Standards for Pellets in African Markets

Pellet quality determines their performance and price. In African markets, there are no universal standards, but buyers look for specific characteristics. The most important are moisture content, ash content, durability, and calorific value. Moisture should be below 10% for good combustion. Ash content should be below 5% for wood pellets; for agricultural residues, up to 10% is acceptable. High ash means more cleaning and lower heat. Durability is measured by tumbling pellets; good pellets have less than 1% fines after tumbling.

Calorific value (energy content) should be 16-18 MJ/kg for wood pellets and 14-16 MJ/kg for agricultural pellets. You can test this with a bomb calorimeter, but for small producers, a simple burn test in a stove gives a good indication. Pellets should burn with a steady flame, produce minimal smoke, and leave little ash. If they smoke heavily or produce clinkers, the ash content is too high or moisture is too high.

For export or premium markets, you may need certification. The ENplus standard (European) is recognized globally, but expensive to obtain. For local markets, focus on consistency. Buyers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg want pellets that are uniform in size, clean, and dry. A 6 mm pellet is most common for stoves; 8 mm for boilers. Package pellets in 15 kg or 25 kg bags for households, and 500 kg or 1 tonne for boilers.

To compete, offer a guarantee: if pellets don't meet moisture or ash specs, replace them. Train your workers to check quality daily. Keep records of raw material source, production date, and test results. As the market grows, standards will emerge. By producing high-quality pellets now, you build a reputation that will pay off as more African households and industries switch to biomass pellets.

<h2>Practical Reference: Making Pellets for Stoves and Boilers</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the module <strong>Making Pellets for Stoves and Boilers</strong> in the <em>Briquettes &amp; Pellets</em> course. It is written for African agribusiness operators who want to turn crop residues, sawdust, and other biomass into a sellable pellet product for domestic cookstoves, institutional boilers, and small industrial thermal systems. The focus is business-first: what you need to know, what it costs, what can go wrong, and how to meet buyer expectations.</p>

<p>Pellets are densified biomass cylinders, typically 68 mm in diameter, made by forcing ground and conditioned biomass through a die under high pressure. Unlike briquettes, which are usually larger (often 30100 mm) and made with lower-pressure presses or binders, pellets are uniform, flowable, and designed for automatic feeding systems. In African markets, pellets are increasingly used in improved cookstoves, poultry brooding, tobacco curing, tea drying, and institutional boilers.</p>

<h2>What Are Pellets and How They Differ from Briquettes</h2>

<p>Pellets and briquettes are both densified biomass fuels, but they serve different markets. Pellets are small, uniform, and dense, with bulk densities typically between 600 and 750 kg/m. They are produced by extruding milled biomass through a die, often with steam or water conditioning, and the natural lignin in the feedstock acts as a binder. Briquettes are larger, often made with manual or hydraulic presses, and may require clay, cassava starch, or other binders to hold their shape.</p>

<p>For an African agribusiness, the choice between pellets and briquettes depends on your target customer. Pellets command higher prices in urban and institutional markets because they feed automatically and burn efficiently. Briquettes are often cheaper to produce and better suited to rural households using manual stoves. However, pellet production demands more capital, more consistent feedstock, and tighter quality control.</p>

<h2>Small-Scale Pellet Mills: Manual and Motorized Options</h2>

<p>African producers have three main equipment pathways: manual presses, motorized flat-die mills, and ring-die mills. Manual presses are cheapest but have very low output (1030 kg/hour) and are best for pilot production or demonstration. Motorized flat-die mills are the most common entry point for small businesses, with outputs of 100500 kg/hour and prices ranging from USD 1,500 to USD 8,000 depending on capacity and whether they are locally fabricated or imported. Ring-die mills are more expensive (USD 10,000+) but produce higher-quality pellets and are better for continuous operation.</p>

<p>In Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Nigeria, and Ghana, local fabricators are increasingly producing flat-die pellet mills using locally available motors and steel. This reduces import costs and improves access to spare parts. However, local fabrication quality varies, so buyers should test the mill on their own feedstock before purchase and confirm die hardness and roller alignment.</p>

<h2>Preparing Material for Pelletizing: Grinding and Moisture Control</h2>

<p>Feedstock preparation is where most small pellet businesses fail. Pellets need consistent particle size and moisture. If particles are too large, the mill jams; if moisture is too high, pellets crumble; if too low, the feedstock will not bind and the die overheats.</p>

<p>Grinding is usually done with a hammer mill. For most flat-die mills, a 35 mm screen is appropriate. Sawdust may need only light grinding, while maize cobs, rice husks, and straw require more size reduction. Moisture should be adjusted to 1215% for most feedstocks. In humid African conditions, drying may be necessary using sun drying, solar dryers, or a rotary dryer. In dry conditions, water may need to be added using a sprayer or mixer.</p>

<p>Mixing different feedstocks can improve pellet quality. For example, sawdust mixed with rice husk or maize cob can improve durability. However, high ash feedstocks like rice husk reduce calorific value and increase boiler maintenance. For cookstove markets, a mix of sawdust and agricultural residues often works well.</p>

<h2>Operating a Pellet Mill: Step-by-Step Guide</h2>

<p>Operating a pellet mill safely and efficiently requires discipline. Start by checking the die and rollers for wear, then warm up the mill with a small amount of oily feedstock or a commercial conditioner. Feed material slowly at first, then increase to full load as the die temperature rises. Watch the ammeter: if the motor draws too much current, reduce feed rate. Listen for unusual noises and check pellet quality every 1530 minutes.</p>

<p>For flat-die mills, the rollers press material through the die from the inside out. For ring-die mills, rollers press material from the outside in. In both cases, the key variables are feed rate, moisture, particle size, and die condition. Keep a log of settings and output so you can repeat good runs.</p>

<h3 style="color:#b30000;margin-top:0"> SAFETY WARNING: Pellet Mill Hazards</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0">Pellet mills can cause severe injury. Never reach into the die or roller area while the machine is running or connected to power. Always isolate the power supply before clearing jams, changing dies, or performing maintenance. Wear tight-fitting clothing, safety glasses, and hearing protection. Dust from biomass can be explosive; keep the work area clean and avoid open flames. In African workshops with unreliable electricity, use a lockout/tagout procedure and ensure only trained operators start the mill.</p>
</div>

<h2>Cooling, Sieving, and Bagging Pellets</h2>

<p>Freshly pressed pellets are hot, soft, and fragile. They must be cooled before bagging, or they will crumble and mould. In small operations, cooling can be done in shallow trays or on a perforated floor with a fan. Commercial operations use counter-flow coolers. Target cooling to within 5C of ambient temperature.</p>

<p>Sieving removes fines and broken pellets. Fines can be re-pelletized, sold as a lower-grade product, or used as fuel in the dryer. Bagging should be done in dry conditions. Use 1550 kg woven polypropylene bags with inner liners for moisture protection. Label bags with product type, moisture content, ash content, and production date. For institutional buyers, provide a technical data sheet with calorific value, ash, moisture, and durability index.</p>

<h2>Quality Standards for Pellets in African Markets</h2>

<p>There is no single African pellet standard, but buyers increasingly refer to ISO 17225-2 (wood pellets) or ISO 17225-6 (non-woody pellets). In practice, most African markets use informal specifications: diameter 68 mm, length 1030 mm, moisture below 10%, ash below 5% for cookstoves, and durability above 95%. For boilers, ash below 3% is preferred, and chlorine and sulphur must be low to avoid corrosion.</p>

<p>Export markets, especially in Europe, require certified pellets under ENplus or ISO 17225. African producers targeting export should plan for laboratory testing, traceability, and consistent feedstock. For local markets, focus on consistent quality, dry bags, and reliable supply. Reputation is the strongest marketing tool.</p>

<ul>
<li>Confirm feedstock supply: at least 10 tonnes/month of consistent material within 50 km.</li>
<li>Test feedstock moisture before grinding; target 1215% for most materials.</li>
<li>Grind to 35 mm and check with a sieve or by hand.</li>
<li>Warm up the mill and feed slowly; monitor motor current and pellet quality.</li>
<li>Cool pellets to ambient temperature before bagging; never bag hot pellets.</li>
<li>Sieve out fines and re-pelletize or sell as a secondary product.</li>
<li>Label bags with moisture, ash, and date; keep records for every batch.</li>
