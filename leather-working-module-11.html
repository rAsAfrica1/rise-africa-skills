1. Costing and Pricing

To make money from leather, you must know your costs. Start by listing every expense for one batch. For example, to make 10 goat leather bags, you need 10 hides (at $5 each = $50), chemicals for tanning ($20), thread ($5), buckles ($10), and your labour (10 hours at $3/hour = $30). Add electricity and water ($5). Total cost = $120. That's $12 per bag. But you also have overheads: rent, tools, transport. If monthly overhead is $100 and you make 50 bags, add $2 per bag. So your true cost is $14 per bag. Never price below this. To set a price, decide your profit margin. A common rule is to double your cost for wholesale, triple for retail. So wholesale price = $28, retail = $42. Check competitor prices at local markets or online. If your bag is unique, you can charge more. For export, add export costs like packaging, shipping, and export duty. For example, shipping 10 bags to Europe might cost $50, so add $5 per bag. Also consider currency exchange rates if selling in dollars or euros. Keep a simple notebook or use a free app like Wave for invoicing. Always calculate cost per item, not per batch, to avoid mistakes. Review your prices every three months as material costs change. Remember, undercharging kills your business faster than overcharging.

2. Supply Chain Management

A supply chain is the path from raw hide to finished product. In Africa, you might source hides from local slaughterhouses, tanneries, or farmers. Build relationships with at least three suppliers to avoid shortages. For example, if one butcher has 10 hides per week, and you need 20, find another. Inspect hides for damage: cuts, holes, or rot. Pay a fair price; a good goat hide costs $3-$7 depending on quality. After tanning, you need chemicals like lime, tannin, and dyes. Buy in bulk from suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Cairo to save money. For example, a 50kg bag of tannin might cost $100, but buying 10 bags reduces unit cost. Store chemicals safely away from children and moisture. For tools, invest in a fleshing knife ($15), a beam ($50), and a stitching awl ($10). When selling, you need packaging: recycled paper or cloth bags, and labels. If exporting, work with a freight forwarder who knows customs. Keep records of every purchase and sale. Use a simple spreadsheet or notebook. Plan your production based on orders to avoid dead stock. For example, if you have 100 hides, but only 50 orders, tan half and store the rest properly. Join a cooperative of leather workers to buy supplies together and negotiate better prices. Good supply chain management means no delays, no waste, and happy customers.

3. Marketing Strategies

Marketing is telling people about your leather goods and convincing them to buy. Start with your local market: visit weekly markets, craft fairs, and trade shows. Set up an attractive stall with your best items. Price clearly. Offer a small discount for bulk buys. Use word of mouth: give business cards to satisfied customers and ask them to refer friends. For example, a customer who buys a belt might tell five others. Social media is powerful. Create a free Instagram or Facebook page. Post clear photos of your products in natural light. Show the process: tanning, cutting, stitching. Use hashtags like #AfricanLeather, #HandmadeLeather, #MadeInKenya. Post at least three times a week. Respond to comments quickly. You can also sell on WhatsApp: create a catalog with prices and share with your contacts. For export, list on platforms like Etsy or Amazon Handmade. But note fees: Etsy charges 6.5% per sale. Consider partnering with local boutiques or hotels that sell African crafts. Offer them a 30% wholesale discount. Attend trade missions organized by your government or NGOs. For example, the African Leather and Leather Products Institute (ALLPI) runs training and trade fairs. Always collect customer contact details for future marketing. A simple SMS reminder about a new product can bring repeat sales. Remember, marketing is not a one-time event; it's ongoing.

4. Export Regulations and Documentation

Exporting leather products can increase your income, but you must follow rules. First, check if your country allows raw hide export. Many African countries ban raw hide export to encourage local processing. For finished goods, you need an export license from your Ministry of Trade. Register your business with the tax authority and get a Tax Identification Number (TIN). For each shipment, you need a commercial invoice, packing list, and certificate of origin. The certificate of origin proves your goods are made in your country, which may qualify for lower tariffs under agreements like AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act) or EU-ACP. You also need a phytosanitary certificate if using plant-based tannins. For animal products, a veterinary health certificate is required. Work with a customs broker to prepare these documents. For example, a broker in Mombasa might charge $100 per shipment. Label your products correctly: include country of origin, material, and care instructions. If exporting to the EU, leather goods must meet REACH regulations on chemicals. Test your dyes for azo compounds. For small shipments, use courier services like DHL or FedEx; for bulk, use sea freight. Sea freight from Lagos to London might cost $500 for 100kg. Keep copies of all documents for five years. Non-compliance can lead to fines or confiscation. Join export promotion agencies for guidance.

5. Branding and Labeling

Branding makes your leather products stand out. Start with a name and logo. Choose a name that reflects your African heritage, like 'Sahara Leather' or 'Zulu Craft'. Design a simple logo using free tools like Canva. Use it on labels, packaging, and social media. A label must include: your brand name, product material (e.g., 'Genuine Goat Leather'), country of origin, and care instructions (e.g., 'Wipe with dry cloth'). For export, add a barcode or QR code that links to your website. Print labels on sturdy paper or card. You can buy adhesive labels at office supply stores or order custom ones from local printers. For example, 1000 labels might cost $50. Branding also means consistent quality. If a customer buys a wallet and loves it, they will trust your brand for a belt. Tell your story: how you source hides, how you tan, how you support local artisans. Use storytelling on your website and social media. For example, 'Each bag is hand-stitched by Mary in Kibera.' This adds value. Consider getting a trademark to protect your brand. In Kenya, trademark registration costs about $100. Also, use eco-friendly packaging to appeal to conscious buyers. A simple cloth bag with your logo can be reused. Remember, branding is not just a logo; it's the total experience of your product.

6. Customer Relationship Management

Customer relationship management (CRM) means keeping your customers happy so they buy again and tell others. Start by collecting contact details: phone number, email, and location. Use a notebook or a free CRM app like HubSpot. After a sale, send a thank-you message within 24 hours. For example, 'Thank you for buying our leather belt. We appreciate your support.' Ask for feedback: 'How was your experience?' If they have a problem, fix it quickly. A customer who complains and gets a solution becomes loyal. Offer a loyalty program: after five purchases, give a 10% discount or a free small item. For example, a keychain. Keep in touch with a monthly newsletter or WhatsApp broadcast. Share new products, care tips, and special offers. Remember birthdays and anniversaries if you have that data. For bulk buyers, like hotels or boutiques, visit them regularly. Bring samples. Ask about their needs. If they order 50 bags, deliver on time and follow up. Handle returns gracefully: if a customer is unhappy, offer a refund or exchange. This builds trust. Train your staff to be polite and helpful. A smile costs nothing but earns much. Use social media to respond to comments and messages. A quick reply can turn a query into a sale. Finally, measure your customer satisfaction. A simple survey with emojis works. Happy customers are your best marketing.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Business and Marketing of Leather in Africa



This practical reference consolidates the core operational knowledge a leather entrepreneur needs to convert hides and skins into profitable, compliant, and marketable products. It is written for tanners, leather goods makers, cooperatives, and SME exporters operating in African markets from the tanneries of Fez and Kano to the emerging leather clusters in Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa. The focus is on decisions that affect cash flow, margin, and market access.



1. Costing and Pricing


Leather businesses fail most often not because of poor craftsmanship but because of inaccurate costing. A hide has a purchase price, but the true cost includes chemicals (lime, sodium sulphide, chrome or vegetable tannins), water, energy, labour, wastage, and overheads. In many African tanneries, water and energy can represent 1525% of processing cost, so these must be tracked per square foot or per kilogram.



Use a simple unit-costing model: Total Cost Usable Output = Cost per Unit. Always separate fixed costs (rent, salaried staff, licences) from variable costs (raw hides, chemicals, piece-rate labour). Price using a markup that covers risk and reinvestment a minimum of 3040% gross margin for local sales, and 50%+ for export orders where compliance and logistics costs are higher.

2. Supply Chain Management


Leather supply chains in Africa are fragmented. Hides are collected from abattoirs, villages, and informal slaughter slabs, often with poor preservation (flaying damage, salt shortage, delayed collection). Up to 30% of raw hides in some regions are lost to spoilage or poor flaying a direct hit to margin. Vertically integrating or contracting reliable hide collectors is one of the highest-return moves a tannery can make.



Key practices: establish collection centres within 100 km of slaughter points; train flayers on knife technique and salting; pay by quality grade, not weight alone; and maintain cold-chain or salt-cure discipline. For exporters, traceability from abattoir to finished leather is increasingly demanded by EU and US buyers under due-diligence rules.





3. Marketing Strategies


African leather has a strong story: heritage craftsmanship, unique local designs, and increasingly sustainable vegetable-tanned options. But story alone does not sell. Segment your market:



  • Local B2B: shoe makers, upholsterers, bag manufacturers sell on consistent quality and reliable delivery.

  • Regional B2C: fashion accessories, belts, sandals sell through markets, boutiques, and social commerce (WhatsApp Business, Instagram, Jumia).

  • Export B2B: international brands and traders sell on compliance, traceability, and certified quality.


Digital marketing works well for small producers. A well-photographed product on Instagram or TikTok with a clear price and delivery promise can outperform a trade fair. For B2B, LinkedIn and targeted email outreach to sourcing managers in Europe, Turkey, and China remain effective.



4. Export Regulations and Documentation


Exporting leather from Africa requires compliance with both local and destination-country rules. Common requirements include:



  • Export licence and registration with the national revenue or trade authority.

  • Certificate of Origin (e.g., AfCFTA, COMESA, EU-ACP preferences).

  • Phytosanitary or veterinary certificate for raw hides; processed leather usually needs a sanitary certificate.

  • Compliance with EU REACH (restricted substances), US CPSIA (for children's products), and buyer-specific chemical management protocols (e.g., ZDHC, LWG).

  • Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and customs declaration.




SAFETY AND COMPLIANCE WARNING

Chrome tanning involves hexavalent chromium (Cr VI), a known carcinogen. Never allow Cr VI to form through poor pH control or oxidation. Workers must wear gloves, eye protection, and respiratory masks in finishing areas. Wastewater containing chromium must be treated before discharge untreated effluent can lead to fines, closure, and export bans. In the EU, leather containing >3 mg/kg Cr VI cannot be placed on the market. Non-compliance can destroy a business overnight.

5. Branding and Labeling


Branding is what separates a commodity hide from a premium product. For African leather businesses, branding should communicate origin, process, and quality. Labeling must be accurate and legally compliant:



  • State material composition (genuine leather, bonded leather, PU never mislabel).

  • Include country of origin and manufacturer details.

  • Use care instructions (e.g., "wipe with dry cloth, avoid prolonged sunlight").

  • For export, include batch/lot numbers for traceability.

  • Certifications (LWG, ZDHC, Fair Trade) add credibility and can justify higher prices.



6. Customer Relationship Management


Repeat business is cheaper than new business. Keep a simple CRM even a spreadsheet recording customer name, product preferences, order history, payment behaviour, and follow-up dates. For B2B buyers, consistent communication on lead times and quality issues builds trust. For B2C, loyalty discounts, WhatsApp broadcast lists, and after-sales care advice increase repeat purchases. In African markets where trust and relationship are central, personal follow-up often outperforms automated systems.



Quick Reference Checklist



  • Calculate true cost per square foot/kg including chemicals, water, energy, and wastage.

  • Set price with minimum 30% gross margin (local) or 50%+ (export).

  • Secure at least two reliable hide suppliers with quality grading and salting training.

  • Verify export documentation: Certificate of Origin, sanitary cert., invoice, packing list, bill of lading.

  • Test leather for Cr VI and restricted substances before export.

  • Label products accurately with material, origin, care, and batch number.

  • Maintain a customer record with order history and follow-up dates.

  • Treat tannery wastewater and protect workers with PPE compliance is non-negotiable.

Bottom line: In African leather, margin is made at the hide collection point, protected in the tannery, and realised through compliant, well-branded marketing. Master costing, supply chain discipline, and export compliance and the business will grow.
