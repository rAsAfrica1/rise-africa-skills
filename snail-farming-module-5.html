5. Breeding and Nursery Management

Snail farming is a breeding business. If you cannot reliably produce new snails, you have no foundation for growth. This module covers selecting breeders, mating, egg collection, hatchling care, and reducing mortality in the nursery — the most delicate stage of snail farming.

Module 5 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 5.1 — Selecting breeding stock

Not every adult snail should breed. Choose carefully or you will produce weak offspring and slow-growing generations.

What to look for in breeding stock:

  • Large size. The biggest 20% of your adults. Size is heritable — big parents produce big offspring.
  • Strong, smooth shells. No cracks, chips, thin spots, or deformities.
  • Active behaviour. Snails that move, feed, and explore are healthier than sluggish ones.
  • Age 12–24 months. Peak breeding age. Too young or too old produces fewer eggs.
  • Same species. Never mix Achatina fulica with A. achatina for breeding.
  • Distinct parents. Do not breed brother to sister repeatedly — genetic weakness develops.

How to organise breeding stock:

  • Keep at least 40–50 breeding adults per colony. Larger numbers preserve genetic diversity.
  • Rotate breeders every 12 months. Introduce new unrelated snails at least once a year.
  • Separate breeders into their own pen. Higher attention, lower density (25/m²).
The breeder rule: Only the top 20% of your adults breed. The other 80% go to market or are grown out. This selection pressure improves your stock every generation — the same way livestock farmers improve their herds.
Practical exercise 5.1: Weigh and inspect 50 adults. Rank by size and shell quality. Select the top 10 as future breeders. Move them to a dedicated breeding pen.

Lesson 5.2 — Mating behaviour and timing

Snails are hermaphrodites — each snail has both male and female organs. Most species still require two snails to mate. Some can self-fertilise but rarely do so successfully.

Mating behaviour:

  • Circling. Two snails approach, circle each other for 30–90 minutes, touching with tentacles.
  • Courtship. Some species (especially Achatina) have elaborate courtship, including "love darts" — calcium-based darts used in signalling.
  • Sperm exchange. Both snails exchange sperm. Both will lay eggs later.
  • Delay. Eggs are laid 3–14 days after mating.
  • Frequency. In good conditions, snails mate 2–4 times per year.

When mating is likely:

  • After the first rains (natural trigger)
  • When temperature is 22–28°C
  • When humidity is above 75%
  • When food is abundant
  • When calcium is provided

What blocks mating:

  • Overcrowding (they cannot find space to court)
  • Malnutrition
  • Calcium deficiency
  • Hot, dry conditions
  • Too much handling
  • One sex-mature snail — remember they need a partner
Practical exercise 5.2: In your breeding pen, sit quietly for 30 minutes each day for a week. Watch for mating behaviour. Note which snails pair, when, and how often. Log your observations.

Lesson 5.3 — Egg collection and incubation

Snails lay eggs in small holes in the soil, 5–8 cm deep. Each hole contains 20–60 eggs.

Signs of egg laying:

  • A snail sitting motionless in one spot, head buried in soil
  • Loose soil mounds, 3–5 cm across
  • Small, whitish, oval eggs (3–6 mm) visible when you dig gently
  • A "slime seal" — a shiny patch on the surface where the hole was closed

Egg collection protocol:

  1. Check bedding every 3–5 days for signs of laying.
  2. When you find eggs, gently lift them with a soft spoon or cloth.
  3. Place eggs in a small tray with 2–3 cm of damp (not wet) soil.
  4. Cover the tray lightly. Egg cartons, a light cloth, or a loose lid.
  5. Keep in the dark at 24–28°C. Never direct sun.
  6. Check moisture every 2 days. Spray gently if soil is drying.
  7. Eggs hatch in 14–30 days. Do not disturb them.

Do not dig up eggs unnecessarily. If possible, leave eggs in the pen and let them hatch naturally. Move only eggs you must (overcrowded pens, poor drainage). Every move increases mortality by 5–15%.
Practical exercise 5.3: Prepare 3 incubation trays. When you find eggs, place 30–50 per tray. Log date, number of eggs, and expected hatch date. Track hatch rate — aim for 70%+.

Lesson 5.4 — Hatchling care — the critical 60 days

Hatchlings are the most delicate stage. Most beginner failures happen here. Get the next 60 days right and your success rate doubles.

Hatchling pen requirements:

FactorRequirement
Pen sizeSmall tray or box, 20 × 30 cm for 100 hatchlings
Bedding2–3 cm of fine, damp soil. No lumps.
Humidity80–90% — higher than adults
Temperature24–27°C — never below 20°C
LightDark or dim — no direct light
FoodVery finely chopped leaves, moringa powder, powdered calcium
PredatorsAnts, mites, small beetles — constant watch

Daily care:

  • Spray lightly once or twice daily
  • Add a small amount of finely chopped pawpaw or lettuce leaf
  • Remove any dead hatchlings or uneaten food
  • Check for ants — the #1 killer
  • Do not touch the hatchlings directly

What kills hatchlings:

  • Ants. Every farmer's nightmare. Use a water moat.
  • Dehydration. Humidity must be 80–90%.
  • Starvation. Food must be fine enough for their small mouths.
  • Disease. Dirty bedding, dead snails left in pen.
  • Predators. Mites, small beetles, lizards, spiders.
  • Direct handling. Their shells are too fragile for fingers.
The 60-day rule: Treat the first 60 days with maximum care. Every hour of attention here saves weeks of losses later. Hatchlings that survive 60 days rarely die of natural causes.
Practical exercise 5.4: Set up 2 hatchling trays. Place 100 hatchlings each. Log every death, every day, for 60 days. Calculate survival rate — aim for 70%+. Adjust conditions to improve.

Lesson 5.5 — Reducing mortality — the daily discipline

Snail mortality should drop as you gain experience. Here is the realistic ladder.

ExperienceHatchling mortalityAdult mortality
Year 1 (beginner)40–60%15–25%
Year 225–40%10–15%
Year 3+15–25%5–10%

Daily disciplines that reduce mortality:

  1. Clean pen morning and evening. Remove dead snails, uneaten food, waste.
  2. Check humidity twice daily. Spray as needed.
  3. Watch for ants. Repair any ant barrier immediately.
  4. Feed at consistent times. Snails are creatures of habit.
  5. Do not overcrowd. Move to larger pens when density hits 50/m².
  6. Isolate sick snails. One sick snail can infect others.
  7. Log every mortality. Patterns reveal problems.

Signs of a sick snail:

  • Retracted inside shell for days
  • Shell thin, broken, or discoloured
  • Slime abnormal (yellow, cloudy, excessively thick)
  • Reduced feeding
  • Swollen mantle or body

Action: isolate immediately. Provide fresh food and calcium. If it does not recover in 3–5 days, remove it from the population.

Practical exercise 5.5: Start a mortality log. Every death: date, stage, size, likely cause. After 3 months, look for patterns. Fix the biggest cause first.

Lesson 5.6 — Managing generations and rotation

To keep your farm productive over years, you must manage generations.

Rotate breeding every 12–18 months:

  1. Keep track of parentage. Which snails are the parents of which offspring.
  2. Introduce new genetics every 12–18 months. Buy unrelated snails from another farm or exchange with another farmer.
  3. Retire older breeders. After 18 months of breeding, snails produce fewer eggs. Rotate them to the "table" (harvest) or "grow-out" pen.
  4. Promote the best offspring. From each generation, select the top 20% for future breeding stock.
  5. Never breed siblings for more than 2 generations. Genetic weakness (small size, slow growth, disease susceptibility) develops quickly.
GenerationAction
Founder stockBuy 200+ juveniles from a reliable source
Gen 1 (year 2)Select top 20% as new breeders. Introduce 20+ new unrelated snails.
Gen 2 (year 3)Repeat selection. Introduce new genetics again.
Gen 3+Continue cycle. Your stock becomes locally adapted and hardy.
The genetic rule: Every 18 months, introduce at least 20 unrelated snails to your breeding stock. Even if your current snails are healthy, genetic diversity prevents slow decline in size, growth rate, and disease resistance.
Practical exercise 5.6: Identify 1 other snail farmer in your region or country. Arrange to exchange 20 snails every 12–18 months. This single habit protects your stock forever.
