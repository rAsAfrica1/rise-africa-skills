Lessons

1. Making a Machete or Slasher

To make a machete or slasher, start with a piece of high-carbon steel, such as an old leaf spring from a vehicle (5160 steel) or a flat bar of 1075 steel. A typical machete blade is 4560 cm long, 46 cm wide, and 35 mm thick. For a slasher, the blade is shorter and heavier, about 3040 cm long and 68 mm thick, with a curved edge for chopping. Begin by cutting the steel to rough shape using a hacksaw or angle grinder. If using a leaf spring, anneal it first by heating it to 800850C (cherry red) and cooling slowly in wood ash or vermiculite to soften it for shaping.

Next, forge or grind the bevel. If forging, heat the blade to 850C and hammer the edge to about 1.52 mm thickness. If grinding, use a belt grinder or angle grinder to create a bevel of 1520 degrees. Keep the blade cool by dipping it in water frequently to avoid overheating. For a slasher, leave a thicker spine for strength. Shape the handle tang: it should be about 1215 cm long and 23 cm wide, tapering to fit into a wooden handle.

Heat treatment is critical. Heat the blade evenly to 800830C (non-magnetic) and quench in warm oil (canola or motor oil) at about 5060C. For a machete, temper at 200220C for one hour to achieve a Rockwell hardness of 5055 HRC. For a slasher, temper at 220250C for toughness. Clean the blade with sandpaper to remove scale.

Finally, attach a handle. Use hardwood like mvule, mukau, or ebony. Shape the handle to fit your hand, then secure it with two or three rivets or pins made from mild steel or brass. Drill holes through the tang and handle, insert pins, and peen them over. Sharpen the edge with a file and whetstone at a 20-degree angle. Test on a piece of wood or grass; it should cut cleanly without chipping. Always wear safety glasses and gloves during grinding and heat treatment.

2. Forging and Sharpening a Hoe Blade

A hoe blade is typically made from medium-carbon steel like 1045 or 1080. Start with a flat bar 2025 cm long, 1015 cm wide, and 58 mm thick. If using scrap, a car axle or plough disc works well. Begin by forging the blade shape: heat the steel to 850900C (bright orange) and hammer it to a rectangular or trapezoidal shape. The cutting edge should be about 23 mm thick, while the spine remains 56 mm thick for strength. For a typical African hoe, the blade is angled at about 4560 degrees to the handle. You can forge a socket or an eye for the handle. To make an eye, punch a hole through the heated steel using a drift pin, then shape it around a mandrel.

After forging, anneal the blade by heating to 800C and cooling slowly in ash. Then grind or file the cutting edge to a 3040 degree bevel. This is steeper than a knife because a hoe takes heavy impact with soil. Keep the edge about 11.5 mm thick at the tip. For sharpening, use a mill file or angle grinder, then finish with a coarse whetstone. Do not over-sharpen; a slightly blunt edge is better for rocky soil.

Heat treatment: Heat the blade to 800830C and quench in oil. Temper at 250300C for one hour to achieve a hardness of 4550 HRC. This balances wear resistance and toughness. Clean off scale with a wire brush.

For the handle, use a strong wood like eucalyptus, acacia, or mvule. The handle should be 6090 cm long and 34 cm thick, shaped to fit the eye or socket. If the hoe has an eye, insert the handle from the top, then wedge it with a metal or hardwood wedge. If it has a socket, simply fit the handle into the socket and secure with a pin or nail. Test the hoe on soil; it should dig easily without the edge rolling. Maintain by sharpening with a file every few hours of use.

3. Making a Hand Cultivator

A hand cultivator is a small tool with 35 curved tines used for loosening soil and weeding. To make one, you need a piece of spring steel or mild steel rod, about 68 mm diameter. Spring steel from a car coil spring is ideal. Start by cutting three to five pieces of rod, each 1520 cm long. Anneal them by heating to 800C and cooling slowly. Then bend each rod into a curved tine shape using a vice and hammer. The tines should curve downward and forward, with the tips about 58 cm apart. The overall width of the cultivator head should be 1015 cm.

Next, make the head frame. Use a piece of flat steel 34 mm thick, 23 cm wide, and 1520 cm long. Bend it into a U-shape or a slight arc. Weld or forge the tines onto this frame. If welding, use a 2.5 mm electrode and clean the joints. If forging, heat the frame and tines to 900C and hammer them together, then forge-weld with borax as flux. Ensure the tines are evenly spaced and angled at about 45 degrees to the ground.

Heat treat the tines: heat to 800830C and quench in oil. Temper at 200220C for one hour to achieve 5055 HRC. This gives springiness and wear resistance. The frame can remain softer.

For the handle, use a 3040 cm length of hardwood like mukau or mvule, 34 cm diameter. Fit the handle into a socket or eye on the frame. If there is no socket, weld a piece of pipe (23 cm diameter) to the frame to accept the handle. Secure the handle with a pin or screw. Sharpen the tine tips with a file to a point. Test the cultivator in loose soil; it should break up clods and pull weeds without bending. Keep the tines sharp and oil the metal to prevent rust.

4. Crafting a Digging Fork

A digging fork has 34 strong tines used for loosening soil and lifting root crops. Start with a piece of high-carbon steel, such as a car axle (1045) or a plough tine. You need a bar 23 cm diameter and 4050 cm long for the tines and a socket for the handle. Begin by cutting four pieces of steel rod, each 2530 cm long, for the tines. Anneal them by heating to 800C and cooling slowly. Forge one end of each tine into a point, and the other end into a flat tang for attaching to the head.

Make the head: use a flat steel bar 5 mm thick, 3 cm wide, and 20 cm long. Bend it into a U-shape or a shallow V. Weld or forge the tines onto this bar, spacing them 57 cm apart. The tines should curve slightly forward and downward. Forge a socket from a piece of pipe or by wrapping steel around a mandrel. The socket should fit your handle, about 34 cm diameter. Weld the socket to the top of the head.

Heat treatment: Heat the tines to 800830C and quench in oil. Temper at 250300C for one hour to achieve 4550 HRC. This gives a balance of strength and toughness. The head can be left softer.

Attach a handle: use a strong wood like eucalyptus, acacia, or mvule. The handle should be 80100 cm long and 34 cm thick. Shape one end to fit the socket, then insert and secure with a metal pin or screw. You can also add a metal ferrule (a ring) around the socket to prevent splitting. Sharpen the tine tips with a file. Test the fork by digging into hard soil; it should penetrate without bending. Maintain by cleaning and oiling after use. A well-made fork can last for years.

5. Making a Hand Trowel

A hand trowel is a small, one-handed tool for planting and weeding. Make it from a piece of high-carbon steel, such as an old saw blade (1075) or a car spring. You need a piece about 1520 cm long, 58 cm wide, and 23 mm thick. Begin by annealing the steel if it is hard. Draw the shape of the trowel blade on the steel: a pointed oval or a narrow triangle, about 1012 cm long and 56 cm wide at the base. Cut it out with a hacksaw or angle grinder.

Next, forge or grind the blade. If forging, heat to 850C and hammer the edges to thin them, leaving the center thicker for strength. The edge should be about 1 mm thick. If grinding, use a belt grinder to create a bevel of 2025 degrees. Curve the blade slightly to make a scoop shape. You can do this by hammering over a pipe or using a vice. The tang should be about 810 cm long and 11.5 cm wide, tapering to fit into a handle.

Heat treatment: Heat the blade to 800830C and quench in oil. Temper at 200220C for one hour to achieve 5055 HRC. This makes it hard enough to cut soil but not brittle. Clean with sandpaper.

For the handle, use a hardwood like mukau or mvule. Shape it to a comfortable grip, about 1012 cm long and 23 cm thick. Cut a slot in one end to fit the tang, then insert the tang and secure with a ferrule (a metal ring) and epoxy glue, or drill a hole and pin it with a nail. Sharpen the blade with a file and whetstone. Test in soil; it should dig easily and not bend. A good trowel is indispensable for transplanting. Keep it clean and oiled.

6. Handles and Hafting: Wood Selection and Fitting

The handle is as important as the tool head. A poor handle causes blisters, breaks easily, and wastes your effort. Choose wood that is strong, shock-resistant, and locally available. Good species include mvule (African teak), mukau (melia), eucalyptus, acacia, and ebony. Avoid softwoods like pine or mango; they split and wear out fast. Harvest wood from the heartwood of a mature tree, and air-dry it for at least 612 months to prevent cracking. Green wood shrinks and loosens.

For a machete or hoe, the handle should be 3060 cm long (depending on tool) and 34 cm thick. For a fork or cultivator, 80100 cm long. Shape the handle with a drawknife, spokeshave, or rasp. It should be slightly oval to fit the hand, with a swell at the end to prevent slipping. Sand smooth with 80, then 120 grit paper. Do not varnish; use linseed oil or beeswax to finish. This keeps the wood from drying out and gives grip.

Fitting the handle to the tool head depends on the type of tang or socket. For a tanged tool (machete, trowel), drill a hole slightly smaller than the tang into the handle. Heat the tang to red hot and burn it into the wood for a perfect fit, then remove and quench. Coat the tang with epoxy or wood glue, insert, and secure with a ferrule (a metal ring) and a pin (nail or rivet) if needed. For a socketed tool (hoe, fork), shape the handle end to fit the socket tightly. Insert and drive a wedge into the end of the handle if the socket is open at the top, or use a screw through the socket. For an eye (like a hoe), insert the handle from the top and wedge it with a hardwood or metal wedge.

Always test the fit: the head should not wobble. If it does, add a shim or re-fit. A well-hafted tool feels like an extension of your arm. Maintain handles by sanding out rough spots and re-oiling every few months. Replace cracked handles immediately to avoid injury.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Hand Tool Making for Cutting and Digging

In African smallholder and semi-commercial agriculture, a well-made hand tool is not a consumable it is a capital asset. A machete that holds an edge through a season of cassava harvesting, a hoe that survives rocky soils in the Ethiopian highlands, or a digging fork that resists bending in clay-loam these tools determine labour productivity, crop timing, and ultimately household income. This practical reference consolidates the core technical parameters you need to make, heat-treat, haft, and maintain six essential cutting and digging tools. It is written for the artisan-entrepreneur: someone who intends to sell tools, not just use them.

The business case is straightforward. Imported hand tools in most African markets retail at 25 the cost of locally forged equivalents, and supply chains are unreliable. A trained village blacksmith or workshop using recycled leaf-spring steel, locally sourced hardwood, and basic forge equipment can produce a machete at 3040% of the imported retail price while capturing a healthy margin. The constraint is rarely demand it is consistent quality. This module addresses that constraint directly.

Tool-by-Tool Technical Reference

1. Machete or Slasher

The machete is the single most important cutting tool in African agriculture. Blade length typically ranges from 3060 cm depending on use: 3040 cm for weeding and light clearing, 4555 cm for sugarcane and maize stalk work, and 5565 cm for heavy bush clearing (slasher). Steel selection is critical. Recycled car leaf-spring steel (5160 or equivalent, 0.550.65% carbon) is the standard in most African workshops because it is tough, readily available, and forgeable with charcoal. Blade thickness should be 2.53.5 mm at the spine, tapering to 0.51.0 mm at the edge. The edge geometry should be a convex (apple-seed) grind for impact resistance, not a flat grind.

2. Forging and Sharpening a Hoe Blade

The hoe is the primary tillage tool across sub-Saharan Africa, with regional forms (the Ethiopian gesso, the West African daba, the East African jembe). Blade weight ranges from 300 g (light weeding hoe) to 1.5 kg (heavy breaking hoe). The critical forging step is drawing out the blade from a flat bar or from recycled steel, then shaping the socket or tang. Heat treatment: austenitise at 830870 C (non-magnetic), quench in warm oil or brine depending on steel, then temper at 200300 C for 1 hour to achieve 5055 HRC. A hoe that is too hard will chip on stones; too soft will bend and lose edge retention.

3. Making a Hand Cultivator

Hand cultivators (three-tine or five-tine) are used for shallow weeding and soil loosening between crop rows. Tines are typically forged from 68 mm round or square bar, bent to a 6080 angle, and set 58 cm apart. The tine tips should be hardened and tempered to 4550 HRC hard enough to resist wear, tough enough to bend rather than snap when hitting a stone. The socket or eye must be a snug fit to the handle; a loose eye is the most common failure point.

4. Crafting a Digging Fork

Digging forks are less common than hoes in African smallholder systems but are essential for root crop harvesting, manure handling, and compacted soil work. Four tines are standard, forged from 810 mm bar, 2530 cm long, with a slight curve. The fork head is welded or forge-welded to a socket. Tines should be tempered to 4045 HRC softer than a hoe blade to allow flex under load. A digging fork that is too hard will snap at the tine root; too soft will bend permanently.

5. Making a Hand Trowel

The hand trowel is the highest-volume, lowest-unit-value tool in the set and therefore the most commercially important for a workshop seeking steady cash flow. A trowel is forged from 1.52.5 mm sheet or from flattened bar, 1218 cm long, 69 cm wide, with a tang or socket. The blade should be dished slightly for soil retention. Heat treatment to 4550 HRC. The handle is typically 1014 cm of turned or carved hardwood, fitted with a ferrule.

6. Handles and Hafting: Wood Selection and Fitting

Handle failure is the leading cause of tool returns and reputational damage. Wood selection matters more than most artisans acknowledge. Ideal species across Africa include Combretum spp. (moderate density, good shock absorption), Terminalia spp., Acacia spp. (hard but brittle use for short handles only), and Olea spp. (olive, excellent but scarce). Density should be 0.650.90 g/cm. Moisture content at fitting should be 812% too dry and the handle shrinks loose, too wet and it swells and splits the eye. Grain must run straight along the length; any run-out is a fracture initiation site.

SAFETY WARNING FORGE, QUENCH, AND GRINDING HAZARDS
  • Quench oil fires: Never quench in open containers near the forge. Use a metal quench tank with a lid. Keep a dry-powder extinguisher within reach water on an oil fire causes flashover.
  • Hot metal identification: Cold steel and 800 C steel look identical in shade. Never pick up a blade by hand until it has been colour-verified (black at room temperature) or touched with a wet rag.
  • Grinding dust: Sharpening and grinding generate fine metal and abrasive dust. Wear eye protection and an N95 or better respirator; wet-grind where possible to suppress dust.
  • Handle fitting: Never strike a handle onto a blade with a metal hammer use a wooden or rubber mallet. Splinters from split handles are a leading cause of hand injury in workshops.
  • Ventilation: Forge gases (CO) and quench oil vapours are toxic. Forge outdoors or with forced cross-ventilation. Do not work alone.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Steel verified: Spark-test or known-source recycled steel selected; carbon content estimated before forging.
  2. Blade geometry correct: Convex grind on cutting tools; thickness tapers from spine to edge; no sharp stress risers at tang.
  3. Heat treatment logged: Austenitising colour and quench medium recorded; temper cycle completed and timed.
  4. Hardness checked: File test or hardness tester confirms target HRC for the tool class.
  5. Handle wood seasoned: Moisture content 812%; straight grain; no visible cracks or run-out.
  6. Hafting secure: Eye or socket fits snugly; wedge or ferrule fitted; handle cannot rotate or pull free under load.
  7. Edge finished: Final bevel honed; no burr; cutting test on local crop material passed.
  8. Safety and finish: Sharp edges protected for transport; tool labelled with maker mark and steel type for warranty tracking.
