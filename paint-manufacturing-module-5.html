Lessons

1. Pre-mixing and Wetting of Pigments

Pre-mixing is where you combine the pigment powder with the liquid ingredientsresin, solvent, and additivesto form a paste. The goal is to wet every pigment particle with liquid, so they don't clump together. In Africa, many small paint factories use a high-speed disperser (also called a mixer) for this step. A typical batch starts with 200 liters of alkyd resin in a 500-liter stainless steel tank. Turn on the disperser at low speed (around 500 rpm) and slowly add 150 kg of titanium dioxide powder. If you dump it in too fast, you get lumps that are hard to break later. Add the pigment over 1015 minutes while the mixer runs. Then add 20 liters of mineral turpentine to adjust viscosity, and 5 kg of dispersing agent (like a polyacrylate) to help the particles separate. After all pigment is in, increase speed to 1500 rpm for 2030 minutes. The mixture should look like a smooth, thick cream. Check the temperatureit should not exceed 50C. If it gets too hot, reduce speed or add a little solvent. In hot African climates, you may need to cool the tank with a water jacket or work in the early morning. Wetting is not just mixing; it's a chemical process where the liquid replaces air on the pigment surface. A good pre-mix will save you time and energy in the milling stage. Always wear a dust mask and goggles when handling pigment powders.

2. Dispersion Techniques and Equipment Operation

Dispersion means breaking apart pigment clusters (agglomerates) and spreading them evenly in the liquid. After pre-mixing, you need to apply high shear force. The most common equipment in African paint workshops is the high-speed disperser (HSD) with a saw-tooth blade. The blade diameter should be about one-third of the tank diameter. For a 500-liter tank, use a 150-mm blade. Set the blade height so it is about one blade diameter from the bottomthis creates a rolling vortex. Start at low speed (500 rpm) to avoid splashing, then increase to 20003000 rpm. The tip speed should be around 1520 meters per second. At this speed, you will see a whirlpool and hear a characteristic whine. Disperse for 3045 minutes. For harder pigments like iron oxide, you may need a bead mill or a triple-roll mill. A bead mill uses small ceramic beads (12 mm) to grind particles. In a small factory, a horizontal bead mill with a 20-liter chamber can process 100 liters per hour. Always check the ammeter on the motorif it draws too much current, you are overloading the mill. Reduce feed rate or increase bead load. In Africa, power fluctuations can damage motors, so use a voltage stabilizer. Clean equipment immediately after use; dried paint is very hard to remove. Use a solvent like xylene or acetone. Safety: never open a running mill; beads can fly out. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby because solvents are flammable.

3. Milling and Particle Size Reduction

Milling reduces pigment particles to their final size, usually 520 microns. Smaller particles give better color strength, gloss, and stability. After dispersion, the paste may still have particles larger than 50 microns. You need a mill to grind them down. In Africa, three types of mills are common: ball mill, bead mill, and triple-roll mill. A ball mill is a rotating cylinder with steel or ceramic balls. It is slow (1224 hours) but cheap and good for hard pigments. A bead mill is faster (12 hours) and uses small beads. A triple-roll mill has three rollers that squeeze the paste; it is good for viscous pastes but requires skill. For a small paint factory, a bead mill is often the best choice. For example, a 30-liter bead mill with 1.5-mm zirconia beads can grind a 100-liter batch in 2 hours. The feed rate should be about 10 liters per minute. Check the particle size with a Hegman gauge (see next lesson). You want a reading of 67 on the gauge (which means 1525 microns). If it's lower, run the mill longer or reduce feed rate. Milling generates heat, so cool the mill with water. In hot climates, monitor temperature; if it exceeds 60C, stop and cool. After milling, the paste is called 'mill base' and is ready for let-down. Always wear ear protectionmills are noisy.

4. Monitoring Dispersion Quality: Hegman Gauge

The Hegman gauge (also called a grind gauge or fineness of grind gauge) is a simple stainless steel block with one or two grooves. The groove depth goes from 100 microns at one end to 0 microns at the other. You place a drop of paint in the deep end and draw a straight-edged scraper down the groove. The point where you see specks or scratches indicates the particle size. The scale is marked 0 to 8, where 8 means 0 microns (perfectly smooth) and 0 means 100 microns. For most architectural paints, you want a Hegman reading of 57 (which is 2040 microns). For automotive or high-gloss paints, you need 78 (010 microns). To use the gauge, clean it with solvent and a soft cloth. Place a few drops of the mill base at the deep end. Hold the scraper perpendicular and pull it down smoothly in 12 seconds. Immediately look at the groove under good light. Note the scale number where you first see a speck. Do this three times and take the average. In Africa, where dust is common, always cover the gauge between uses. If your reading is too low (e.g., 34), your dispersion is poor. You may need to add more dispersing agent, increase milling time, or check for worn beads. A good Hegman reading saves you from producing paint that settles or looks gritty. Keep a record for each batch.

5. Let-Down and Final Mixing

Let-down is the step where you add the remaining resin, solvent, and additives to the mill base to turn it into finished paint. The mill base is thick and concentrated; you need to dilute it to the right viscosity and pigment volume concentration (PVC). For a typical emulsion paint, you might have 200 kg of mill base. In a large mixing tank, add 300 kg of resin (e.g., acrylic emulsion) and 100 kg of water. Start the mixer at low speed (300 rpm). Slowly add the mill base. Then add 20 kg of coalescing agent, 5 kg of defoamer, and 10 kg of thickener. Mix for 3045 minutes until uniform. Check viscosity with a flow cup (Ford cup #4). For brush application, aim for 2025 seconds. Adjust with water or thickener. Add biocides (2 kg) to prevent spoilage in hot climates. Also add colorants if needed. For solvent-based paints, use a similar process with solvent instead of water. Always add the mill base to the let-down tank, not the other way around, to avoid shocking the emulsion. After mixing, let the paint stand for 1 hour to release air bubbles. Then check pH (should be 89 for emulsion). If pH is low, add ammonia. This step is critical for consistency. In Africa, many small factories skip proper let-down and get lumpy or separated paint. Take your time.

6. Filtration and Packaging

After let-down, the paint may contain small lumps, dried skin, or foreign particles. Filtration removes these to give a smooth product. Use a nylon filter bag or a vibrating screen. For small batches, a simple bag filter with 50100 micron mesh works well. Place the bag in a drum or over a tank. Pour the paint through the bag. For larger batches, use a basket mill or a self-cleaning filter. In Africa, many factories use a mesh screen (80120 mesh) stretched over a frame. Change the filter when the flow slows down. After filtration, the paint is ready for packaging. Fill into clean containersplastic buckets (5L, 20L) or metal tins. Use a filling machine or a simple gravity filler. Weigh each container to ensure accurate fill. For 20L buckets, fill to 20 kg (assuming density ~1.0). Seal the lid tightly with a rubber mallet. Label with product name, batch number, date, and color. Store in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. In hot African climates, avoid storing in metal containers that can rust. Always keep a sample from each batch for quality control. Clean all equipment immediately with water (for emulsion) or solvent (for solvent-based). Proper filtration and packaging prevent customer complaints and returns.

<h2>Practical Reference: Dispersion and Milling in Paint Manufacturing</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA skills course on Paint Manufacturing, specifically the module <strong>Production Process: Dispersion and Milling</strong>. It is written for small and medium-sized paint producers operating in African markets, where imported raw materials, unreliable power, and variable pigment quality are common constraints. The goal is to help you produce stable, saleable paint with the equipment you have and to reduce batch failures that erode margin.</p>

<p>Dispersion and milling are the heart of paint production. If pigment particles are not properly wetted, separated, and reduced in size, you will see settling, poor colour strength, low opacity, and inconsistent batches. Each of those problems costs money: rework, customer complaints, and lost repeat business. This reference covers the full sequence from pre-mixing through filtration and packaging, with practical checks you can apply on the factory floor.</p>

<h2>1. Pre-mixing and Wetting of Pigments</h2>

<p>Pre-mixing is where you combine pigment, dispersant, solvent (or water), and part of the binder under low-to-medium shear. The purpose is not to grind it is to wet the pigment surface and break down loose agglomerates before high-shear dispersion. In African workshops with limited equipment, a simple high-speed disperser (HSD) or even a heavy-duty mixer can handle pre-mix if the viscosity is controlled.</p>

<p>Wetting is a surface chemistry problem. Pigment particles are surrounded by air and moisture; the dispersant must displace that air and attach to the pigment surface. If wetting is poor, you will see "fish eyes," craters, or a grainy film. In humid coastal markets (Lagos, Mombasa, Durban), pigment stored in open bags absorbs moisture and wets poorly always check bag seals and use freshly opened stock.</p>

<p>Practical rule for small producers: add liquids first, then pigment slowly under agitation. Never dump pigment into a dry mixer. For TiO, a typical pre-mix might be 4050% pigment loading by weight in water with 0.51.5% dispersant on pigment weight. For carbon black or organic pigments, higher dispersant levels are needed because of finer particle size and higher surface area.</p>

<h2>2. Dispersion Techniques and Equipment Operation</h2>

<p>High-speed dispersion uses a toothed disc (Cowles blade) to create shear, impact, and cavitation. The disc tip speed matters more than RPM. For most architectural paints, a tip speed of 1520 m/s is sufficient. Below 10 m/s, you are mixing, not dispersing. Above 25 m/s, you risk over-heating and degrading heat-sensitive pigments.</p>

<p>Equipment options in African markets range from imported HSD units (R 30,000R 120,000) to locally fabricated dispersers built on a steel frame with a Chinese motor. Local fabrication is viable if the shaft is balanced and the blade is properly sized. A poorly balanced shaft destroys bearings and gives inconsistent dispersion.</p>

<p>Operating sequence: start the mixer at low speed, add pre-mix, raise to dispersion speed, and hold for 1530 minutes depending on pigment. Check temperature if the batch exceeds 50C, reduce speed or add cooling. In hot climates, this is a real risk. Dispersion is complete when a Hegman gauge reading stabilises.</p>

<h2>3. Milling and Particle Size Reduction</h2>

<p>Milling reduces pigment particles to their optimum size, typically 520 microns for architectural paints. Bead mills, basket mills, and sand mills are common. In small African operations, a bead mill or horizontal mill is the workhorse. The key variables are bead size, bead loading, flow rate, and temperature.</p>

<p>Bead size selection: 1.52.5 mm zirconia beads for general architectural pigments; 0.81.2 mm for fine organic pigments. Bead loading is typically 7085% of mill volume. Flow rate must be slow enough to give residence time but fast enough to avoid over-heating. A common mistake is running too fast you get heat, wear, and poor dispersion.</p>

<p>In markets where electricity is unreliable, batch milling (rather than continuous) is more practical. You mill a batch, check Hegman, and either let down or re-mill. This avoids the risk of a power cut leaving a continuous mill full of settled pigment.</p>

<h2>4. Monitoring Dispersion Quality: Hegman Gauge</h2>

<p>The Hegman gauge (also called a grind gauge or fineness of grind gauge) is a stainless steel block with a tapered groove, typically 0100 microns or 08 Hegman units. You draw down a sample with a scraper and read the point where specks first appear. The reading tells you the largest particle size in the batch.</p>

<p>For most interior and exterior architectural paints, a Hegman reading of 67 (approximately 1525 microns) is acceptable. For high-gloss or automotive coatings, you need 78. For textured or low-cost paints, 45 may be acceptable. Always take the reading after milling, before let-down, and record it in your batch log.</p>

<p>Hegman gauges are inexpensive (R 1,500R 4,000) and should be in every paint workshop. Without one, you are guessing. A simple visual check rubbing a drop between glass slides is not a substitute.</p>

<h2>5. Let-Down and Final Mixing</h2>

<p>Let-down is the addition of the remaining binder, water or solvent, thickener, and additives after dispersion and milling. The purpose is to bring the batch to the final viscosity, solids, and colour. Add let-down materials slowly under low shear to avoid shocking the dispersion.</p>

<p>Thickener addition is critical. Add pre-diluted thickener in small increments, checking viscosity with a Stormer viscometer or a simple flow cup. Over-thickening is hard to reverse; under-thickening causes settling. In hot climates, thickener demand may be higher because of evaporation.</p>

<p>Final mixing should be gentle 1015 minutes at low speed. High shear at this stage can break the dispersion and cause flocculation. After mixing, check pH (for water-based), viscosity, and colour against a standard.</p>

<h2>6. Filtration and Packaging</h2>

<p>Filtration removes undispersed particles, skin, and contamination before packaging. A simple bag filter or vibrating screen with 50100 micron mesh is adequate for most architectural paints. For premium products, use 2550 micron filtration.</p>

<p>Packaging should be clean, labelled, and sealed. In African markets, 5 L, 20 L, and 200 L containers are common. Check that containers are dry and free of rust. A dirty container ruins a good batch. Label with batch number, date, colour, and viscosity so you can trace complaints.</p>
