Module 3

The Envelope

Insulated panels, vapour barrier and floor. The envelope decides 60–80% of your running cost for the life of the room.

Read time: 30 minutesThen: Module 4 — Refrigeration
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Explain why the envelope, not the refrigeration unit, decides the running cost
  • Choose between polyurethane, polystyrene and polyisocyanurate panels on evidence
  • Size panel thickness honestly for your climate and your load
  • Install a vapour barrier correctly on the warm side and understand why wrong-side barriers fail
  • Design a floor that stops ground moisture and stays dry under the slab
  • Specify a cold room door assembly that will not be the reason your room fails
  • Produce a written envelope specification for your own cold room build

Lesson 3.1

Why the envelope is the whole business

Every kilogram of heat that enters the room must be removed by the refrigeration unit. That means every kilogram of heat that enters is electricity or diesel. Over a year, over ten years, the envelope is not the wall around your room. It is the meter that decides your power bill.

Consider the difference between a room built with good panels and a room built with bad ones:

The difference is not small. Over 10 years, the badly-built room can cost ,000 more to run than the well-built one, and the well-built envelope costs at most ,000–6,000 more upfront. The envelope pays for itself in 2–4 years. After that, it is money in the pocket every month, for as long as the room stands.

The rule: the refrigeration unit is easy to replace. The envelope is nearly impossible to replace. Spend on the envelope first, always.

What the envelope does, physically

The envelope has three jobs, and they are often confused:

  1. Insulation — slows the transfer of heat from outside to inside. This is the job of the foam core in the panel.
  2. Vapour barrier — stops warm, moist outside air from reaching the cold interior, where it would condense into liquid water or, worse, freeze into ice inside the panel. This is a separate layer from the insulation and it is the layer most often missing or wrongly installed.
  3. Air seal — stops warm air physically entering through gaps. Joints, cable penetrations, drain pipes, door frames. A 5 mm gap around a door frame costs you more in heat gain than 10 mm of missing panel thickness.

Insulation without a vapour barrier will saturate with water within a year in a humid climate, and wet foam conducts heat five to ten times better than dry foam. The insulation is still there, but it is no longer doing its job. This is why the vapour barrier is not optional and not the same thing as insulation.

Lesson 3.2

Panel materials compared honestly

Insulated panels are made of a rigid foam core bonded between two faces. The choice of core, faces, thickness and joint system decides everything.

The three core materials

CoreTypical thermal conductivity (λ)Fire behaviourCostNotes
Polystyrene (EPS)0.035–0.040 W/m·KMelts, does not readily support flame aloneLowestAbsorbs water; degrades if vapour barrier fails. Cheapest but least forgiving.
Polyurethane (PUR)0.022–0.028 W/m·KBurns; produces toxic smokeMiddleBest insulation per mm. The industry standard for cold rooms worldwide.
Polyisocyanurate (PIR)0.022–0.028 W/m·KBurns but forms a protective char layer; better than PUR in fireHighestBest performance. Worth the premium for rooms in buildings with occupants above or beside them.

For most rural 10-beast rooms in Africa, polyurethane (PUR) is the correct choice. It offers the best balance of insulation performance, cost and availability. Polystyrene is only acceptable if the panel has an excellent vapour barrier and the room is in a dry climate — which is not most of Africa.

Faces

The two faces of a panel determine its strength, hygiene and lifespan:

  • Painted steel (0.4–0.6 mm) — the standard. Easy to clean, food-safe, good strength. Beware thin gauge — a panel face of 0.3 mm dents easily under a trolley impact and creates a crevice that traps bacteria.
  • Stainless steel — the best for hygiene and corrosion, at 3–6× the cost of painted steel. Rarely justified for a 10-beast rural room, but standard for pharmaceutical and high-end food rooms.
  • GRP / fibreglass — durable in humid and coastal environments, food-safe, but not as common in Africa. Some suppliers use it as a wall face where corrosion is severe.
  • Aluminium — light and food-safe, but soft. Dents easily, corrodes in contact with dissimilar metals, and rarely used alone as a cold room face.

Thickness — the number that decides your running cost

Panel thickness must be sized for the temperature difference between inside and outside. For a room held at 2°C in a climate that reaches 35°C, the difference is 33 K. That is a significant load.

The following thicknesses are typical for rooms held at 0 to 4°C, in a climate where outside temperatures reach 30–35°C:

ApplicationWall thicknessCeiling thicknessFloor insulation
Small cool store (10°C)50–75 mm75–100 mmNone, or 25 mm
Chilled room (2–4°C)75–100 mm100–125 mm50 mm XPS under slab
Freezer room (−18°C)125–150 mm150–175 mm75–100 mm XPS under slab

The reason ceilings are thicker than walls is that warm air rises. The temperature difference across a ceiling is 2–5 K greater than across the walls, and the ceiling is the surface most vulnerable to condensation. Never make the ceiling thinner than the walls.

Do not use a general figure blindly. The correct thickness for your room depends on your specific climate, the internal temperature you hold, the panel core material, and how often the door opens. The table above is a starting point. Your supplier or a refrigeration engineer can calculate the exact thickness for your design conditions — a calculation takes ten minutes and is worth doing.

Joint systems

Panels join to each other by one of four methods:

  • Cam-lock — an internal locking mechanism that pulls two panels tightly together. The industry standard. Every joint should have a cam-lock every 600–800 mm of panel height, plus a rubber gasket in the groove to seal the joint.
  • Tongue-and-groove — a shaped edge that interlocks with the adjacent panel. Simpler, but needs a gasket and sealant to be vapour-tight.
  • Butt joint with cover strip — cheapest. Almost never vapour-tight in practice and it creates a food-trap crevice. Avoid in food rooms.
  • Rebated with sealant — used in refit and repair. Acceptable if the sealant is a food-grade silicone and the joint is closed tight while wet.

For a 10-beast room, specify cam-lock joints with gaskets on every panel. Every joint should be vapour-tight and food-safe on the internal face. Every corner should have a matching cam-lock edge, not a bare butt.

Lesson 3.3

The vapour barrier — where it goes and why it matters so much

This is the lesson that saves the most money over the life of the room, and it is the one most often got wrong.

The physics, briefly

Warm air holds more water vapour than cold air. When warm moist air cools, the water vapour condenses into liquid water. Every cold room has warm moist air outside, cold dry air inside, and an envelope in between. If warm moist air can reach a cold surface anywhere inside the envelope, condensation happens at that surface.

Where that cold surface is inside the room, the water drips onto the floor, and you see it — a puddle is annoying but harmless and easy to fix.

Where that cold surface is inside the panel — that is, if the warm moist air has penetrated the outer panel face and reached the cold interior of the panel's foam — the water condenses inside the foam. It has nowhere to go. It builds up. The panel becomes saturated. The foam's insulation value collapses. The panel's metal face corrodes from the inside. In a freezer, the water freezes inside the panel and mechanically splits the panel apart.

The vapour barrier exists to stop this. It is a continuous, sealed plastic membrane installed on the warm side of the insulation. On a cold room in a warm climate, that is the outside of the panel or the outside face of the walling that surrounds the panel.

The single worst mistake: barrier on the wrong side

If the vapour barrier is installed on the cold side (the inside face of the panel), warm air still penetrates the panel from outside, cools on the way through, and condenses just inside the vapour barrier. The barrier then traps the condensation against the panel, and the panel rots faster than it would have without any barrier at all.

The vapour barrier is a one-way gate. It must be on the warm side. On a cold room in a hot climate, that means the outer face. On a freezer in a cool climate, the same principle applies — barrier on the warm side, which is the outside in this case too.

The rule: vapour barrier goes on the warm side. Always. If you see a vapour barrier being installed on the cold side, stop the work and correct it before the panels are locked shut.

What the vapour barrier is made of

  • Polyethylene sheet — 0.15–0.25 mm, UV-stabilised. Cheap, effective, easy to install. The standard for most rooms.
  • Aluminium foil — higher performance, sometimes used as a reflective layer as well. More expensive, fragile, must be fully taped.
  • Self-adhesive membrane — used around penetrations and awkward junctions. Not the whole envelope.

Polyethylene sheeting is what most rooms use, and it is fine as long as it is continuous, sealed and on the warm side.

How to install it correctly

  1. Lay it in one continuous sheet where possible. Every join is a place water can find its way through.
  2. Lap all joins by at least 150 mm and seal them with a proper vapour-barrier tape, not duct tape. Duct tape dries, cracks and fails within two years.
  3. Wrap around all penetrations. Every pipe, cable, drain and door frame must have the barrier sealed to it. A single unsealed penetration is a leak in the whole envelope.
  4. Do not puncture it later. If you need to fix something to the wall after the barrier is in place, seal the hole with self-adhesive membrane. Never leave an unsealed puncture.
  5. Protect it from damage during the rest of the build. Once damaged, its performance drops sharply.
  6. Continue the barrier across the floor and up the walls as one membrane. Do not stop the barrier at the floor level. The floor and wall barrier must be one continuous envelope.
The most expensive mistake in cold room construction: a vapour barrier that is not continuous. A single unsealed penetration, a single join taped with the wrong tape, a single puncture that was never repaired — any one of these will eventually saturate the panel it sits behind. This is a mistake you discover three years later, when the foam has rotted, and the only fix is to rebuild the room.

Lesson 3.4

The floor — the part everyone forgets

The floor is the envelope element most often neglected, and the consequences are serious. In a tropical climate, the ground beneath a cold room can be at 25–30°C. That is a substantial heat load coming up through the floor, and it carries moisture with it.

The floor has four jobs

  1. Carry the load. A full 10-beast room holds 2,500 kg of carcasses plus the rails, the trolleys, and any personnel. The floor must take this without deflection or cracking.
  2. Insulate. Ground heat gain is a real load and cannot be ignored in a warm climate.
  3. Stop moisture. Rising damp and capillary action will wick water into the cold room floor, where it will freeze (in a freezer) or pool (in a chill room).
  4. Drain. Wash-down water must run off the floor to a drain, not stand in pools.

The correct floor build-up, from the ground up

Top: food-safe, cleanable, anti-slip finish ┌───────────────────────────────┐ │ Concrete wearing surface │ 75–100 mm, screeded to slope │ (sloped 1:100 to drain) │ ├───────────────────────────────┤ │ Vapour barrier (on warm side)│ Continuous, lapped, sealed ├───────────────────────────────┤ │ Insulation │ 50–75 mm XPS (extruded polystyrene) │ │ NOT EPS - EPS absorbs water ├───────────────────────────────┤ │ Vapour barrier (second line │ Optional but recommended in high water table areas │ of defence) │ ├───────────────────────────────┤ │ Blinding / compacted hardcore│ 100 mm compacted hardcore or lean mix ├───────────────────────────────┤ │ Undisturbed subsoil │

The vapour barrier goes beneath the insulation, not on top of it. Why? Because the moisture is rising from the ground. The warm side of the floor is the ground side. The barrier must sit on the ground side of the insulation to stop the rising moisture from reaching the cold slab.

Do not skip the floor vapour barrier. In a wet soil area, without a floor barrier, the insulation will slowly fill with water and the room will lose its efficiency within 12–24 months. In a freezer room, the water will freeze under the slab, expand, and physically crack the entire floor.

XPS vs EPS for floors

Extruded polystyrene (XPS) is the correct choice for floor insulation. It has a closed-cell structure that resists water absorption, and it retains its insulation value even in wet conditions. Expanded polystyrene (EPS) absorbs water and loses performance; it is not suitable for floor use below the water table or in wet ground.

XPS is more expensive than EPS. It is worth the difference. A floor that fails is not a small repair.

The surface — the part you actually touch

The top surface of the floor is what gets cleaned, walked on, and hit by trolleys. It must be:

  • Food-safe and cleanable — smooth, non-porous, no cracks or crevices
  • Anti-slip — a wet floor in a cold room is a slip hazard. Use a fine anti-slip finish, not a polished surface.
  • Durable — a 100 mm concrete wearing surface with a hardened top is standard. A resin coating is better but more expensive.
  • Sloped to drain — 1:100 is enough. Do not make the slope steep; you will be working on this floor.

Do not use tiles in a cold room floor. Grout lines crack with thermal cycling, tiles lift, and water gets underneath. A smooth poured or screeded surface is always better.

Lesson 3.5

The door — the single largest heat leak in the room

Everything else in the envelope is static. The door opens and closes. It is the largest single cause of heat gain in a working cold room, and it is the element most often under-specified.

What a good cold room door is

  • Thickness. The door should be at least as thick as the wall panels, and preferably 25 mm thicker. An insulated panel door of 100–125 mm is standard for a chilled room.
  • Construction. Rigid insulated panel with cam-lock edges, or a metal-framed panel door. Not a hollow metal door with foam sprayed into it.
  • Gasket. A magnetic or compression gasket running continuously around the door opening. When the door closes, the gasket must compress evenly all the way around. A gasket that leaks at one corner leaks air continuously.
  • Hinges. Heavy-duty, adjustable, mounted so the door swings smoothly and closes with a light push. Cheaper hinges sag and the door stops sealing within 12 months.
  • Safety release. This is non-negotiable. Every cold room door must be openable from the inside, even if someone is locked in. This is either a mechanical internal release handle, or a "safety ball" release. Never a cold room door that cannot be opened from inside.
  • Threshold. A raised threshold or a strip heater along the bottom to stop the door freezing shut in a freezer room. Not needed in a chill room, but relevant if you ever hold the room near 0°C.

Door position and swing

  • Doors must open outward. This is both a fire-safety and a cold-room-safety requirement. A door that opens inward traps a person who is inside when the door is blocked from outside.
  • Place the door on the side of the room furthest from the sun, or shaded by a porch. A door in full afternoon sun gets a solar heat load every time it is opened.
  • Do not place the door facing the prevailing wind. Every time the door opens, the wind pushes warm air in.
  • Provide a small airlock or curtain, even a plastic strip curtain, if the room will be opened more than 10 times per day.

Strip curtains — cheap and worth it

A set of plastic strip curtains inside the door costs –200 and cuts heat gain through the doorway by 40–70%. They are the single cheapest efficiency improvement you can make to a working cold room, and they pay for themselves within weeks in a busy operation.

The rule: a good door with a bad gasket is a bad door. Check the gasket every month. Replace it every 2–3 years. It costs almost nothing and it saves the compressor.

Lesson 3.6

Bringing it together — the envelope specification

When you approach a supplier, this is the one-page specification you hand over. It leaves nothing for them to guess at, and it is what you check the delivered material against.

Wall panels

  • Core: polyurethane (PUR), 40 kg/m³ density minimum
  • Thickness: 75–100 mm depending on climate and internal temperature
  • Faces: painted steel, 0.5 mm minimum, food-safe coating, internal face white
  • Joint: cam-lock every 600–800 mm vertical
  • Gasket: food-grade rubber or EPDM in every groove

Ceiling panels

  • Same as walls, but 25 mm thicker minimum
  • Panel span must not exceed the manufacturer's maximum unsupported span. Longer spans need intermediate support.

Vapour barrier

  • Polyethylene, 0.20 mm minimum, UV-stabilised
  • Installed on the warm side (outside of the panel or inside the walling that encloses it)
  • All joins lapped 150 mm minimum, taped with vapour-barrier tape, not duct tape
  • All penetrations sealed with self-adhesive membrane
  • Continuous with the floor barrier

Floor

  • Compacted hardcore or lean mix blinding, 100 mm
  • Vapour barrier, 0.20 mm, below insulation
  • XPS insulation, 50 mm minimum (75 mm for freezer), closed-cell
  • Concrete wearing surface, 75–100 mm, sloped 1:100 to drain, anti-slip finish

Door

  • Insulated panel door, 100–125 mm thick
  • Magnetic or compression gasket, continuous around the perimeter
  • Heavy-duty adjustable hinges
  • Internal safety release — mandatory
  • Opens outward
  • Strip curtain on the inside face

Penetrations and services

  • Every pipe, cable and drain through the envelope must pass through a sealed sleeve or duct
  • Sleeves should be installed during the panel erection, not drilled after
  • Every sleeve must be sealed with self-adhesive membrane both inside and outside the panel
  • Electrical cables must enter through a proper gland, not a raw hole
Photograph everything before the panels are locked shut. The vapour barrier, the floor insulation, the sleeves and the gaskets all disappear once the room is finished. Take photographs with a tape measure visible in shot. This is your record for warranty, insurance, and any repair you need in ten years.

Knowledge check — Module 3

1. Why does the envelope decide your running cost more than the refrigeration unit?

Correct: heat gain = running cost. Good insulation reduces heat gain. Every kilowatt-hour of heat the envelope lets in is a kilowatt-hour the compressor must remove.

2. On which side of the insulation must the vapour barrier go in a cold room in a hot climate?

Correct: warm side. The vapour barrier must be on the side warm moist air approaches from, which is the outside. On the cold side it traps moisture against the panel and makes the problem worse.

3. Why is XPS preferred over EPS for cold room floor insulation?

Correct: closed-cell, water-resistant. EPS absorbs water and loses its insulation value. In a floor it will fail within 1–2 years.

4. Why must every cold room door open outward?

Correct: safety. Outward-opening doors plus an internal release handle are non-negotiable safety requirements. A person locked in a cold room must be able to get out.

5. Why should you photograph the vapour barrier, floor insulation and sleeves before the panels are locked shut?

Correct: they disappear. Once the envelope is closed, you cannot inspect these layers without destroying the room. The photograph is your only evidence of how it was built.

Practical assignment — Module 3

Produce a written envelope specification for your own cold room, in the format of Lesson 3.6. This is a paper exercise plus a supplier exercise. It does not require building anything.

Submit:

  1. Your envelope specification, in the six sections of Lesson 3.6 (walls, ceiling, vapour barrier, floor, door, penetrations). Each section must state exactly what you will buy and why.
  2. At least two supplier quotations for insulated panels, with thickness, core material, face gauge and joint system stated. If the supplier cannot state these, the quotation is not usable — say so and find another supplier.
  3. A photograph of a sample panel or a panel joint, obtained from a supplier, showing the cam-lock and gasket.
  4. A one-page construction sequence for the envelope: what order the floor, walls, ceiling, barrier and door are installed, and where the vapour barrier is placed in that sequence.
  5. A photograph of a completed cold room door assembly from any local supplier or installation, with a note on the gasket condition, hinge type and internal release mechanism.
  6. One paragraph (150 words) naming the single envelope decision that most affects your long-term running cost, and stating what choice you made and why.

Marks are weighted toward points 1, 4 and 6. The envelope specification is the document you hand to a supplier. The construction sequence is what protects the vapour barrier. The paragraph is the decision you will live with for 20 years.
