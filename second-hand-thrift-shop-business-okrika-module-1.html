1. What is Okrika? The Second-Hand Clothing Trade in Africa

Okrika is the popular name for second-hand clothing in Nigeria, derived from the port city of Warri, where early imports arrived. In other parts of Africa, it's known as 'mitumba' in East Africa, 'salaula' in Zambia, and 'kafa' in Ghana. The trade involves importing used clothes from Europe, North America, and Asia, then selling them in local markets or boutiques. Africa imports over $1 billion worth of used clothing annually, with Nigeria alone importing about 100,000 tonnes per year. The business is a lifeline for millions, from importers who buy bales to street vendors who sell single items.

The okrika value chain starts with exporters in Western countries who collect donated or discarded clothes. These are sorted into grades and compressed into bales weighing 45kg to 100kg. Bales are shipped to major African ports like Lagos, Tema, Mombasa, and Durban. Local importers clear the goods and sell to wholesalers, who then sell to retailers. Retailers may sell from market stalls, shops, or even online via WhatsApp and Instagram. Some entrepreneurs wash, iron, and repackage items to sell as 'boutique' thrift, fetching higher prices.

Starting an okrika business requires little capital. You can begin with as little as 20,000 to buy a few pieces from a local market and resell. But to scale, you need to understand sourcing, pricing, and customer preferences. The trade is competitive, but demand is consistent because new clothes are expensive. In many African countries, over 70% of clothing purchases are second-hand. This module will guide you through the basics of the okrika market, from understanding customer motivations to setting goals and budgeting. By the end, you'll know how to spot opportunities and avoid common pitfalls.

Remember, okrika is not just about cheap clothes; it's about providing affordable fashion. Successful sellers treat it as a real business, not a side hustle. They track inventory, negotiate with suppliers, and market their goods. As you go through this course, keep in mind that the okrika trade is dynamictrends change, but the need for affordable clothing remains.

2. Why Okrika Sells: Customer Motivations and Buying Habits

Customers buy okrika for three main reasons: price, quality, and uniqueness. Price is the biggest driver. A new branded shirt might cost 15,000, while a similar okrika shirt sells for 2,000. In countries like Kenya, a new dress can cost KSh 3,000, but a mitumba dress is KSh 300. For low-income families, okrika is the only affordable option. Even middle-class buyers shop okrika to save money for other needs. Your pricing must reflect this: if you price too high, customers will go to the next stall.

Quality matters. Many okrika items are from fast-fashion brands that are still in good condition. Customers look for durable fabrics, intact zippers, and no stains. They also prefer brands they recognize, like Zara, H&M, or Gap. Some shoppers dig through piles for hours to find a gem. You can increase sales by sorting your stock: separate 'premium' items (branded, like-new) from 'budget' items. Premium can sell for 5-10 times the cost, while budget items move fast at low prices.

Uniqueness is another motivation. Okrika offers styles not available in local new-clothing stores. Vintage pieces, retro designs, and foreign labels attract fashion-forward buyers. University students and young professionals often hunt for okrika to create a personal style. They buy on weekends, especially Saturdays, and are active on social media. To reach them, post photos of your best items on WhatsApp Status, Instagram, or Facebook Marketplace. Use clear photos and include measurements. Offer delivery via motorbike or bus for a small fee.

Buying habits vary by location. In rural areas, customers buy for necessityschool uniforms, work clothes. In cities, they buy for fashion. Market days are busiest; in Nigeria, many okrika markets operate on specific days (e.g., Tuesday and Friday in some Lagos markets). Keep track of what sells: sizes, colors, and types. For example, men's jeans and women's blouses are always in demand. Children's clothes sell quickly because kids outgrow them fast. By understanding these motivations, you can stock the right items and set prices that attract repeat customers.

3. Types of Okrika: Clothes, Shoes, Bags, and Accessories

Okrika covers a wide range of items. Clothes are the most common: men's shirts, trousers, jeans, suits, and traditional wear; women's dresses, skirts, blouses, and underwear (though underwear is often new, not used). Children's clothing is a huge segment. When buying bales, you'll find mixed items, but some bales are sorted by category, like 'men's shirts' or 'children's wear'. Shoes are also popular: sneakers, sandals, heels, and boots. Bags include handbags, backpacks, and luggage. Accessories include belts, hats, scarves, and jewelry.

Each category has its own market. Clothes are the easiest to start with because they are lightweight and easy to display. Shoes require more care: check soles, stitching, and sizes. Many okrika shoes are still wearable but may need cleaning or minor repairs. Bags can be high-margin if you find branded ones like Gucci or Louis Vuitton (though most are fake). Accessories are small and cheap to ship, but they sell in high volumes. You can specialize in one category or offer a mix. Specializing helps you become known for something, e.g., 'the jeans lady' or 'the sneaker guy'.

When sourcing, know the grades. Bales are often graded: Grade A (like-new), Grade B (good condition with minor wear), Grade C (visible wear but usable). Grade A costs more but sells for higher prices. Some importers sell 'unsorted' bales, which are cheaper but contain a mix of grades and sometimes unsellable items (called 'chaff'). As a beginner, buy from local wholesalers who allow you to inspect before buying, even if the price per piece is higher. Avoid buying sealed bales unless you trust the supplier.

Display and storage: Clothes should be hung or folded neatly. Shoes should be paired and cleaned. Use shelves or tables to organize by size and type. For online sales, take photos against a plain background. Price tags help customers decide quickly. Remember, presentation increases perceived value. A clean, well-organized stall can charge 20-30% more than a messy one. Also, consider offering alterations or laundry services for an extra fee. This adds value and sets you apart.

4. Sourcing Locations: Local Markets vs. Import Bales

You can source okrika from two main channels: local markets and import bales. Local markets are the easiest for beginners. In Nigeria, markets like Yaba (Lagos), Kantin Kwari (Kano), and Ariaria (Aba) have wholesale sections where you can buy single pieces or small bundles. In Kenya, Gikomba Market in Nairobi is famous. In Ghana, Kantamanto Market in Accra. At these markets, you can inspect items, negotiate prices, and buy in small quantities. Prices are higher per piece, but you avoid the risk of buying a whole bale with unsellable items. Start here to learn what sells.

Import bales are for scaling up. You buy a sealed or opened bale from importers who clear containers at ports. A 45kg bale of mixed clothes might cost 30,000 to 80,000 in Nigeria, depending on grade and season. In Kenya, a bale of mitumba can cost KSh 15,000 to KSh 30,000. The advantage is lower cost per piece, but the risk is higher: you might get many low-quality items. To reduce risk, buy from reputable importers and ask to see samples from the bale. Some importers allow you to 'open' the bale and inspect before buying, but you'll pay a premium. Others sell 'pressed' bales (already sorted) for more.

Where to find importers? Visit major ports or border towns. In Nigeria, Lagos (Apapa, Tin Can) and Onne Port. In Kenya, Mombasa. In Ghana, Tema. You can also find them in large markets; they often have warehouses nearby. Networking is key: talk to other sellers, join okrika WhatsApp groups, and attend trade fairs. Some importers advertise on social media. Always verify credibilityask for references and start with a small purchase.

Timing matters. Bales arrive in cycles; prices are lower when supply is high (often after major holidays when donations spike). Also, consider shipping costs if you buy from abroad directly. For beginners, local markets are safer. Once you have a customer base and know what sells, transition to bales. You can also combine both: buy bales for popular items and fill gaps with local market purchases. Keep records of your sources, costs, and sell-through rates to make informed decisions.

5. Legal and Health Considerations for Selling Used Clothes

Selling okrika is legal in most African countries, but there are regulations you must follow. In Nigeria, the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) sets quality standards for imported used clothing. Some items are prohibited, like used underwear, socks, and nightwear, due to health risks. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) inspects mitumba imports. In Ghana, the Ghana Standards Authority regulates. You must ensure your goods are not damaged, stained, or carrying pests. If you import, you need an import license and must pay customs duties. As a retailer, you may need a business permit from your local government and pay taxes. Register your business to avoid fines and build trust.

Health considerations are critical. Used clothes can carry bacteria, fungi, or insects. Always wash and disinfect items before selling, especially those that touch the skin. Use hot water and detergent, or add disinfectant like Dettol. For shoes, clean with soap and sun-dry. This protects you and your customers. Some customers are allergic to certain fabrics or detergents, so rinse thoroughly. If you buy from bales, assume everything is dirty until cleaned. Set aside a cleaning area and wear gloves when handling unsorted items. Also, be aware of bedbugs and lice; inspect items carefully and treat if needed.

Ethical and environmental considerations: The okrika trade is criticized for dumping waste in Africa. As a seller, you can reduce waste by repairing minor damages, upcycling, or selling unsellable items as rags to local mechanics or cleaners. Don't burn unsold clothes; it pollutes the air. Instead, donate to charities or recycle. Also, be honest with customers about the condition of items. Label 'as is' items clearly. This builds trust and reduces returns.

Taxes: In many countries, small businesses with low turnover are exempt from VAT, but you may still need to pay income tax. Keep records of sales and expenses. In Nigeria, you can register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) as a business name for about 10,000. In Kenya, register with the Registrar of Companies. Consult a local business advisor or visit your tax office for guidance. Compliance may seem like a burden, but it protects you from legal issues and helps you grow.

6. Setting Your Business Goals and Start-Up Budget

Before you start, set clear goals. What do you want from this business? A side income of 50,000 per month? A full-time shop? Write down your goals: short-term (3 months), medium-term (1 year), long-term (3 years). For example, 'In 3 months, sell 100 items and make 100,000 profit.' 'In 1 year, open a small shop.' 'In 3 years, import my own bales.' Goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). This gives you direction and motivation.

Next, create a start-up budget. Estimate your costs: sourcing, transport, cleaning supplies, display materials, permits, and marketing. For a small start from a local market, you might spend: 20,000 on initial stock (50-100 items), 2,000 on transport, 1,000 on detergent and bleach, 5,000 on a second-hand table or rack, 2,000 on a business permit (if required), and 5,000 on data and airtime for online marketing. Total: about 35,000. If you buy a bale, it could be 50,000 to 100,000. Start small to test the market.

Funding sources: personal savings, family loans, or microfinance. In Nigeria, you can join a cooperative (esusu) or apply for a small business loan from organizations like LAPO or Accion. In Kenya, groups like SMEP or KWFT. Avoid high-interest loans. You can also start by selling on consignment: take items from a wholesaler, sell, and pay later. This reduces upfront cost.

Track your finances from day one. Use a notebook or a simple app like Excel or a free accounting app. Record every sale, expense, and inventory. Calculate your profit margin: (Selling price - Cost price) / Selling price. Aim for at least 50% margin on individual items. Reinvest profits to grow. Set aside 10% for emergencies. Review your goals monthly and adjust. Remember, okrika is a volume business; the more you sell, the more you earn. But don't sacrifice quality for quantity. Happy customers return and refer others. With discipline and smart planning, your okrika business can thrive.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Understanding the Okrika Business and Market

This practical reference consolidates the core knowledge you need before you buy your first bale or rent a stall. Okrika the widely used West and Central African term for second-hand clothing is not a side hustle by accident. In many African cities it is a multi-billion-dollar informal industry that clothes the majority of households and supports thousands of livelihoods, from port clearing agents to street hawkers and boutique resellers. Understanding the market structure, customer psychology, product categories, sourcing routes, and the legal/health rules will determine whether your business survives its first six months.

1. What Okrika Is and Why It Dominates African Retail

Okrika refers to used clothing, shoes, bags, and accessories imported in compressed bales, mostly from Europe, North America, and increasingly Asia. The name is popularly linked to the port of Okrika in Rivers State, Nigeria, though the trade itself is pan-African: "mitumba" in Kenya and Tanzania, "salaula" in Zambia, "kafa" in Ghana, "frip" in Francophone West Africa. The UN's trade data shows that used clothing exports to Africa are dominated by the EU, UK, and USA, with the continent importing billions of dollars' worth annually.

Why it sells: price. A new imported shirt in Lagos, Nairobi, or Accra can cost 510 times a comparable Okrika item. For a market where a large share of workers earn daily wages, affordability is the primary driver. Quality is the second driver older garments from Europe often use heavier cotton and better stitching than fast-fashion replacements.

Okrika customers are not a single market. They buy for different reasons, and your pricing, location, and stock mix must match the segment you serve.

3. Types of Okrika and Profitability

Not all Okrika is equal. Bales are graded, and the grade you buy determines your margin. "First grade" or "cream" bales contain wearable, near-new items; lower grades contain mixed, damaged, or unsellable stock. Your sorting skill is your real capital.

4. Sourcing: Local Markets vs. Import Bales

You have two main sourcing routes. Local wholesale markets (e.g., Katangua in Lagos, Kantamanto in Accra, Gikomba in Nairobi) let you buy single items or small bundles. Margins are thinner, but risk is low and you can test demand. Import bales are cheaper per item but require capital, clearing agents, and the ability to sort fast. A 4555 kg bale can cost anywhere from the equivalent of USD 80 to USD 400 depending on grade, origin, and country.

Start local. Buy small, sell fast, learn what moves. Only move to bales once you have consistent demand and cash flow.

SAFETY & LEGAL WARNING

Several African countries have restricted or banned used clothing imports to protect local textile industries Rwanda, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and others have imposed or proposed bans or higher tariffs. Selling imported Okrika without paying applicable customs duties or without a valid import licence can lead to seizure of goods, fines, or arrest. Additionally, unsorted bales may contain contaminated, mouldy, or chemically treated items. Always inspect stock, wash or sanitise before resale where possible, and comply with your country's import, health, and consumer-protection regulations. This is not legal advice confirm rules with your national revenue authority and standards bureau before importing.

5. Setting Business Goals and a Start-Up Budget

Before you spend a single naira, cedi, shilling, or rand, write down three numbers: your target monthly profit, your maximum start-up capital, and your break-even point. A simple Okrika stall can start with as little as USD 150300 in local-market stock; a bale-based operation typically needs USD 5002,000 including transport, clearing, and stall rent. Keep a 20% cash buffer for slow-selling stock and unexpected fees.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Choose your customer segment first low-income households, young professionals, or resellers. Your stock and location depend on this.
  • Start with local wholesale market purchases before committing to full import bales.
  • Learn to sort fast and honestly separate A-grade (resale), B-grade (discount), and C-grade (clearance/waste).
  • Check import and health regulations in your country before buying bales; bans and tariffs change.
  • Price by category, not by bale cost shoes and bags carry higher margins than mixed clothing.
  • Keep 20% of capital as a buffer for unsold stock, transport, and market fees.
  • Track daily sales and stock turnover if an item hasn't sold in 30 days, discount it.
  • Build a repeat-customer base through WhatsApp, Instagram, or a loyalty discount Okrika is a trust business.
