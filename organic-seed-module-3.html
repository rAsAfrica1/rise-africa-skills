🧠 Brain Opener

A seed business without legal paperwork is not a business. It is a risk. One letter from a regulator can shut you down. Here is what the law requires, in plain language.

  • Who regulates seed in your country?
  • What is QDS seed and why does it matter?
  • How much does organic certification cost?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

Seed is regulated by the national seed authority or agricultural ministry. QDS (Quality Declared Seed) is a lower-cost certification route used in Ethiopia, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, Mozambique, Malawi, and Zambia. Organic certification costs 8,000 to 15,000 dollars for third-party international certification, but only a fraction of that for Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS) or national organic certifications.

📖 Nyasha Almost Breaks the Law

Nyasha was twenty-six and had been selling organic tomato seed to neighbours for a year. She was making good money. She had never registered anything.

Then her seed reached a small export agent in Harare.

"Your seed is beautiful," the agent said. "But I cannot buy it."

"Why not?"

"Because you are not registered. If I export unregistered seed and it fails a test in Europe, I lose my licence. And you would be fined 5,000 dollars or imprisoned for a year."

Nyasha had never heard of any of this. She thought selling seed to neighbours was like selling tomatoes at the market.

She went to the Ministry of Agriculture. She sat in a queue for three hours. Then a young officer explained everything to her.

First, she needed to register as a seed producer. That was free but took three months.

Second, she needed to have her seed tested at a certified lab. That cost 30 dollars per sample.

Third, she needed to label each packet with variety name, batch number, germination rate, and her registration number.

Fourth, for organic certification, she could either pay 8,000 to 15,000 dollars for third-party certification - which she could not afford - or join a Participatory Guarantee System with other farmers in her area. The PGS route cost her only 200 dollars a year.

Nyasha joined a PGS in her district. She registered as a seed producer. She got her seed tested. She labelled properly.

Eight months later, she delivered her first export order - 40 kilograms of certified organic tomato seed at 90 dollars per kilogram.

She had almost lost everything by being ignorant. Instead, she was now the only PGS-certified organic tomato seed producer in her district.

💡 The Big Idea

The law is not your enemy - it is your protection

Every regulation exists because someone broke the rules. Registered producers can charge 3 to 5 times more than unregistered ones, because buyers trust them. Certification is expensive but it pays. The choice is not whether to comply - it is whether to be a professional or an amateur.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The three tiers of seed production

Tier one - Registered commercial seed producer. Highest regulation. Full certification, lab testing, branded packaging. Sells to exporters and major agro-dealers. Highest prices.

Tier two - QDS (Quality Declared Seed). Middle regulation. Used in Ethiopia, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, Mozambique, Malawi, Zambia. Lower cost than full certification. Sells to NGOs, cooperatives, local farmers. Good prices.

Tier three - Local seed exchange. Lowest regulation. Sells to neighbours and community. Smallest margins. Many farmers start here and stay too long - which is a mistake.

2

Organic certification - the two routes

Third-party certification. A company like Ecocert, Ceres, or Control Union inspects your farm, audits your records, tests your seed, and issues a certificate valid for one year. Cost: 8,000 to 15,000 dollars for the first year, then 5,000 to 8,000 dollars per year. This is what you need to export to Europe or America.

Participatory Guarantee System (PGS). A group of farmers inspect each other. A local council reviews and certifies. Cost: 100 to 500 dollars per year. Accepted for national organic markets and by some buyers. This is what most small African organic producers start with.

You can begin with PGS and upgrade to third-party later, once you have export contracts.

3

The three-year conversion period

You cannot claim organic on day one. The land must be free of banned chemicals for 2 to 3 years. During conversion, you farm organically but cannot label the produce organic. You can label it "in conversion" - which some buyers pay a small premium for.

The conversion clock starts the day you stop using chemicals. Write that date in a permanent record. It is the beginning of your organic business.

🛠 Hands-On - Write Your Registration Roadmap

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • A phone (to call the ministry)
  1. Find your country seed authority. It is usually under the Ministry of Agriculture.
  2. Call or visit them. Ask three questions: how to register as a seed producer, what testing is required, and what packaging rules apply.
  3. Write down every step, every fee, and every document needed.
  4. Find the nearest Participatory Guarantee System group. Ask how to join.
  5. Draw a timeline: month 1, month 2, month 3 - what happens when.
  6. Show the timeline to a family member. Ask them to hold you to it.
Why it works: Registration feels slow. But it opens doors that stay closed forever to unregistered producers.
