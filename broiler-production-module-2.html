1. Understanding Broiler Genetics and Commercial Breeds

When you decide to raise broilers for profit in Africa, the first business decision you make is not about feed or housing. It is about genetics. The breed or hybrid you choose determines how fast birds grow, how much feed they convert into meat, how well they survive heat and disease, and ultimately whether your farm makes money or loses it. This lesson gives you the practical knowledge to choose wisely. Broiler production is different from keeping indigenous chickens. Indigenous birds are hardy and survive on free range, but they take five to six months to reach two kilograms. Commercial broilers reach two to two and a half kilograms in five to seven weeks under good management. That speed is the source of profit, but it also means commercial birds demand more from you in housing, feed, water, and health care. The main commercial broiler genetics available across Africa come from a few global breeding companies. The most common are the Cobb 500, the Ross 308, the Arbor Acres, the Hubbard, and the Indian River. These are not breeds in the traditional sense. They are hybrid lines produced by crossing selected parent stocks. When you buy day-old chicks from a hatchery, you are buying the final generation of a carefully controlled breeding programme. You cannot reproduce them on your farm and get the same results. If you keep their eggs and hatch them, the offspring will be slower growing and less uniform. This is why successful broiler farmers buy fresh day-old chicks every cycle rather than breeding their own. The Cobb 500 is the most widely used broiler in Africa. It grows very fast, has excellent feed conversion, and performs well in open-sided houses common in tropical climates. It reaches around two point two kilograms in thirty-five days under good management. Its weakness is that it is sensitive to poor ventilation and heat stress, so it needs good housing. The Ross 308 is similar in performance and is popular in South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria. It has strong breast meat yield, which matters if you sell to processors or restaurants. The Arbor Acres is known for robust early growth and good livability, making it a forgiving choice for farmers who are still improving their management. The Hubbard and Indian River lines are often used where farmers want slightly slower growth but stronger legs and better tolerance of less controlled conditions. There are also slower-growing and coloured broilers such as the Kosmos, the Sasso, and the Kuroiler. These grow slower, reaching market weight in eight to twelve weeks, but they tolerate heat, poor housing, and free-range conditions much better. They are popular in markets where consumers prefer firmer meat or where farmers cannot afford high-input housing. In many African cities, however, the market rewards fast-growing white broilers because they are cheaper per kilogram and preferred by hotels, fast food outlets, and supermarkets. Your choice should follow your market, not your personal preference. If you sell to urban supermarkets and restaurants, choose a fast-growing white hybrid. If you sell to rural consumers who value taste and hardiness, consider a coloured or dual-purpose bird. Another factor is the parent stock and hatchery quality. A good breed performs badly if the chicks come from a poorly managed hatchery. Always buy from hatcheries that follow proper biosecurity, vaccinate breeding stock, and deliver chicks within a few hours of hatching. Ask about the age of the breeder flock. Chicks from very young or very old breeders are smaller and less uniform. Ask for the breed name and the hatch date. Reputable hatcheries provide this information without hesitation. In Africa, the cost of day-old chicks is a major part of your total investment. Prices vary widely. In Nigeria, a fast-growing broiler chick may cost between eight hundred and one thousand five hundred naira depending on the season and the hatchery. In Kenya, prices range from eighty to one hundred and fifty shillings. In Ghana, expect between eight and fifteen cedis. These prices rise during festive seasons and fall when demand is low. Smart farmers plan their production cycle around these price movements. Buying chicks when prices are low and selling when demand is high can add significant profit. Genetics also affects feed cost, which is sixty to seventy percent of your total production cost. A bird with a feed conversion ratio of one point six needs one point six kilograms of feed to produce one kilogram of meat. A bird with a ratio of two point zero needs two kilograms. On a farm producing one thousand birds, that difference can mean hundreds of kilograms of feed per cycle. Over a year, it can determine whether you stay in business. This is why paying a little more for quality chicks from proven genetics is almost always cheaper in the long run. Finally, understand that genetics and management work together. The best breed in the world will fail in a poorly ventilated, dirty house with contaminated water and irregular feeding. Before you invest in expensive chicks, make sure your brooder, feeders, drinkers, and ventilation are ready. Prepare your house at least one week before chicks arrive. Disinfect, dry, and warm the brooder. Have feed and water available from the moment the chicks arrive. The goal of this module is to help you match genetics to your market, your management capacity, and your budget. In the next lesson, we will look closely at how to identify healthy day-old chicks and avoid the costly mistake of buying weak stock.

2. Identifying Healthy Day-Old Chicks

The day your chicks arrive is one of the most important days on your farm. The quality of the chicks you receive determines how well they will grow, how much medication they will need, and how many will survive to market. Many farmers lose money not because of bad feed or disease, but because they accepted poor quality chicks from the hatchery. This lesson teaches you how to inspect, select, and receive day-old chicks like a professional. Start with the source. Buy chicks only from hatcheries that are registered, that vaccinate their breeder flocks, and that have a reputation for consistent quality. Visit the hatchery if possible. Look at the hygiene of the hatchery, the condition of the breeder houses, and how chicks are handled. A clean hatchery with healthy breeder birds is more likely to produce strong chicks. Ask other farmers about their experience with the hatchery. Word of mouth is powerful in the African poultry industry. When the chicks arrive, inspect them before you accept them. Do not let the driver leave until you have checked the whole batch. A healthy day-old chick is active and alert. It stands upright, moves around, and responds to sound and movement. It has bright, round, shiny eyes. Its navel or belly button is completely closed and clean, with no sign of infection, swelling, or a black scab. Its legs are straight and firm, not splayed or twisted. Its toes are well formed. Its beak is clean and properly shaped. Its down or fluff is dry, soft, and evenly spread. The chick feels warm and has a full, slightly rounded belly, which means it has absorbed the yolk properly. It should weigh what the breed standard says, usually around thirty-eight to forty-five grams for fast-growing broilers. Now look for signs of poor quality. Weak chicks are dull, sleepy, and huddle together. They may have wet, sticky down, which means they were chilled or hatched too early. Chicks with open or red navels, also called mushy chick disease, are infected and likely to die or perform poorly. Chicks with crooked legs, twisted necks, or crossed beaks have genetic or incubation problems and should be rejected. Chicks with dry, wrinkled legs or a shrunken appearance are dehydrated. Chicks with pasted vents or droppings stuck to their rear ends may have digestive problems. Any chick that cannot stand or that lies on its side should be removed immediately. Count the chicks. You paid for a certain number, so make sure you receive that number plus a small allowance for transport losses. Reputable hatcheries usually add two to three percent extra chicks to cover losses. If the count is short, raise it with the supplier immediately and in writing. Also check the chick boxes. They should be clean, well ventilated, and not overcrowded. If chicks have been packed too tightly or transported for too long without ventilation, they will be stressed and dehydrated. Transport is a major cause of chick mortality in Africa. Chicks should be transported in a clean, ventilated vehicle, ideally during the cool part of the day. Avoid transporting chicks in the heat of the afternoon or in the cold of early morning. The journey should be as short as possible. If you are far from the hatchery, arrange for the chicks to be delivered early in the morning or late in the evening. Never transport chicks in the boot of a car or in a closed, unventilated space. They can suffocate within minutes. When the chicks arrive at your farm, move them into the brooder immediately. The brooder should be preheated to thirty-two to thirty-five degrees Celsius at chick level. Have clean water with glucose or vitamins available. Dip the beaks of a few chicks into the water to teach them to drink. Then place them gently under the brooder. Watch their behaviour. If they spread out evenly and are active, the temperature is right. If they huddle under the heat source and chirp loudly, they are cold. If they move away from the heat source and pant, they are too hot. If they crowd in corners, there may be a draught. Adjust the brooder accordingly. Record everything. Write down the date, the hatchery, the breed, the number of chicks, the number of dead on arrival, and any abnormalities you noticed. This record helps you claim compensation if the hatchery is at fault, and it helps you decide whether to use that hatchery again. Keep the first week mortality below one percent. If you lose more than two percent in the first week, something is wrong. It could be chick quality, brooder temperature, water quality, or disease. Investigate immediately. Do not wait until half the flock is dead. In Africa, many farmers buy chicks from middlemen or traders rather than directly from hatcheries. This adds risk because you cannot verify the source, the age of the chicks, or the vaccination history. If you must buy from a trader, insist on seeing the hatchery invoice and the vaccination record. Better still, form a buying group with other farmers and order directly from the hatchery. This reduces cost and improves quality. Another practical tip is to order chicks in numbers you can manage. It is better to raise five hundred chicks well than two thousand chicks poorly. Overstocking leads to poor ventilation, wet litter, disease, and stress. Start small, learn the system, and expand as your skills and market grow. Finally, remember that chick quality is not only about the chick itself. It is also about how you receive and handle it. A strong chick can be ruined by a cold brooder, dirty water, or delayed feeding. Prepare everything before the chicks arrive. Clean and disinfect the brooder. Warm it for at least twenty-four hours. Fill the drinkers with clean water and add glucose or electrolytes. Fill the feeders with good quality starter feed. Then receive the chicks calmly and carefully. Your attention on day one pays off for the entire cycle.

3. Matching Breed Choice to Market Demand

Choosing a broiler breed without understanding your market is like planting maize without knowing who will buy it. In Africa, broiler markets are diverse. A farmer selling to a rural community has different needs from a farmer supplying a supermarket in Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra. This lesson helps you analyse your market and choose the breed that fits it. Start by identifying your customers. Are they individual households, local restaurants, hotels, supermarkets, fast food outlets, or processors? Each customer group has different preferences. Households in rural areas often prefer firmer meat with more flavour. They may be willing to pay more for coloured or slower-growing birds. Urban households, especially middle and high income, often prefer white, soft, fast-growing broilers because they cook quickly and are cheaper per kilogram. Restaurants and hotels usually want uniform, well-fleshed birds with good breast meat. Supermarkets want consistent size, clean packaging, and reliable supply. Processors want high breast meat yield and uniform carcasses. Once you know your customer, you can choose the breed. Fast-growing white hybrids like Cobb 500, Ross 308, and Arbor Acres are best for urban markets, supermarkets, and restaurants. They reach market weight quickly, have high feed conversion, and produce uniform carcasses. Slower-growing coloured breeds like Sasso, Kosmos, and Kuroiler are better for rural markets, organic markets, and customers who value taste and hardiness. Some farmers raise both types to spread risk and serve different customers. Consider the market weight your customers want. In many African cities, the preferred live weight for broilers is between one point eight and two point five kilograms. Some markets prefer smaller birds around one point five kilograms, especially for single meals. Others prefer larger birds for parties and restaurants. Choose a breed and a slaughter age that matches this demand. If you sell at the wrong weight, you may be forced to accept a lower price. Think about the season. Demand for chicken rises during festive periods such as Christmas, Eid, Easter, and Independence Day celebrations. Prices also rise. If you can time your production so that birds are ready during these periods, you can earn more. This means planning your chick placement four to six weeks before the festive season. Work backwards from the market date. For example, if you want to sell during Christmas week, place your chicks in the second week of November for a fast-growing breed. Also consider the cost of production. Fast-growing breeds need high quality feed and good housing. Slower-growing breeds can tolerate cheaper feed and simpler housing, but they take longer and eat more total feed. Calculate the cost per kilogram of meat produced, not just the cost per bird. Sometimes a slower breed with lower input costs can be more profitable if your market pays a premium for it. Study your competitors. What breeds are other farmers in your area raising? What prices are they getting? What gaps exist in the market? Maybe everyone is selling live birds, but restaurants want dressed and chilled chicken. Maybe everyone is selling large birds, but there is demand for small, affordable birds. Finding a niche can protect you from price competition. Build relationships with buyers before you stock. Talk to restaurant owners, hotel chefs, supermarket buyers, and market traders. Ask them what they want and when they want it. If possible, get a verbal or written commitment before you place your chicks. This reduces the risk of having birds ready with no buyer. Many African farmers fail because they produce first and look for market later. Do the opposite. In some countries, there are formal grades and standards for broiler carcasses. Learn these standards if you plan to sell to formal buyers. Uniformity matters. A batch of birds with mixed sizes is harder to sell at a good price. Fast-growing hybrids are more uniform than local breeds, which is another reason they dominate commercial markets. Consider the availability of chicks. It is no use choosing a breed that is not available in your area or that is only available at certain times of the year. Check with hatcheries and suppliers before you commit. Some breeds are imported and may be expensive or delayed. Local hatcheries may carry only one or two breeds. Plan your production around what you can reliably source. Also think about disease pressure in your area. Some breeds are more resistant to common diseases like Newcastle disease, infectious bursal disease, and coccidiosis. No breed is fully resistant, but some cope better. Talk to veterinary officers and experienced farmers about which breeds perform best in your local conditions. Keep records of your performance. After each cycle, write down the breed, the number of chicks, the feed used, the mortality, the average weight, and the price received. Over time, these records will show you which breed is most profitable for your farm. Do not rely on guesswork. Data is your best business adviser. Finally, be flexible. Markets change. A breed that is profitable this year may not be next year. New breeds and hybrids are introduced regularly. Stay informed through farmer groups, extension officers, and industry publications. Attend agricultural shows and poultry seminars. Talk to other farmers. The more you know, the better your decisions. In summary, breed choice is a business decision. Match the breed to your market, your management capacity, and your budget. Do not choose a breed because it is popular or because a friend recommends it. Choose it because it fits your customers and your farm. That is the path to profitable broiler production in Africa.

4. Sourcing Chicks and Working with Hatcheries

Your relationship with your chick supplier can make or break your broiler business. In Africa, the chick supply chain is often informal, and quality varies widely. This lesson teaches you how to find reliable hatcheries, place orders, and protect yourself from poor quality chicks. Start by identifying hatcheries in your region. Ask other farmers, veterinary officers, feed suppliers, and extension workers for recommendations. Visit the hatcheries if possible. Look at their facilities. A good hatchery is clean, well organised, and follows strict biosecurity. You should see proper egg storage, incubators, hatchers, and chick handling areas. The staff should be knowledgeable and willing to answer your questions. Ask about their breeder flocks. Where are they located? How old are the breeders? What vaccines do they receive? How often are they tested for diseases? A hatchery that cannot answer these questions clearly is a risk. Ask about their hatch rate and chick quality. A good hatchery has a hatch rate above eighty percent and produces uniform, strong chicks. Ask for references from other farmers. Call those farmers and ask about their experience. Did the chicks perform well? Was the count accurate? How did the hatchery handle complaints? This due diligence takes time, but it saves money and heartache later. When you are ready to order, place your order early. Good hatcheries are often booked weeks in advance, especially before festive seasons. Provide clear details: breed, number of chicks, date of delivery, and place of delivery. Confirm the price and payment terms in writing. If possible, pay a deposit and the balance on delivery. Avoid paying the full amount in advance to a supplier you do not know well. On delivery day, inspect the chicks as described in the previous lesson. Count them. Check their condition. If there are problems, document them with photos and notes. Do not accept chicks that are clearly sick or weak. If you accept them, you may not be able to claim compensation later. If the hatchery is far away, arrange for transport carefully. Use a clean, ventilated vehicle. Avoid overcrowding. Transport during cool hours. If you are buying a small number of chicks, you may be able to use public transport, but this is risky. Chicks can be exposed to heat, cold, dust, and stress. It is better to pay for a dedicated vehicle or to combine orders with other farmers to share transport costs. Consider forming a farmer group or cooperative to buy chicks together. This has several advantages. You can order in larger quantities and negotiate better prices. You can share transport costs. You can share information about hatchery quality. You can support each other with management advice. Many successful broiler farmers in Africa belong to farmer groups. If you cannot find a good hatchery nearby, you may need to look further afield. Some farmers import chicks from Europe, South Africa, or Egypt. Importing is expensive and complicated. You need permits, customs clearance, and specialised transport. It is usually only worthwhile for large operations. For most small and medium farmers, buying from a reputable local hatchery is the best option. Be wary of very cheap chicks. If a price seems too good to be true, it probably is. Cheap chicks may be from unvaccinated breeders, may be old or weak, or may be a different breed than advertised. Saving a small amount on chicks can cost you a large amount in mortality, medication, and poor growth. Quality pays. Also be wary of middlemen who sell chicks without proper storage or transport. They may buy from hatcheries and resell at a markup, but they may not handle the chicks well. If you must buy from a middleman, ask for the hatchery invoice and the vaccination record. Check the chick boxes for the hatchery name and hatch date. If the information is missing, walk away. Keep records of every purchase. Write down the date, hatchery, breed, number of chicks, price, and any problems. This helps you track performance and make better decisions in future. If a hatchery consistently provides good chicks, build a long-term relationship. Loyal customers often get priority during peak seasons and better prices. If a hatchery provides poor chicks, do not go back. There are other suppliers. Do not let loyalty to a bad supplier ruin your business. Payment terms matter. Some hatcheries require full payment before delivery. Others accept a deposit and balance on delivery. Some offer credit to regular customers. If you need credit, discuss it openly and agree on terms. Do not default on payments. In the African poultry industry, reputation is everything. A farmer who pays on time and treats suppliers fairly will always find good chicks. Finally, think about biosecurity when receiving chicks. The delivery vehicle should not enter your farm if possible. Receive the chicks at the gate or at a designated receiving area. Clean and disinfect the chick boxes before disposal. Wash your hands and change your clothes after handling new chicks. Keep new chicks away from older birds. These simple steps reduce the risk of introducing disease. In summary, sourcing chicks is a business relationship. Choose your hatchery carefully. Order early. Inspect on delivery. Pay fairly. Keep records. Build trust. Do these things and you will have a reliable supply of good chicks, which is the foundation of profitable broiler production.

5. Brooding Management for Day-Old Chicks

The first seven days of a chick's life determine its future. A chick that is well brooded will grow faster, resist disease better, and convert feed more efficiently. A chick that is poorly brooded will struggle for the rest of the cycle, and no amount of good feed or medication will fully compensate. This lesson covers practical brooding management for African conditions. Brooding is the art and science of providing warmth, water, feed, air, and space to young chicks. The goal is to keep chicks comfortable and healthy during their first week, when they cannot regulate their own body temperature. Start with preparation. The brooder house must be cleaned and disinfected at least one week before chicks arrive. Remove old litter. Wash walls, floors, and equipment. Disinfect with a suitable product. Allow to dry. Then spread fresh, dry litter such as wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped straw. Do not use wet, mouldy, or dusty litter. It causes respiratory problems. Litter should be five to eight centimetres deep. Create a brooding area using cardboard, plywood, or metal sheets. This keeps chicks close to heat and water and protects them from draughts. The brooding area should be about half a square metre per fifty chicks for the first week. As chicks grow, expand the area. Heat is critical. For the first three days, the temperature at chick level should be thirty-two to thirty-five degrees Celsius. Reduce it gradually to about thirty degrees by the end of the first week. By the end of the second week, it should be around twenty-eight degrees. By the fourth week, chicks can usually cope with normal ambient temperatures if the house is well ventilated. Use a reliable heat source. In Africa, common options include charcoal stoves, kerosene lamps, gas brooders, and electric bulbs. Each has advantages and disadvantages. Charcoal is cheap but produces carbon monoxide, which can kill chicks if the house is not ventilated. Kerosene is convenient but expensive and can cause fires. Gas is clean and controllable but requires equipment and fuel supply. Electric bulbs are simple but useless during power cuts. Many farmers use a combination. Whatever you choose, make sure it is safe and reliable. Watch the chicks to judge temperature. If they are spread out evenly and active, the temperature is right. If they huddle under the heat source and chirp loudly, they are cold. Increase heat or reduce draughts. If they move away from the heat source and pant, they are too hot. Reduce heat or improve ventilation. If they crowd in corners, there is a draught. Fix it. Water is as important as heat. Chicks can survive a day without feed but not without water. Provide clean, fresh water at all times. Use shallow drinkers that chicks can reach easily. Add glucose or electrolytes to the first water to give chicks energy. Dip the beaks of a few chicks into the water to teach them to drink. Wash and refill drinkers at least twice a day. Never let drinkers run dry. Feed should be available from day one. Use a good quality broiler starter feed with high protein, usually twenty-two to twenty-four percent. Offer feed in shallow trays or feeder lids for the first few days, then move to proper feeders. Keep feeders clean and free of wet or mouldy feed. Do not overfill feeders. Chicks waste feed if feeders are too full. Feed little and often for the first week. Good ventilation is essential. Chicks need fresh air but must not be exposed to draughts. Open vents or windows slightly to allow air exchange. Avoid strong winds blowing directly on chicks. In hot climates, ventilation helps remove excess heat and moisture. In cold climates, it removes ammonia and moisture while retaining heat. Balance is key. Use a thermometer and hygrometer to monitor temperature and humidity. Humidity should be around sixty to seventy percent in the first week. Too low and chicks dehydrate. Too high and litter gets wet, leading to disease. Stocking density matters. Do not overcrowd. For the first week, allow about twenty to twenty-five chicks per square metre. Reduce density as chicks grow. By market age, allow about ten to twelve birds per square metre in open-sided houses. Overcrowding causes stress, poor growth, and disease. Light helps chicks find feed and water. Provide continuous light for the first three days, then reduce to twenty hours of light and four hours of darkness. This gives chicks rest and saves energy. Use dim light to prevent feather pecking. Biosecurity starts on day one. Keep visitors away from the brooder. Use footbaths. Wash hands before entering. Do not mix chicks from different sources. Remove dead chicks immediately. Dispose of them properly by burning or burying. Keep rodents and wild birds out of the house. Record everything. Write down daily temperature, mortality, feed and water consumption, and any signs of disease. These records help you spot problems early and improve your management. Common brooding problems include pasted vents, starve-outs, and omphalitis. Pasted vent occurs when droppings stick to the chick's rear, blocking the vent. Gently clean with warm water. Starve-outs are chicks that fail to find feed or water. They are small, weak, and often die. Help them by dipping beaks and placing them near feed. Omphalitis is navel infection. It comes from dirty hatchery conditions or dirty brooders. Prevent it with good hygiene. If you see many weak or dying chicks in the first week, check temperature, water, feed, and ventilation. Do not assume it is disease. Most first-week mortality is caused by management, not disease. Fix the management first. Finally, prepare for the transition out of the brooder. By the end of the second week, chicks should have more space and less heat. By the third week, they should be off supplementary heat in most African climates. Expand the brooding area gradually. Provide more feeders and drinkers. Keep the litter dry. Continue good biosecurity. A well-brooded chick is a profitable chick. Invest time and attention in the first week, and the rest of the cycle will be much easier.

<h3 style="color:#c0392b;margin-top:0;"> Safety Warning: Vaccine Handling and Chemical Use</h3>
<p><strong>Vaccines:</strong> Newcastle Disease and Infectious Bursal Disease vaccines are live biological products. Handle them as follows:</p>
<ul>
<li>Store between +2C and +8C. Never freeze. Never expose to direct sunlight.</li>
<li>Reconstitute only with the diluent supplied. Use within 2 hours of mixing.</li>
<li>Administer via eye drop, nostril, or drinking water according to the manufacturer's instructions. Do not inject live vaccines unless the label specifies injection.</li>
<li>Dispose of used vials and needles in a sharps container or sealed plastic bottle. Never re-use needles.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Disinfectants and chemicals:</strong> Formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, and concentrated chlorine are hazardous. Wear gloves, eye protection, and a face mask. Never mix chlorine with acid-based cleaners toxic gas will be released. Store all chemicals in original labelled containers, locked away from children and feed. If a chemical splashes on skin or eyes, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention.</p>
<p><strong>Antibiotic use:</strong> WHO and FAO discourage routine prophylactic antibiotics in broiler production. Use only under veterinary prescription, observe withdrawal periods, and never use antibiotics classified as critically important for human medicine (e.g., colistin, fluoroquinolones) unless legally permitted and prescribed.</p>
</div>

<h2>Record-Keeping: The 5-Minute Daily Log</h2>

<p>Profit in broiler production is managed through records, not memory. Keep a simple daily log with these columns: date, age (days), mortality (number and cause if known), feed consumed (kg), water consumed (litres), temperature (min/max), and any treatments or observations. At the end of each cycle, calculate: feed conversion ratio (FCR), mortality %, average daily gain, and cost per kg live weight.</p>

<p>A 1,000-bird flock with FCR of 1.65 and 4% mortality is performing well. FCR above 1.85 or mortality above 7% signals a management problem usually in brooding, ventilation, or feed quality. Your records are also required by many buyers, processors, and microfinance institutions.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pre-arrival:</strong> Brooder pre-heated to 3234C at chick level (not ceiling level) at least 4 hours before chicks arrive. Litter depth 58 cm, dry and non-slip.</li>
<li><strong>Chick inspection:</strong> Score 50 chicks on the 5-point system. Reject or discount any batch scoring below 7.5/10. Record hatchery batch number.</li>
<li><strong>First 24 hours:</strong> Chicks must find feed and water within 2 hours. Check crop fill at 24 hours at least 95% of chicks should have full, soft crops.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Reduce by 1C every 23 days from 3234C at day 0 to 2426C by day 14. Watch chick behaviour, not just the thermometer.</li>
<li><strong>Water:</strong> Clean water available at all times. Use 1 drinker per 80100 chicks in week 1. Sanitise drinkers daily.</li>
<li><strong>Feed:</strong> Use broiler starter crumbles (or mash if crumbles unavailable). Provide 1 feeder tray per 100 chicks for the first 3 days, then transition to tube feeders.</li>
<li><strong>Vaccination:</strong> Follow the hatchery and veterinary schedule. Typical: Newcastle at day 710, IBD at day 14. Record vaccine batch and date.</li>
<li><strong>Daily records:</strong> Log mortality, feed, water, temperature, and observations every day. Review weekly and act on trends.</li>
</ul>
