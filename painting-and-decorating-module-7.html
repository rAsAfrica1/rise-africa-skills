1. Surface Preparation in Tropical and Arid Climates

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important factor that determines whether a paint job lasts two years or ten: surface preparation. In Africa, surfaces behave differently from the temperate climates where most paint manuals are written. You deal with high humidity, intense UV, harmattan dust, salt-laden coastal air, and substrates that range from sun-baked laterite walls to porous cement blocks and old oil-based coatings that have gone chalky. Your reputation and your profit margin both depend on getting preparation right. A decorator who skips preparation will be called back for peeling, blistering, and flaking, and callbacks eat your profit faster than any other problem. Start every job with a proper assessment. Walk the site with the client and note the substrate type, the age of the building, the presence of damp, cracks, efflorescence, mould, rust, or previous coatings. In coastal cities like Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban, salt attack is constant. In the Sahel and the Horn, thermal shock between 40 degree days and cool nights opens hairline cracks. In the forest belts of West and Central Africa, biological growth and persistent damp are your main enemies. Your assessment should produce a written scope: what you will clean, what you will repair, what you will prime, and what you will exclude. This protects you when a client later blames you for an old structural crack you never agreed to fix. Cleaning comes first. Remove all loose material, dust, grease, and biological growth. For interior walls, a stiff brush and a damp cloth remove harmattan dust, which is fine enough to act as a release agent under paint. For exterior walls, use a pressure washer where water supply allows, but be careful with old render, which can be blasted away. In areas with limited water, use dry brushing followed by a damp sponge. Kill mould and algae with a diluted bleach solution or a proprietary fungicidal wash, then rinse and allow to dry completely. Never paint over active mould; it will bleed through and return within months. For efflorescence, the white salt deposits common on cement and block walls in humid regions, brush off the crystals dry, then wash with a mild acid solution if the wall is sound, and rinse thoroughly. Efflorescence indicates moisture movement, so investigate the source: leaking gutters, rising damp, or a cracked roof. Painting over it without fixing the cause is a waste of your client money and your time. Repair defects next. Fill cracks with a suitable filler. For hairline cracks, a flexible acrylic filler works well because it moves with the wall. For larger cracks, open them into a V shape, remove dust, and fill with a sand-cement mortar or a two-part epoxy filler for structural cracks. Allow proper curing time, which in humid conditions can be longer than the label suggests. Replace crumbling render and patch holes with a mortar mix that matches the existing wall. In many African markets, local masons can supply the right sand and cement ratio, but you must supervise the mix. Too much cement makes the patch brittle; too little makes it weak. After patching, sand smooth and dust off. Priming is the bridge between preparation and finishing. Choose the primer for the substrate and the topcoat. Alkali-resistant primer is essential on new cement, plaster, and concrete because these substrates are alkaline and can burn or saponify oil-based paints. In humid zones, use a fungicidal primer on walls prone to mould. On metal, use a rust-inhibiting primer, and on galvanised steel or aluminium, use an etch primer or a dedicated galvanised primer, because ordinary primers will not adhere. On wood, use a wood primer or an aluminium wood primer to block tannins from species like iroko or mahogany, which can bleed through and stain the finish. Moisture testing is non-negotiable. Use a moisture meter if you have one, or tape a square of plastic sheet to the wall for 24 hours and check for condensation underneath. If the wall is damp, do not paint. Find the source and fix it. Painting damp walls traps moisture and guarantees failure. In many African cities, rising damp is common in older buildings without damp-proof courses. In these cases, recommend a breathable system or a damp-proof membrane rather than a standard paint. Finally, document everything. Take before and after photographs, keep a written scope, and note the products and batch numbers you use. This protects you in disputes and helps you price future jobs accurately. Preparation is not glamorous, but it is where professionals separate themselves from roadside painters. Do it thoroughly, and your topcoats will look better, last longer, and earn you referrals. In the next lesson we will look at choosing the right paint systems for African conditions.

2. Choosing Paint Systems for African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2. Now that your surface is prepared, you must choose the right paint system. A paint system is not just a colour; it is a combination of primer, undercoat, and topcoat designed to work together on a specific substrate in a specific environment. In Africa, the range of environments is enormous, from the humid coast to the dry interior, from high-UV highveld to tropical rainforest. Choosing the wrong system is one of the most common causes of premature failure, and it is entirely avoidable. Start with the substrate. Cement, plaster, and concrete are the most common wall surfaces across the continent. They are porous, alkaline, and prone to cracking. For these, use an alkali-resistant primer followed by a quality acrylic or PVA emulsion. In dry, high-UV regions, choose a 100 percent acrylic exterior emulsion rather than a cheap PVA, because acrylics resist UV degradation and chalking far better. In humid coastal areas, choose a system with good fungicidal and water-repellent properties. Many international brands sell region-specific exterior emulsions for Africa; ask your supplier for the technical data sheet and read the UV and fungal resistance ratings. Wood is common for doors, windows, shutters, and ceilings. In Africa, wood is exposed to intense sun, driving rain, and termites. For exterior wood, use a wood primer, an undercoat, and a flexible exterior gloss or satin. In high-UV areas, a flexible acrylic or hybrid coating performs better than a rigid oil-based gloss, which can crack as the wood moves. For interior wood, a solvent-based or water-based gloss is fine, but ensure the wood is dry and treated against borers. In many markets, local timber such as mahogany, iroko, and teak contains natural oils and tannins. Use an aluminium wood primer or a stain-blocking primer to prevent bleed-through. Metal is another major substrate. Roof sheets, gates, grilles, and railings are everywhere. For ferrous metal, remove rust by wire brushing or sanding to bare metal, apply a rust-inhibiting primer, then an undercoat and a gloss or direct-to-metal topcoat. For galvanised steel, which is common for roofing and fencing, use an etch primer or a specialised galvanised primer. Painting galvanised steel without the right primer is a classic mistake; the paint will flake off within months. For aluminium, use a dedicated aluminium primer or a self-etching primer. In coastal areas, metal corrodes faster, so use a system with high salt resistance and inspect and maintain it annually. Consider the climate zone. Coastal zones have high humidity, salt, and biological growth. Choose fungicidal, salt-resistant, and breathable systems. Arid zones have high UV, high temperatures, and dust. Choose UV-stable, heat-resistant, and dust-shedding finishes. Tropical zones have heavy rain and persistent damp. Choose water-repellent, fungicidal, and quick-drying systems, because rain can arrive without warning. Highland zones have cooler temperatures and strong UV. Choose flexible coatings that can handle thermal movement. In all zones, avoid cheap paints that look good for six months and fail within a year. Your client may push for the cheapest option, but explain the total cost of ownership. A premium paint that lasts eight years is cheaper than a budget paint that needs recoating every two years, especially when you factor in labour and scaffolding. Colour choice matters too. Dark colours absorb heat and can cause thermal stress on walls and metal. In hot regions, recommend light colours for exteriors to reduce heat gain and extend coating life. In dusty regions, mid-tones hide dust better than pure white. In humid regions, light colours reduce mould visibility. Always confirm colour choices with the client in writing, and where possible, apply a sample patch and let it dry before final approval. Sheen level is another decision. Matt finishes hide surface imperfections but are harder to clean. Silk and satin are easier to clean and are good for interiors. Gloss is durable and washable but highlights defects. For exteriors, a satin or low-sheen finish often balances durability and appearance. For interiors in dusty areas, a washable matt or silk is practical. Finally, think about availability and consistency. In many African markets, supply chains can be unpredictable. Choose products that are readily available locally, and buy enough for the whole job in one batch to avoid colour and sheen mismatch. Record batch numbers. If you must use different batches, mix them together in a large container before use. Keep a small quantity of each product for touch-ups. In the next lesson we will cover application techniques that deliver a professional finish in the field.

3. Application Techniques for Professional Results

Welcome to Lesson 3. Good preparation and the right paint system will still fail if your application is poor. In this lesson we focus on practical application techniques that deliver a professional finish in African field conditions. Whether you are brushing a door frame in Accra, rolling a wall in Nairobi, or spraying a warehouse in Johannesburg, the principles are the same: control the film thickness, maintain a wet edge, and work with the climate, not against it. Start with tools. Buy the best brushes and rollers you can afford. Cheap brushes shed bristles, leave brush marks, and waste paint. For solvent-based paints, use natural bristle brushes. For water-based paints, use synthetic bristle brushes. For rollers, choose the nap length for the surface: short nap for smooth walls, medium nap for light texture, and long nap for rough render or brick. Keep a range of sizes: a 100mm brush for cutting in, a 50mm brush for trim, a 225mm roller for walls, and a small roller for edges. Clean tools immediately after use. In many African markets, good tools are available but expensive, so train your team to care for them. Brushing is the most common method for trim, edges, and small areas. Load the brush about one-third of the bristle length, tap off excess, and apply in long, even strokes. Work the paint into the surface, then lay off with light strokes in one direction to smooth the finish. Do not over-brush, especially with fast-drying paints, because it causes lap marks. For cutting in, use a steady hand and a angled brush. Masking tape helps, but remove it while the paint is still slightly wet to avoid pulling the film. Rolling is the fastest way to cover large wall areas. Pour paint into a roller tray, load the roller evenly, and roll in a W pattern to spread the paint, then fill in with parallel strokes. Maintain a wet edge: do not let the paint dry at the edge of your section before you continue. Work in sections of about one metre square, and always finish a wall in one continuous session where possible. In hot, dry conditions, paint dries fast, so work quickly and in smaller sections. In humid conditions, paint dries slowly, so allow more time between coats and avoid overloading the roller, which can cause runs. Spraying is efficient for large, smooth surfaces such as ceilings, doors, and metalwork. Airless sprayers are ideal for emulsions on big jobs. HVLP sprayers are good for fine finishes. Always mask adjacent surfaces, and use proper respiratory protection, especially with solvent-based paints. Spray in even passes with a 50 percent overlap, keeping the gun perpendicular to the surface and at a consistent distance. In dusty environments, spray when the air is calm, and consider a temporary screen. In humid environments, watch for blushing, a milky haze caused by moisture trapped in the film, especially with solvent-based lacquers. Add a retarder if necessary, or wait for drier conditions. Climate control is critical. In hot weather, paint in the early morning or late afternoon to avoid direct sun, which can cause the paint to dry too fast and crack. In cold weather, most paints need a minimum temperature to cure; check the data sheet. In humid weather, avoid painting when relative humidity is above 85 percent, because moisture can be trapped under the film. In dusty weather, such as harmattan season in West Africa, cover surfaces and avoid painting outdoors when possible. If you must paint, use a fast-drying system and clean the surface immediately before coating. Film thickness matters. Too thin, and the paint will not protect the substrate. Too thick, and it may crack, run, or fail to cure. Follow the manufacturer coverage rate, which is usually given in square metres per litre per coat. Measure your area accurately and calculate the quantity needed. Do not stretch paint to save money; it always shows. Apply the number of coats specified, usually two topcoats for exteriors and two for interiors, with the appropriate primer and undercoat. Between coats, allow proper drying time. Sand lightly where necessary to remove dust nibs and improve adhesion. In the field, keep a wet edge by working in a logical sequence: start at the top, work down, and finish a wall before moving to the next. On large walls, divide into manageable sections and maintain a consistent technique. For ceilings, use a roller on an extension pole and work away from the light source so you can see imperfections. For trim, paint from the top down and keep a clean rag for drips. Quality control is your responsibility. Inspect each coat for runs, sags, holidays, and missed spots. Use a light at a low angle to reveal defects. Fix problems immediately while the paint is still workable. Keep a touch-up kit with the same batch of paint. At the end of each day, clean tools, cover paint containers, and secure the site. In the next lesson we will look at pricing, quoting, and running a profitable decorating business.

4. Pricing, Quoting, and Running a Profitable Decorating Business

Welcome to Lesson 4. Many skilled painters in Africa fail not because they cannot paint, but because they cannot price. Undercharging is the most common business mistake in the trade. In this lesson we will cover how to calculate costs, prepare a professional quote, and manage a decorating business for profit. Start with your costs. There are direct costs and indirect costs. Direct costs include labour, paint, primers, fillers, sandpaper, masking tape, brushes, rollers, thinners, cleaning materials, and transport. Indirect costs include tools depreciation, vehicle costs, insurance, phone and data, office or storage rent, marketing, and your own salary. Many painters forget to include their own time in the cost calculation. You must pay yourself. Decide on a realistic daily rate for yourself and your team. In most African markets, a skilled painter should earn a living wage that covers food, rent, transport, school fees, and savings. If you charge less than that, you are subsidising the client. Calculate the labour time accurately. A common mistake is to estimate only the painting time and forget preparation, masking, cleaning, drying time, and touch-ups. Preparation often takes 40 to 60 percent of total labour on a repaint. Measure the area to be painted in square metres. Include walls, ceilings, trim, doors, windows, and metalwork. For each surface, note the number of coats and the coverage rate of the paint. Divide the area by the coverage rate to get litres per coat, then multiply by the number of coats. Add 10 percent for waste and touch-ups. Price the paint at the current market rate, and check availability before quoting. If a product is scarce, either stock it yourself or add a contingency. Labour is calculated by area or by day. Area-based pricing is common for walls: a rate per square metre that includes labour and sometimes materials. Day-based pricing is common for complex or unpredictable jobs. Choose the method that protects you. For repaints with unknown substrate conditions, use a day rate plus materials, or include a clear clause that additional preparation will be charged separately. Now build the quote. A professional quote includes your business name and contact details, the client name and site address, the date, a quote number, a description of the work, the products to be used, the number of coats, the areas covered, the price breakdown, the total price, the payment terms, the validity period, and your terms and conditions. Be specific. Instead of saying paint the house, say prepare and paint exterior walls, two coats of acrylic emulsion over one coat of alkali-resistant primer, including filling of hairline cracks, excluding structural repairs and roof work. This protects you from scope creep. Payment terms are critical for cash flow. A common structure is a deposit of 50 to 60 percent on acceptance, a progress payment of 20 to 30 percent when preparation is complete or when the first coat is applied, and the balance on completion. Never start a job without a deposit. The deposit covers materials and reduces the risk of non-payment. For larger jobs, agree a payment schedule in writing. In many African markets, clients may ask for credit. Be cautious. If you extend credit, do it only for trusted clients with a signed agreement and a clear payment date. Late payment is a major cause of business failure. Follow up promptly and professionally. If payment is delayed, stop work and communicate in writing. Do not let a job continue indefinitely while you fund it from your own pocket. Manage your materials. Buy in bulk where you have secure storage, because bulk prices are lower. Keep a simple inventory of tools and materials. Track paint usage per job so you can improve your estimates. Keep receipts for all purchases. If you are registered for tax, keep proper records. Even if you are not, good records help you see which jobs are profitable and which are not. Marketing matters. Most decorating work comes from referrals, so deliver quality and ask satisfied clients for referrals and testimonials. Take before and after photographs and use them on WhatsApp Business, Facebook, and Instagram. Join local business groups and builder associations. Partner with architects, quantity surveyors, estate agents, and property managers. Offer a free site visit and a written quote within 48 hours. Professionalism wins work. Finally, build a reserve. Set aside a percentage of each job for tool replacement, vehicle maintenance, and slow periods. In the next lesson we will cover health, safety, and environmental responsibility on site.

5. Health, Safety, and Environmental Responsibility on Site

Welcome to Lesson 5. Painting and decorating involves real hazards: chemicals, dust, fumes, working at height, and electrical risks. In Africa, where enforcement of safety regulations varies, the responsibility falls heavily on you as the professional. A safe site protects your team, your client, and your business. A serious accident can destroy your reputation and your finances. Start with personal protective equipment, known as PPE. Every painter should have safety glasses or goggles, a dust mask or respirator, gloves, and protective clothing. For solvent-based paints and thinners, use a respirator with the correct filter, not a simple dust mask. For spraying, use a proper spray respirator and ensure adequate ventilation. Wear non-slip safety boots, especially on wet or dusty surfaces. Use hard hats where there is a risk of falling objects, and harnesses for work at height. Keep PPE clean and replace worn items. Do not let workers use thinners to clean their hands; provide proper hand cleaner and water. Chemical safety is critical. Paints, thinners, and cleaners can cause skin irritation, burns, headaches, dizziness, and long-term health problems. Read the safety data sheet for every product. Store chemicals in clearly labelled containers, away from food and water, and out of reach of children. Never mix chemicals unless the manufacturer instructs it. Keep thinners away from flames, because they are highly flammable. In hot climates, store paint and solvents in a shaded, ventilated area, because heat builds pressure in containers and increases fire risk. When working indoors, ventilate the space by opening windows and doors, and use fans to move air. If ventilation is poor, use an air-fed respirator or take frequent breaks in fresh air. Dust control is important, especially during sanding. Sanding generates fine dust that can damage lungs and irritate eyes. Use sandpaper with a dust extraction system where possible, or wet sanding to reduce dust. Wear a dust mask, and clean up dust regularly with a damp cloth rather than dry sweeping, which spreads it. In areas with silica dust from concrete or render, take extra care, because prolonged exposure can cause serious lung disease. Working at height is a major cause of accidents. Use proper ladders, scaffolding, or mobile towers. Inspect equipment before use. Secure ladders at the correct angle, and never overreach. On roofs, use fall protection and never work alone. In many African cities, informal scaffolding made from timber is common. If you use timber, inspect it for cracks and ensure it is properly braced. Do not overload platforms. Keep the site tidy to prevent trips and falls. Electrical safety matters when using power tools, sprayers, and lights. Use residual current devices, known as RCDs, and check cables for damage. Do not use water near electrical equipment. In areas with unreliable power, use generators safely, with proper ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning. Never run generators indoors or in enclosed spaces. Fire safety is essential. Keep a fire extinguisher on site, and know how to use it. Store oily rags in a metal container with a lid, because they can self-ignite. Do not smoke near solvents or paint. Have an emergency plan, including emergency numbers and a first aid kit. Train your team in basic first aid and ensure someone on site knows what to do in an emergency. Environmental responsibility is increasingly important to clients and communities. Avoid dumping paint, thinners, or cleaning water into drains, rivers, or the ground. Collect waste in containers and dispose of it at approved sites. Reuse what you can: clean rollers and brushes properly so they last longer, and use leftover paint for touch-ups or donate it to community projects. Choose low-VOC and water-based products where suitable, because they are safer for your team and the environment. In areas with water scarcity, use water-efficient cleaning methods. Respect the client property and the neighbourhood. Cover floors and furniture, protect plants, and clean up at the end of each day. Noise from sanders and sprayers can disturb neighbours, so agree working hours with the client and avoid early morning or late evening work. In many African communities, informal workers and children may be present on site. Keep them away from chemicals and tools, and never let children handle paint or thinners. Finally, document your safety practices. Keep a simple site safety checklist, record toolbox talks, and note any incidents. This shows clients that you are professional and helps you improve. Safety is not a cost; it is an investment in your team, your reputation, and your future. This concludes Module 7. You now have the foundation to prepare surfaces, choose systems, apply paint professionally, price your work profitably, and work safely. Apply these lessons on every job, and you will build a decorating business that lasts.

<h3>Costing a Job: The Business-First Method</h3>

<p>Price the job in five lines: materials, labour, access/equipment, contingency, and profit. Most decorators in Africa underprice labour and forget access. Scaffolding, ladders, masking, and transport eat margin if they are not quoted.</p>

<p><strong>Worked example 200 m exterior repaint, Nairobi, 2024 prices:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Paint (2 coats, 9 m/L planning) = 45 L @ KES 850/L = KES 38,250</li>
<li>Primer / sealer = 20 L @ KES 600/L = KES 12,000</li>
<li>Labour (2 painters 4 days KES 1,200/day) = KES 9,600</li>
<li>Access, masking, transport = KES 6,000</li>
<li>Contingency (10%) = KES 6,585</li>
<li>Subtotal = KES 72,435</li>
<li>Profit margin (25%) = KES 18,109</li>
<li><strong>Quoted price = KES 90,544 (approx. KES 453/m)</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Always quote per square metre so the client can compare, but build your price from the bottom up. If a competitor quotes KES 250/m, they are either using a cheaper system, skipping the primer, or losing money. Your job is to explain the difference, not to match the price.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c00; background:#fff5f5; padding:14px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING CHEMICALS AND SOLVENTS</p>
<p style="margin:0;">Solvent-based paints, thinners, and wood preservatives contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that are harmful if inhaled or absorbed through skin. In hot African conditions, evaporation is faster and vapour concentration is higher. Always work in ventilated spaces, wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a respirator rated for organic vapours (not a dust mask). Never smoke or use open flames near solvents. Store thinners away from direct sun pressurised containers can rupture. Pregnant workers must avoid solvent exposure. Dispose of paint residue and cleaning solvents as hazardous waste, never into drains, rivers, or open ground. Refer to the product Safety Data Sheet (SDS) before every job.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Measure before you mix:</strong> confirm total m and substrate type before opening any tin.</li>
<li><strong>Check the data sheet:</strong> confirm thinning ratio, pot life, and recoat time for the exact product batch.</li>
<li><strong>Test a patch:</strong> apply a 1 m test area and check adhesion and colour before committing to the full job.</li>
<li><strong>Respect the weather:</strong> do not apply exterior paint in direct sun above 35C, in rain, or when humidity is above 85%.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records:</strong> photograph the substrate, the primer coat, and the finished work. This protects you in disputes.</li>
<li><strong>Quote in writing:</strong> include system specification, number of coats, brand, and exclusions (e.g. repairs, access).</li>
<li><strong>Protect the margin:</strong> never discount below your contingency line without removing scope.</li>
<li><strong>Train your team:</strong> one untrained helper can ruin a finish and cost you the client.</li>
</ul>
