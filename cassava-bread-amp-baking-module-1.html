Lessons

1. Identifying fresh cassava roots in local markets

When you buy cassava roots at the market, you need to pick ones that are fresh and good for baking. Fresh cassava roots are firm and hard. Press the root with your thumb; it should not leave a dent. The skin should be brown and rough, with no soft spots or mould. Avoid roots that are wet or have a sour smell. A fresh root snaps cleanly when you break it. The inside should be white or creamy white, with no black lines or discoloration. If you see black streaks, the root is old or diseased. Size matters: medium-sized roots, about 2 to 3 inches in diameter, are best. Very thick roots can be woody and fibrous. Also, look at the ends: they should be clean and dry, not mushy. In many African markets, cassava is sold in piles. Ask the seller to cut a small piece for you to check. If the inside is white and smells fresh, it's good. If it smells like almonds (bitter) or is yellowish, reject it. Buy cassava that was harvested recently, ideally within 2 days. In hot weather, cassava spoils fast. So, buy from a seller who has a fresh supply. If you can, buy directly from a farmer. You can also test by scratching the skin: fresh cassava skin is hard to scratch, while old cassava skin peels off easily. Another trick: fresh cassava is heavy for its size. Pick up two roots of the same size; the heavier one has more water and is fresher. After buying, use it within 2 to 3 days. If you must store it, keep it in a cool, dark place. Remember, cassava that is not fresh will give you poor-quality flour and bread. So, take your time to choose well.

2. Understanding sweet vs bitter cassava varieties

Cassava comes in two main types: sweet and bitter. The difference is in the amount of cyanide, a natural poison. Sweet cassava has low cyanide and can be eaten after peeling and cooking. Bitter cassava has high cyanide and must be processed carefully to remove the poison. In Africa, sweet cassava is often eaten boiled or fried, while bitter cassava is used for flour, gari, and fufu. For baking, you can use both, but you must process bitter cassava properly to make it safe. How to tell them apart? Sweet cassava roots usually have a light brown skin and white flesh. They taste slightly sweet when raw. Bitter cassava roots often have a darker skin and yellow or creamy flesh. They taste very bitter and leave a burning sensation in your mouth. Never taste raw bitter cassava to check; it can be dangerous. Instead, ask the seller or farmer. They know their varieties. Common sweet varieties in Africa include 'TME 419' and 'TMS 30572'. Bitter varieties include 'TMS 91/02324' and 'TMS 92/0326'. If you are not sure, treat it as bitter and process it well. For baking, sweet cassava is easier because you can peel, wash, and grate it directly. Bitter cassava needs extra steps: soaking, pressing, and fermenting to remove cyanide. The cyanide is in the water, so you must squeeze it out. A simple test: grate a small piece, squeeze the juice, and put a drop on a piece of paper. If it turns blue with a cyanide test kit (available at some agro-shops), it's bitter. But most home bakers don't have kits. So, rely on local knowledge. In many villages, people know which variety is sweet or bitter. Always ask. If you use bitter cassava without proper processing, you can get sick. So, choose sweet cassava for simple baking, or learn to process bitter cassava safely.

3. How to peel and wash cassava safely

Peeling cassava is simple, but you must do it safely because the raw root contains cyanide. Start by cutting off both ends of the root with a sharp knife. Then, make a shallow cut along the length of the root, through the skin only. Use your thumb to lift the skin and peel it off. If the skin is thick, use a knife to help. Work on a clean surface, like a wooden board or a mat. Wear gloves if you have them, because the raw juice can irritate your skin. If you don't have gloves, wash your hands with soap after peeling. Never touch your eyes or mouth while peeling. After peeling, cut the root into smaller pieces, about 2 to 3 inches long. Remove any woody core or black spots. Then, wash the pieces in plenty of clean water. Use a large bowl or basin. Rub the pieces with your hands to remove dirt and starch. Change the water at least twice. The water will become milky white. That's the starch. For sweet cassava, you can now grate or boil it. For bitter cassava, you must soak the pieces in water for 24 to 48 hours, changing the water every 6 hours. This removes cyanide. After soaking, drain and squeeze the pieces to remove excess water. You can use a clean cloth or a sieve. Then, wash again with clean water. Always wash your hands, knives, and boards with soap and hot water after handling raw cassava. Do not let children or animals eat the peelings. Dispose of peelings in a pit or burn them. If you feel dizzy or sick while peeling, stop and get fresh air. Cyanide gas can be released when peeling bitter cassava. So, work in a well-ventilated area, like outside or near an open window. With practice, peeling 5 kg of cassava takes about 30 minutes. Stay safe and clean.

4. Tools needed for cassava processing on a budget

You don't need expensive machines to start processing cassava for baking. With a few basic tools, you can do everything by hand. First, a sharp knife. A kitchen knife or machete works. Keep it sharp to peel easily. Second, a cutting board or a clean flat stone. Third, a large bowl or basin for washing. Plastic basins are cheap and available in any market. Fourth, a grater. You can use a traditional hand grater, or make one by punching holes in a tin can. But a proper box grater is better. In many African markets, you can buy a cassava grater for a low price. Fifth, a clean cloth or a mesh bag for squeezing. A strong cotton cloth works well. Sixth, a sieve or a basket for draining. Seventh, a drying tray. You can use a clean mat, a wooden board, or a metal sheet. For drying in the sun, a raised platform keeps it clean. Eighth, a mortar and pestle for pounding if you want flour. Or you can take your dried cassava to a local mill. Ninth, a scale for measuring. A simple kitchen scale is fine. Tenth, storage containers. Use airtight jars or plastic buckets with lids. For fermenting bitter cassava, you need a large pot or bucket with a lid. For pressing, you can use a simple wooden press made from two boards and a rope, or just use heavy stones. All these tools can be found locally or made by a carpenter or welder. You don't need electricity. Start small: a knife, basin, grater, and cloth are enough to make cassava flour for your family or small bakery. As your business grows, you can invest in a motorized grater or a hydraulic press. But for now, keep it simple and cheap. Clean your tools after every use to avoid contamination.

5. Storing fresh cassava to avoid spoilage

Fresh cassava roots spoil quickly, often within 2 to 3 days after harvest. To avoid spoilage, you must store them properly. The best way is to use them immediately. But if you must store, keep them in a cool, dark, and dry place. Do not leave them in the sun. In hot African climates, cassava can rot in one day if left outside. One method is to bury the roots in slightly moist sand or soil. This keeps them fresh for up to one week. Use a shaded area, like under a tree or in a shed. Another method is to wrap the roots in dry banana leaves or newspaper and keep them in a basket. This works for 3 to 4 days. Do not wash the roots before storing; washing removes the natural protective layer and speeds up spoilage. Only wash just before peeling. If you have a refrigerator, you can store peeled cassava in a plastic bag in the vegetable compartment for up to 5 days. But many bakers don't have fridges. Another option is to process the cassava into flour or dough and store that instead. Dried cassava flour can last for 6 months in an airtight container. To dry, grate or slice the cassava and sun-dry for 2 to 3 days until brittle. Then mill and store. For fresh roots, check them daily. Remove any that show mould or soft spots. One bad root can spoil the others. So, sort them regularly. If you buy in bulk, only buy what you can use in 3 days. In markets, cassava is often sold in large bundles. If you buy a bundle, share with other bakers or process it quickly. You can also freeze grated cassava. Squeeze out excess water, pack in plastic bags, and freeze. It lasts for 3 months. Thaw before use. Remember, spoilage is the biggest loss for cassava bakers. So, plan your purchases and storage carefully.

6. Health and safety when handling raw cassava

Raw cassava contains cyanide, a poison. Sweet cassava has small amounts, but bitter cassava has a lot. You must handle it safely to avoid getting sick. The first rule: never eat raw cassava. Always peel, wash, and cook or process it. The cyanide is in the juice. So, when you peel or grate, do it in a well-ventilated area. Open windows or work outside. Cyanide gas can be released, especially from bitter cassava. If you feel dizzy, headache, or nausea, stop and move to fresh air. Wear gloves if possible. If you don't have gloves, wash your hands with soap and water immediately after handling. Do not touch your face, eyes, or mouth with unwashed hands. Keep children away from raw cassava and peelings. Do not let animals eat the peelings; they can get sick too. Dispose of peelings by burning or burying deep. For bitter cassava, you must remove cyanide by soaking, fermenting, and pressing. Soak grated or sliced cassava in water for 24 to 48 hours, changing water every 6 hours. Then squeeze out all the water. The cyanide goes into the water. After processing, cook the cassava thoroughly. Boiling, frying, or baking destroys any remaining cyanide. If you are making flour, ensure the cassava is completely dried and milled. Dry it in the sun for 2 to 3 days. A simple test for cyanide: take a small piece of processed cassava and smell it. If it smells like bitter almonds, it may still have cyanide. Cook it more. Never skip the processing steps for bitter cassava. Also, keep your work area clean. Wash knives, boards, and bowls with soap and hot water. Do not use the same cloth for raw cassava and other foods. If you feel sick after handling cassava, drink plenty of water and seek medical help. Prevention is easy: follow the steps, and you will be safe.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cassava Basics for Bakers</h2>

<p>Cassava (<em>Manihot esculenta</em>) is the backbone of West, Central and East African baking economies. In Nigeria alone, the Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (FMARD) estimates over 40 million tonnes of fresh roots are produced annually, with high-quality cassava flour (HQCF) substitution in bread reaching 1020% in commercial bakeries. For a baker, cassava is not a subsistence crop it is a margin opportunity. This Practical Reference consolidates the field knowledge you need to buy, handle, process and store cassava profitably while protecting your staff and customers.</p>

<h3>1. Identifying Fresh Roots in Local Markets</h3>
<p>Fresh cassava roots are highly perishable physiological deterioration begins within 2472 hours of harvest. Bakers must buy on visible sensory indicators, not vendor claims. A fresh root has firm, unshriveled skin, a clean white-to-cream flesh when scratched, and no sour or fermented odour. Roots with blue-black vascular streaking, soft patches, or a hollow sound when tapped have started to rot and will destroy flour colour and taste.</p>

<h3>2. Sweet vs Bitter Varieties The Cyanide Question</h3>
<p>Cassava varieties are classified by cyanogenic glucoside content, expressed as hydrogen cyanide (HCN) potential. "Sweet" varieties generally contain less than 50 mg HCN/kg fresh root and can be boiled and eaten with minimal processing. "Bitter" varieties often exceed 100 mg/kg and require grating, fermentation, pressing and drying to reduce cyanide to safe levels. For bakery flour, most commercial processors prefer sweet varieties for lower detoxification cost, but bitter varieties can be used if the full HQCF process is followed.</p>

<h3>3. Peeling and Washing Safely</h3>
<p>Peel cassava within 24 hours of purchase. Use a sharp stainless steel knife to remove the outer periderm and the inner cortex both contain the highest cyanide concentration. Cut roots into 1015 cm segments, then split lengthwise to expose the central vascular strand, which should be removed if woody. Wash in at least two changes of clean potable water; do not reuse wash water across batches. Wear gloves if handling more than 20 kg per shift prolonged skin contact with raw cassava sap can cause irritation.</p>

<h3>4. Budget Tools for Cassava Processing</h3>
<p>You do not need imported machinery to start. A basic processing line can be assembled for under USD 400 in most African markets:</p>
<ul>
<li>Stainless steel peeling knives (35 units)</li>
<li>Plastic or food-grade basins (46 units, 4060 L)</li>
<li>Manual or motorised grater (local fabricators in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya)</li>
<li>Hydraulic or screw press for dewatering (manual screw press is cheapest)</li>
<li>Solar or cabinet dryer, or raised drying racks with mesh</li>
<li>Hammer mill or disc mill for final flour milling</li>
<li>Calibrated weighing scale (550 kg range)</li>
<li>Food-grade storage sacks with liners</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Storing Fresh Cassava to Avoid Spoilage</h3>
<p>Fresh roots lose quality fast. If you cannot process within 48 hours, use one of these methods: (a) store in a cool, ventilated, shaded room at 2530 C for up to 3 days; (b) re-bury roots in moist sawdust or sand for up to 7 days; (c) freeze peeled, cut roots for up to 3 months; or (d) process immediately into flour or garri. Never stack roots in direct sun or in sealed plastic bags condensation accelerates fungal rot.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fff5f5; padding:14px 18px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Cyanide Poisoning Risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Raw bitter cassava contains cyanogenic glucosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). Eating inadequately processed cassava can cause acute cyanide poisoning symptoms include dizziness, vomiting, headache, confusion and in severe cases respiratory failure. <strong>Never</strong> feed raw cassava peel or wash water to livestock without fermentation, and <strong>never</strong> sell flour that has not completed the full detoxification process (soaking, fermentation, pressing, drying). Children and malnourished adults are at highest risk. Follow FAO/WHO Codex CAC/RCP 73-2013 at all times.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Health and Safety When Handling Raw Cassava</h3>
<p>Beyond cyanide, raw cassava sap contains latex-like compounds that can irritate skin and eyes. Practical controls: wear gloves and eye protection during grating and pressing; wash hands and tools with soap after handling; keep processing areas separate from baking areas to avoid cross-contamination; and train all staff on the difference between sweet and bitter varieties. Record every batch's variety, source, processing date and HCN test result if available.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Buy roots harvested within 48 hours; reject soft, streaked or sour-smelling roots.</li>
<li>Confirm variety (sweet vs bitter) with your supplier and record it.</li>
<li>Peel within 24 hours; remove periderm, cortex and woody central strand.</li>
<li>Wash in at least two changes of clean water; never reuse wash water.</li>
<li>Process bitter varieties through full soakfermentpressdry cycle.</li>
<li>Store fresh roots in shade or moist sand for max 37 days, or freeze peeled roots.</li>
<li>Wear gloves and eye protection; wash hands and tools after every batch.</li>
<li>Keep batch records: variety, source, date, process, yield.</li>
</ol>
