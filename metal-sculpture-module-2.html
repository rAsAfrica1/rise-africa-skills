Lessons

1. Cutting Metal with Hand Tools: Hacksaws and Shears

Start with a 24-tooth-per-inch (TPI) hacksaw blade for cutting mild steel. For thin metal under 3mm, use 32 TPI to avoid snagging. For thick stock over 6mm, use 18 TPI. In Africa, you'll often find blades at hardware stores in large cities; if not, salvage from old saws. Secure your metal in a vise or clamp it to a sturdy table. Mark your cut line with a scriber or a permanent marker. For a clean cut, start the saw at a 45-degree angle to create a notch, then bring it flat. Use long, steady strokesabout 40-50 per minutewith light pressure. Let the blade do the work. Too much pressure breaks teeth. Lubricate with a few drops of engine oil or even cooking oil to extend blade life.

For sheet metal up to 1.5mm thick, use tin snips (aviation snips). There are three types: left-cut, right-cut, and straight-cut. For straight lines, use straight-cut snips. For curves, use left or right depending on direction. Hold the sheet firmly and cut close to the vise. Don't cut all the way to the edge in one gotake small bites. For thicker sheet (up to 2mm), use a bench shear or a chisel and hammer on a hard surface. Always wear gloves; cut edges are razor-sharp.

After cutting, the edges will have burrs. You'll remove those in Lesson 4. For now, focus on accuracy. In many African workshops, a hacksaw and snips are the primary cutting tools. Practice on scrap metalold car panels, roofing sheets, or bed frames. Measure twice, cut once. A common mistake is cutting too fast and breaking blades. Keep spare blades. If you're cutting pipe, rotate it as you saw to keep the cut straight. Remember: sharp tools are safe tools. Replace dull blades immediately.

2. Bending and Forming Metal with Hand Tools

Bending metal by hand requires leverage and control. For thin sheet metal (up to 1.5mm), you can bend it over a sharp edge like a table corner or a piece of angle iron clamped in a vise. For thicker sheet (2-3mm), use a mallet and a forma pipe or a wooden block. Place the metal on the form and strike it with a rubber or wooden mallet to avoid stretching the metal. For rods and bars up to 10mm diameter, use a vise and a hammer. Clamp the rod in the vise at the bend point, then hammer it over. For tighter bends, use a bending forktwo pieces of pipe welded to a handleor a simple pipe bender.

In Africa, many artisans use a 'bending jig' made from steel pipes welded to a base. You can make one: weld three short pipes (20mm diameter) upright on a steel plate. Space them to fit your rod. Insert the rod between pipes and pull. For curves, use a sandbag or a tree stump as a form. For organic shapes, like leaves, you'll need to stretch and shrink metal. This is done by hammering on a sandbag or a wooden stump. Hammering on a sandbag stretches the metal; hammering on a hard surface shrinks it. Alternate to shape curves.

Always anneal (soften) metal if it cracks. Heat it with a torch until it glows red, then let it cool slowly. For aluminum, heat until a soap mark turns black. For copper and brass, quench in water after heating. Practice on scrap. A common project is a simple hook: bend a 6mm rod 180 degrees over a pipe. Use a vice to hold the pipe. Hit the rod with a hammer near the bend to tighten it. Remember: bend a little, check, bend more. Metal has memoryit may spring back. Overbend slightly to compensate. Wear safety glasses; metal can snap.

3. Drilling and Punching Holes in Metal

For holes up to 10mm in thin metal, use a center punch and a drill. Start by marking the hole with a center punch and a hammer. This creates a dimple to guide the drill bit. Use a drill bit made for metalhigh-speed steel (HSS) with a 118-degree point. For hard metals, use cobalt bits. Clamp your work securely. If the metal is thin, place a scrap piece of wood underneath to prevent tear-out. Use a drill press if available; otherwise, a hand drill. Run the drill at a slow speed for metalabout 300-500 RPM for a 6mm bit. Use cutting oil or a few drops of engine oil. Apply steady pressure; don't force it. Let the bit cut. If the bit squeals, slow down and add oil.

For larger holes (over 10mm), use a hole saw or a step drill. A step drill is ideal for sheet metal up to 3mm. It creates multiple sizes. For punching holes, use a hammer and a punch. A hollow punch (also called a wad punch) is used for leather and thin sheet. For metal, use a solid punch and a backing block. Place the metal on a block of lead or hardwood. Strike the punch with a heavy hammer. This works for holes up to 6mm in thin sheet. For larger holes, use a chassis punch or a drill.

In many African workshops, a hand drill is common. If you don't have a drill, you can use a hammer and a nail for very thin metal, but it will be rough. Always deburr holes after drilling. A countersink bit or a larger drill bit twisted by hand can remove the burr. Safety: wear glasses, clamp work, and never hold the work with your hand while drilling. If the bit grabs, it can spin the metal and cut you. Keep bits sharp. Replace when dull.

4. Filing and Deburring Edges

After cutting or drilling, edges have burrssharp, rough bits of metal. Deburring makes your work safe and professional. Start with a coarse file (bastard cut) to remove large burrs. Then use a smooth file (second cut) for a finer finish. For flat edges, use a flat file. For curved edges, use a half-round or round file. Clamp your work in a vise at about waist height. Hold the file with both hands: one on the handle, the other on the tip. Push the file forward with light pressure; lift it on the return stroke. Don't drag it backthat dulls the teeth. File at a slight angle to the edge, about 30 degrees, to avoid catching.

For deburring holes, use a countersink bit or a larger drill bit. Turn it by hand or at slow speed in a drill. Just a few turns will remove the burr. For inside curves, use a round file or a half-round file. For outside curves, use a half-round file. In Africa, old files are common; if you can't find new ones, clean the teeth with a file card (a wire brush). Don't use a file without a handleit can stab your palm. Make a handle from a piece of wood or a cork.

After filing, check for sharp edges with your finger (carefully). A smooth edge should not catch. For a polished finish, use emery cloth or sandpaper wrapped around a file. Start with 80 grit, then 120, then 220. For a mirror finish, use 400 grit and then polish with a buffing wheel. In many workshops, a bench grinder with a wire wheel is used for deburring, but hand filing gives more control. Practice on scrap. A common mistake is filing too much and changing the shape. File a little, check, file more. Keep your files clean and dry to prevent rust.

5. Using Clamps and Vises for Workholding

Workholding is critical for safety and accuracy. A bench vise is your most important tool. Mount it securely to a sturdy table. The jaws should be at about elbow height. For holding round stock, use a vise with V-grooved jaws or use a pipe clamp. For flat stock, use the flat jaws. Always tighten the vise firmlybut not so tight that you crush thin metal. Use jaw pads (soft jaws) made from wood, aluminum, or copper for delicate work. For long pieces, support the other end with a stand or a clamp.

C-clamps and F-clamps are used to hold work to a table or to clamp pieces together for welding or drilling. For drilling, clamp the work to the drill press table. Never hold work by hand. For welding, use clamps to hold pieces in alignment. In Africa, many artisans use homemade clamps from steel bars and bolts. A simple clamp can be made from a piece of angle iron and a bolt. For holding pipes, use a chain vise or a pipe vise. For holding irregular shapes, use a vise with swivel base or a clamp with a ball joint.

When using a vise, place the work so that the force of the tool is against the fixed jaw, not the movable jaw. This prevents the work from slipping. For sawing, cut close to the vise jaws to reduce vibration. For filing, keep the work low to avoid bending. Always check that the vise is tight before applying pressure. A loose work piece can fly out and cause injury. Use a brush to clean chips from the vise. Lubricate the screw occasionally with oil. In shared workshops, label your clamps. Remember: a good workholding setup makes the job easier and safer.

6. Project: Metal Leaves and Organic Shapes

Now you'll combine all the techniques to make a metal leaf. Start with a piece of mild steel sheet, 1mm to 1.5mm thick. Draw a leaf shape (about 10cm long, 5cm wide) on the metal with a marker. Use tin snips to cut out the rough shape. Then use a file to refine the edges and remove burrs. For the stem, use a 6mm rod, about 15cm long. Cut it with a hacksaw. File the ends smooth.

To shape the leaf, place it on a sandbag or a block of wood. Use a ball-peen hammer to stretch the metal in the center, creating a gentle curve. Hammer lightly and evenly. For the veins, use a chisel or a blunt screwdriver to emboss lines. Place the leaf on a soft surface (like a rubber mat) and strike the chisel with a hammer. Move along the leaf to create a central vein and side veins. For a more organic look, bend the edges upward slightly by hammering on the edge over a pipe. This is called 'raising'.

Attach the stem to the leaf. You can weld it, braze it, or use a rivet. For a rivet, drill a 3mm hole in the leaf and the stem, insert a 3mm rivet (or a piece of copper wire), and hammer it flat. If you have a torch, braze with brass rod. For a no-heat method, use a two-part epoxy. In Africa, many artisans use recycled metal from car panels and roofing. Clean the metal with sandpaper before gluing or welding.

To finish, polish with emery cloth and apply a coat of clear lacquer or oil to prevent rust. Make several leaves of different sizes and arrange them on a branch or a wall. This project teaches cutting, bending, drilling, filing, and workholding. Practice on scrap first. A common mistake is hammering too hard and tearing the metal. Work slowly. Your leaf should look natural, not machine-made. Display your work proudly.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Basic Metalworking Techniques for Metal Sculpture</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core hand-tool metalworking techniques you need to turn scrap steel, sheet offcuts, and recycled metal into saleable sculpture. In the African context, where access to industrial machinery is often limited and power can be unreliable, hand-tool competence is not a fallback it is a competitive advantage. A sculptor in Kumasi, Nairobi, or Bulawayo who can cut, bend, drill, file, and clamp with confidence can produce finished work with minimal capital outlay, keeping overheads low and margins healthy.</p>

<p>Treat this module as a workshop bench reference. The techniques here feed directly into the project lesson, where you will produce metal leaves and organic shapes for decorative panels, garden art, and market-ready pieces.</p>

<h3>1. Cutting Metal with Hand Tools: Hacksaws and Shears</h3>

<p>Accurate cutting saves material, time, and money. Every wasted offcut is a direct loss on the job. For straight cuts in bar stock, angle iron, and tube up to about 25 mm, use a hacksaw with a 24 or 32 TPI blade coarser for thick sections, finer for thin tube and sheet. Apply light, steady pressure on the forward stroke only, and let the blade do the work. For sheet metal up to about 1.2 mm, aviation snips (tin snips) are faster and cleaner than a hacksaw. Left-cutting, right-cutting, and straight-cutting shears each have a role; keep them sharp and never cut hardened wire with them.</p>

<p>In many African workshops, hacksaw blades are the consumable that eats profit fastest. Buy quality bi-metal blades in bulk where possible, and teach apprentices to avoid twisting the blade that is the single biggest cause of premature breakage.</p>

<h3>2. Bending and Forming Metal with Hand Tools</h3>

<p>Organic shapes leaves, vines, petals demand controlled bending. For thin sheet and light rod, hand pressure over a pipe or a wooden form works well. For thicker sections, use a bench vise as a bending brake, or clamp the workpiece between two steel pegs and lever it into shape. A mallet, not a hammer, should be your first choice on soft metals like aluminium and copper to avoid stretching and marring. For compound curves, a sandbag or a shaped hardwood former gives you repeatable results.</p>

<p>Consistency sells. If you are producing a run of twenty leaves for a hotel commission, build a simple jig from scrap steel so every leaf curves the same way. That is the difference between craft and production.</p>

<h3>3. Drilling and Punching Holes in Metal</h3>

<p>Holes matter for rivets, bolts, hanging points, and decorative perforation. For sheet under 1 mm, a hollow punch or a sharpened nail set with a hammer is fast. For anything thicker, use a hand drill or drill press with high-speed steel (HSS) or cobalt bits. Always centre-punch the location first, apply cutting oil or even used engine oil, and run the drill slowly with firm pressure. Too much speed burns the bit; too little pressure work-hardens the metal.</p>

<p>In off-grid workshops, a good brace-and-bit or a quality hand drill is worth its weight. Keep a set of bits in a wooden block so they do not rattle together and dull.</p>

<h3>4. Filing and Deburring Edges</h3>

<p>Sharp edges cut hands and devalue work. After every cut, draw-file the edge with a smooth-cut mill file, then finish with a finer file or abrasive paper. For aluminium, chalk the file to prevent clogging. For steel, a light oil on the file helps. Deburring is not optional it is the difference between a piece that looks handmade and one that looks unfinished.</p>

<h3>5. Using Clamps and Vises for Workholding</h3>

<p>You cannot cut, drill, or bend accurately if the workpiece moves. A bench vise is the backbone of the hand-tool workshop. Add G-clamps, F-clamps, and spring clamps for sheet and irregular shapes. Always protect finished surfaces with soft jaws or scrap aluminium. Clamp close to the cut or hole to reduce vibration and chatter. A vise bolted to a sturdy bench even a bench made from hardwood offcuts will outlast most power tools.</p>

<h3>6. Project: Metal Leaves and Organic Shapes</h3>

<p>This project combines all five techniques. Start with 0.81.2 mm mild steel or copper sheet. Draw a leaf template on card, transfer it with a scriber, and cut with aviation snips. Deburr with a file. Drill a small hole at the stem end for a hanging wire or rivet. Bend the leaf along its central vein over a length of pipe, then curl the edges with round-nose pliers or a mallet over a sandbag. Clamp the stem in a vise while you shape it. Finish with a wire brush, then wax or clear lacquer to slow rusting or let it rust deliberately for a patina that sells well in garden art.</p>

<p>Produce leaves in batches of ten to twenty. Sell them individually as pendants, in clusters as wall art, or as components in larger commissions. This is a low-cost, high-margin product line that suits market stalls, craft fairs, and online shops.</p>

<p style="margin:0; color:#900; font-weight:bold;"> SAFETY WARNING EYE, HAND, AND LUNG PROTECTION</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#900;">Metal cutting, drilling, and filing produce sharp swarf, flying chips, and fine dust. Always wear safety glasses or a face shield. Wear cut-resistant gloves when handling sheet metal but never wear gloves near a drill press or any rotating tool, as they can catch and pull your hand in. Use a dust mask when filing or grinding, especially with galvanised or painted metal, which can release toxic particles. Secure all work in a vise or clamp before cutting or drilling. Keep a first-aid kit and clean water in the workshop. In many African workshops, ventilation is poor; work outdoors or near an open door whenever possible.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Select the correct blade TPI for the material and thickness before you start cutting.</li>
<li>Centre-punch every hole location; never drill on a smooth, unmarked surface.</li>
<li>Apply cutting oil or used engine oil to drill bits and saw blades to extend life.</li>
<li>Deburr every cut edge immediately sharp edges cut hands and devalue finished work.</li>
<li>Clamp work close to the cut or hole to reduce vibration, chatter, and blade breakage.</li>
<li>Use a mallet, not a steel hammer, when forming soft metals like copper and aluminium.</li>
<li>Build simple jigs for repeat shapes to keep quality consistent across production runs.</li>
<li>Wear eye protection at all times; remove gloves before using any rotating tool.</li>
</ul>

With a vise, a hacksaw, a file, a drill, and a source of scrap sheet metal, you can produce saleable work within a week of training. The margins in Table 2 are real for disciplined makers who control material waste, build jigs, and sell directly to buyers rather than through intermediaries. Treat every offcut as inventory, every finished edge as marketing, and every repeat shape as a production asset.</p>
