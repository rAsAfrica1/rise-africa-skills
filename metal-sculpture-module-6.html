1. Plasma Cutting: Techniques and Safety

Plasma cutting is the fastest way to slice through steel up to 25mm thick. In Africa, where oxy-acetylene can be expensive and hard to refill, a plasma cutter running on a 220V generator is a game-changer. You need a plasma cutter rated for at least 40 amps, a clean dry air supply (moisture traps are essential in humid coastal areas), and a face shield with shade #8 or darker. Never cut without leather gloves, a leather apron, and closed shoes. Sparks fly at 1500Â°C and will burn through synthetic clothing.

Before cutting, clean the metal with a wire brush to remove rust, paint, and oil. Set your amperage based on thickness: 30A for 6mm, 40A for 10mm, 60A for 15mm. For a clean cut, maintain a 1.5â3mm standoff between the tip and the metal. Drag the tip lightly if you have a drag shield, or use a standoff guide. Start the cut at the edge, not in the middle, unless you are piercing. To pierce, tilt the torch 30Â° to blow molten metal away, then straighten once through.

Move at a steady speedâtoo fast leaves dross (recast metal) on the bottom, too slow creates a wide kerf and excessive dross. Listen to the sound: a steady hiss means good speed. If you see sparks spraying upward, you are too slow. Practice on scrap 6mm plate: draw straight lines, circles, and curves. In many African workshops, plasma cutters are used to cut decorative panels, gates, and sculpture components. Keep a bucket of water nearby to quench hot cuts and reduce fire risk.

Maintenance: drain your air compressor daily, replace the consumables (electrode, nozzle, swirl ring) when the cut quality drops. A worn nozzle gives a ragged edge. Store consumables in a dry place. If you are using a generator, ensure it delivers clean powerâvoltage spikes kill plasma cutters. Always cut in a well-ventilated area, as plasma cutting produces fumes, especially on galvanized or painted steel. Wear a respirator if cutting coated metals.

2. Angle Grinding and Cutting

An angle grinder is the most versatile tool in a metal sculptorâs kit. In Africa, the 115mm (4.5-inch) grinder is common, but for fabrication, a 230mm (9-inch) grinder is better for cutting thick steel. Always use the correct disc: a cutting disc for slicing, a grinding disc for smoothing, and a flap disc for finishing. Never use a cutting disc for grindingâit can shatter. Check the discâs maximum RPM; it must be higher than the grinderâs no-load RPM (usually 11,000 for 115mm, 6,500 for 230mm).

Safety first: wear safety glasses or a face shield, ear plugs, and leather gloves. Tie back long hair and remove loose clothing. The grinder can kick back if the disc bindsâhold it firmly with two hands, and position yourself so the grinder is not in line with your body. For cutting, let the disc do the work; donât force it. Use a metal straight edge or a scribed line as a guide. For clean cuts, score the line with a cut-off wheel, then cut in one smooth motion. If cutting sheet metal, sandwich it between plywood to reduce vibration.

Grinding: hold the grinder at a 15â30Â° angle to the work. Move side to side to avoid gouging. For weld preparation, grind a 30Â° bevel on each plate for a V-groove. For finishing, use a 60â80 grit flap disc to blend welds, then a 120 grit for a smoother finish. In many African workshops, youâll use a grinder to clean up welds, remove rust, and shape metal. Keep a fire extinguisher nearbyâgrinding sparks can ignite sawdust or rags.

Maintenance: unplug the grinder before changing discs. Use the spanner to tighten the discânever hand-tighten. Check the power cord for damage. Replace worn brushes. Store grinders in a dry place. If you are using a generator, ensure it can handle the grinderâs starting surge (usually 2â3 times running watts). A 2.2kW generator will run a 115mm grinder but struggle with a 230mm. Always let the grinder spin down before setting it down.

3. Bending with a Brake and Roller

Bending metal opens up a world of formsâfrom curved sculpture panels to structural brackets. A brake (press brake or box-and-pan brake) makes straight bends; a roller (slip roller) makes curves. In Africa, many workshops build their own brakes from heavy channel iron and hinges. For a manual brake, you need a bed, a clamping bar, and a bending apron. The thickness capacity depends on the brakeâs size: a 1.2m brake can bend 1.5mm sheet, while a 2.4m brake can bend 1mm. Always check the brakeâs ratingânever exceed it.

To bend on a brake: mark your bend line with a scribe or soapstone. Set the back gauge for the distance from the bend to the edge. Clamp the sheet between the bed and clamping bar, aligning the bend line with the front edge of the bed. Lift the apron handles smoothlyâjerking causes cracks. For a 90Â° bend, you may need to overbend slightly (2â3Â°) to account for springback. For a radius bend, use a radius bar or a piece of pipe clamped in the brake. Practice on scrap: bend a 100mm wide strip to 90Â°, then measure the inside radius.

A slip roller has three rolls: one top roll and two bottom rolls. The gap between rolls determines the curve radius. To roll a cylinder, start with the sheet flat, lower the top roll to just touch the metal, then pass the sheet through. Tighten the top roll a quarter turn each pass until you reach the desired radius. For a cone, you need a slip roller with a cone attachment or you can hand-form over a pipe. In many African metal shops, rollers are used to make rings, curved gates, and sculpture bases. Always feed the metal squarelyâif it goes in crooked, it will come out crooked.

Safety: wear gloves when handling sheet metalâedges are sharp. Keep fingers clear of the rolls. For the brake, never put your hands between the clamping bar and the bed. Maintenance: oil the pivot points and gears regularly. Check for cracks in the bending apron. Store brake and roller under a cover to prevent rust. If you donât have a brake, you can bend metal in a vise with a hammer and a block of wood, but for repeatable bends, a brake is essential.

4. Advanced Welding: Vertical and Overhead

Vertical and overhead welding are the tests that separate a hobbyist from a professional. In Africa, these positions are common when building gates, roofs, and sculptures that cannot be rotated. For vertical welding, you can weld uphill or downhill. Uphill (vertical-up) gives deeper penetration and is used for structural joints; downhill is faster but weaker. For overhead, you must fight gravityâmolten metal wants to drip. The key is to use a slightly lower amperage (about 10â15% less than flat) and a shorter arc.

For vertical-up with 7018 electrode (3.2mm), set your welder to about 110â120 amps. Hold the electrode at a slight upward angle (5â15Â°). Use a triangle motion: pause at the sides, move quickly across the center. This gives a clean bead without undercut. For vertical-down with 6013 or 6011, use 90â100 amps and a straight drag motionâno weaving. Downhill is good for thin metal (up to 6mm) but not for thick sections.

Overhead welding: use 6011 or 7018 (3.2mm) at 100â115 amps. Keep the arc shortâabout the diameter of the electrode core. Use a back-and-forth motion or a small circle. The puddle must be small and cool quickly. If the puddle gets too large, it will drip. Tilt the electrode slightly in the direction of travel. For a fillet weld overhead, aim the electrode at the root of the joint, then push slightly into the corner. Practice on scrap plates clamped overheadâstart with a T-joint. In many African workshops, overhead welding is needed for repairing truck frames or building carports.

Safety: wear a leather welding jacket, gloves, and a welding cap. Use a welding screen to protect others from arc flash. Ensure good ventilationâwelding fumes rise, so an overhead weld puts your face right in the plume. Use a respirator if welding galvanized or painted steel. Keep a fire watcher nearby if welding near flammable materials. Maintenance: check your electrode holder and ground clamp for tight connections. Replace worn cables. Clean spatter from your helmet lens. Practice makes perfectâspend at least 20 hours on each position before calling yourself competent.

5. Jigs and Fixtures for Repetition

If you need to make 50 identical brackets, a jig saves time and ensures accuracy. A jig holds the workpiece; a fixture holds the tool. In African metal shops, jigs are often made from scrap steel, bolts, and clamps. Start simple: for a repeated cut, weld a stop block to your cutting table. For drilling, make a drill jig from a piece of flat bar with hardened drill bushings. For welding, a fixture table with slots and clamps is ideal, but you can build a simple jig from angle iron and toggle clamps.

Example: making a hundred 100x100mm angle brackets from 50x50x5mm angle iron. Build a jig: a base plate with two stopsâone for the length, one for the width. Weld a vertical post to hold the angle iron upright. Use a toggle clamp to hold the piece. Then you can drill two 10mm holes in each bracket without measuring. For welding, a jig with copper backing bars helps dissipate heat and prevents distortion. In many African workshops, jigs are used for making window frames, gates, and sculpture armatures.

Design rules: make jigs from sturdy material (at least 6mm plate or 50x50 angle). Use hardened pins for locating holes. Allow for clearanceâ0.5mm is enough for most fits. Make jigs adjustable if you need variations. Label your jigs and store them properly. For repetitive cutting, a stop on your chop saw or band saw is a simple jig. For bending, a back gauge on a brake is a jig. For assembly, a fixture table with a grid of holes (like a welding table) lets you clamp parts at precise angles.

Safety: never modify a jig while itâs in use. Use clamps that can handle the forceâa toggle clamp may slip under heavy welding. Keep jigs cleanâspatter buildup causes misalignment. Maintenance: check for wear on locating pins and stops. Replace worn parts. In Africa, where labor is often cheap but time is valuable, a good jig pays for itself in a day. If you are making multiple copies of a sculpture, a jig for the armature ensures each piece is identical. Start with a simple jig for your most repetitive task.

1. Plasma Cutting: Techniques and Safety

Plasma cutting remains the fastest way to cut sheet and plate steel up to about 25 mm in a small African workshop. A 40â60 amp inverter plasma cutter running on single-phase 220â240 V is the practical sweet spot: it handles 6â12 mm mild steel cleanly, runs off a modest generator or grid supply, and costs far less than a comparable oxy-fuel setup to operate.

If you make the same component more than three times â a leaf, a bracket, a base ring â build a jig. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. PPE first: face shield, leather apron, gloves, ear plugs, steel-toe boots â before any tool is switched on.
  2. Check discs and electrodes: correct type, undamaged, within expiry, matched to material and thickness.
  3. Set the machine: amperage, gas flow, wire speed, or standoff verified against the material chart before the first cut or arc.
  4. Clamp the work: nothing moves during cutting, grinding, bending, or welding â no hand-held workpieces under a disc.
