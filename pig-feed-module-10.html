Lesson 10.1~12 min

What Antibiotic-Free Really Means and What It Will Cost You

In this lesson
  • Distinguish between growth promotion, preventive and therapeutic antibiotic use
  • Summarise the regulatory position on antibiotic growth promoters in the major markets
  • Estimate the performance and financial gap your farm would face going antibiotic-free

Before you promise a buyer antibiotic-free pork, be clear about what you are promising. There are three distinct uses of antibiotics in pig production and they are not equivalent, either ethically or commercially.

Growth promotion means low doses fed continuously to healthy pigs to improve growth and feed conversion. This is what regulators worldwide have been eliminating. Prevention, also called prophylaxis or metaphylaxis, means medicating a whole group of healthy or at-risk pigs to stop a disease that usually appears at that stage. Therapy means treating an animal that is actually sick, on veterinary diagnosis, and this remains legitimate everywhere. Most schemes that call themselves antibiotic-free actually mean no growth promotion and no routine prevention, with therapeutic treatment permitted but the treated animal removed from the programme. A scheme that forbids treating a sick pig is not a welfare-acceptable scheme and you should not sign it.

The global regulatory picture as of 2024-2025 sets the direction of travel. The European Union has had a complete ban on antibiotic growth promoters since 2006, permitting only probiotics, phytogenics, organic acids and enzymes as performance additives. Canada restricted medically important antibiotics to prescription and prohibited growth promotion claims across 2018 to 2020. China imposed a complete ban on antibiotic growth promoters in feed in 2020. The United States ran a voluntary FDA phase-out to 2023 with the transition ongoing through 2025. Brazil and Argentina have growing but not mandated antibiotic-free production, driven by market demand rather than law. Most African countries have weaker enforcement, but exporters and international supermarket chains apply their own standards regardless of local law, so the practical rule for an ambitious producer is to work to the stricter standard.

Now the honest economics. Published summaries put the performance gap between antibiotic growth promoter use and antibiotic-free production at 0 to 3 percent in growth and feed conversion where optimal alternatives are used, widening to 5 to 15 percent where management is suboptimal. That single sentence is the whole business case. If your pens are clean, dry and correctly stocked, if you wean at a sensible age, if water flows properly and if you phase-feed, you will barely notice the difference. If your management is weak, going antibiotic-free will cost you real money and you should fix management first.

Worked example. A producer in Arusha, Tanzania, finishes 200 pigs a year to about 90 kg liveweight. Suppose each pig consumes roughly 230 kg of feed from weaning to market and feed costs 0.45 USD per kg, giving 103.50 USD of feed per pig and 20,700 USD across the year. A 3 percent feed conversion penalty costs 0.03 x 20,700 = 621 USD a year. A 10 percent penalty, which is what poor management would produce, costs 2,070 USD a year. On top of that, alternative additives might add 30 to 60 USD per tonne of nursery feed. If nursery feed is 25 percent of total tonnage, that is roughly 200 x 230 x 0.25 = 11,500 kg or 11.5 tonnes, so at 45 USD per tonne the additives cost about 518 USD. Total realistic annual cost with good management: about 1,140 USD, or 5.70 USD per pig. Whether that works depends entirely on the premium, which is the subject of lesson 6.

A few structural points to settle before you start:

  1. Decide who the programme covers. Most schemes require the pig to be antibiotic-free from birth, which means the sow's medication also matters.
  2. Decide what happens to a treated pig. You need a separate marketing channel for it, because you cannot sell it as antibiotic-free and you should not let it die untreated.
  3. Decide who keeps the records. If nobody owns the treatment book, the claim is worthless at audit.
  4. Decide the start date. Announce it, then run one batch as a trial before you commit to a buyer contract.

Antibiotic-free is a management system, not a feed additive. Producers who treat it as a purchasing decision fail. Producers who treat it as a hygiene, stocking and feeding discipline succeed and get paid for it.

Ranking the Alternatives: Evidence, Price and What to Buy First

In this lesson
  • Rank antibiotic alternatives into evidence tiers and explain the basis for each tier
  • Match specific alternatives to specific production stages and problems
  • Build a purchase priority list that spends limited capital where the evidence is strongest

Walk into any agro-dealer and you will be offered a dozen products promising to replace antibiotics. Most small producers waste their first year buying whichever one had the best sales visit. The published evidence lets you rank them, and ranking them lets you spend a small budget well.

Tier one, most extensively studied: probiotics, organic acids and phytogenics or essential oils. Tier two, moderate evidence: enzymes, immunostimulants and bacteriophages. Tier three, emerging with limited herd data: antimicrobial peptides and short-chain fatty acid salts. Buy from tier one first. Only move down the list when tier one is fully implemented.

Within tier one, match product to problem. If your issue is post-weaning scour in the first ten days, you want stomach pH control: formic acid at 0.35 to 1.4 percent of the diet, or lactic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent, which is particularly effective against ETEC-F18 with 10 to 15 percent PWD reduction reported in susceptible herds. If your issue is poor growth and gut integrity through the whole nursery, you want essential oils: a carvacrol, thymol and cinnamaldehyde blend at 100 to 150 mg per kg gave 15 to 25 percent better average daily gain over days 1 to 14 and 10 to 18 percent over days 1 to 42 in 2023 work, with taller villi, stronger tight junctions and higher trypsin, lipase and amylase activity. Notably, piglets given essential oils in the nursery kept a performance advantage into the fattening period, which means the benefit is not lost when you stop paying for it.

If your issue is inconsistent digestion of a fibrous local ration, you want enzymes. Non-starch polysaccharide enzymes such as xylanase and cellulase, plus protease, deliver about 2 to 5 percent feed conversion improvement, and phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg improves available phosphorus by 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points. Phytase is the most reliably profitable enzyme for a producer using plant-based diets, because 70 to 80 percent of the phosphorus in plant feeds is locked in phytate and unavailable. Reducing your dicalcium phosphate purchase often pays for the phytase outright.

Prebiotics are modest but cheap. Mannan oligosaccharides at 0.1 to 0.2 percent bind E. coli and Salmonella so they cannot adhere to the gut wall. Fructooligosaccharides at 0.5 to 1.0 percent selectively feed Lactobacillus. Expect 3 to 8 percent gain improvement and use them as a top-up, not a foundation. Immunostimulants such as beta-glucans from yeast cell wall act on toll-like receptors and improve antibody response, with a variable 3 to 5 percent production impact.

Worked purchase priority for a farm with 300 USD to spend on a first quarter of antibiotic-free feeding. Assume 3 tonnes of nursery feed in the quarter.

  1. Phytase across all diets: often self-funding through reduced mineral phosphate purchase. Budget 15 USD per tonne across 10 tonnes total feed = 150 USD, offset by phosphate savings.
  2. Lactic or formic acid in nursery phase 1 and 2: at 0.7 percent, 7 kg per tonne, 3 tonnes = 21 kg. At 2.00 USD per kg = 42 USD.
  3. Encapsulated essential oil in nursery: at 0.1 percent, 1 kg per tonne, 3 tonnes = 3 kg. At 25 USD per kg = 75 USD.
  4. Remainder to a Bacillus subtilis probiotic, which survives pelleting, roughly 33 USD.

Total 300 USD, all in tier one, all matched to the highest-risk stage. Note that all prices here are illustrative; collect your own quotes.

A real example. A cooperative of eleven producers outside Kumasi, Ghana, pooled orders so they could buy a 25 kg drum of encapsulated essential oil rather than 1 kg retail packs. Bulk buying cut their unit price substantially and gave them a consistent product across all eleven farms, which meant they could compare results honestly. Group purchasing is the single most effective cost lever available to smallholders on additives, because these products are priced steeply against small volumes.

One warning. Essential oils oxidise rapidly. Unencapsulated oil stored in a hot shed for six months is worth very little. Buy encapsulated product where you can, accept the 1.5 to 2 times premium, and store all additives in sealed containers out of sunlight.

Lesson 10.3~12 min

Growing Your Own Phytogenics: Oregano, Garlic, Turmeric and Local Plants

In this lesson
  • Identify the active compounds in common phytogenic plants and their published dose ranges
  • Assess the practical limits of farm-grown plant material compared with standardised products
  • Estimate the land area and labour needed to supply a phytogenic crop for your own herd

Phytogenics are plant-derived feed additives, and several of the most effective ones grow well across much of Africa. Growing your own is attractive because the imported, standardised products carry an import margin, a freight cost and a currency risk. But you must be honest about the trade-off: a bought product has a guaranteed concentration of active compound, and your garden does not.

Start with what the actives are. Oregano oil is the workhorse, containing more than 60 percent carvacrol plus thymol. Carvacrol is used at 5 to 15 mg per kg of diet and works by disrupting bacterial cell membranes. Thymol at 5 to 15 mg per kg is similar with additional antioxidant properties. Cinnamaldehyde from cinnamon at 2 to 5 mg per kg has a distinct antimicrobial profile. Blends of all three at 100 to 150 mg per kg of diet, meaning 0.05 to 0.15 percent, are what produced the strong 2023 nursery results. Garlic extract, thyme oil, turmeric and cinnamon are all listed as permitted antibiotic alternatives, with garlic and thyme acting as antimicrobials and anti-inflammatories, and cinnamon and turmeric working on immune enhancement and oxidative stress. The combined expected effect from phytochemicals used with good dietary management is about 3 to 8 percent average daily gain improvement.

Now the practical problem. Dried oregano leaf is not oregano oil. A leaf might be one to three percent essential oil by weight, but that varies enormously with variety, soil, rainfall, harvest timing and drying method, and you cannot measure it on farm. This means home-grown phytogenics should be treated as a supportive, low-cost supplement rather than a precisely dosed intervention. Do not tell a buyer you are dosing 120 mg per kg of carvacrol when you are feeding dried leaf.

What home-grown material does well:

  1. It adds antimicrobial and antioxidant pressure at almost no cash cost.
  2. It uses land and labour you may already have idle.
  3. It gives you a story for premium marketing, which Module 11 develops.
  4. It reduces, though does not eliminate, dependence on imported inputs.

What it does not do: replace a validated encapsulated product where you need consistent, measurable performance, such as in a nursery batch you have promised to a contract buyer.

Practical guidance for use. Harvest oregano, thyme or garlic before or at early flowering, when oil content is generally highest. Dry in shade, not direct sun, because ultraviolet light and heat drive off the volatile oils that you are trying to keep. Dry fast enough to prevent mould, because a mouldy herb crop reintroduces the mycotoxin risk from Module 9. Grind coarsely just before mixing, not months in advance, since ground material loses volatiles rapidly. Store in sealed containers away from light.

Worked example from Ethiopia. A producer near Bishoftu runs 30 finisher pigs and wants to include dried oregano leaf at a conservative 0.5 percent of the finisher diet. Thirty pigs eating 2.5 kg a day is 75 kg of feed a day, so 0.5 percent is 0.375 kg of dried leaf daily, about 137 kg a year. Dried leaf is roughly 20 to 25 percent of fresh weight, so that is around 550 to 685 kg of fresh material a year. Oregano yields vary widely with management, so rather than guess a yield figure, he plants a measured 100 square metre trial plot, harvests it twice, weighs what he actually gets, and then scales. That is the correct method: measure your own yield rather than trusting a number from another climate.

He also splits the difference. Home-grown oregano goes into the grower and finisher diets where the performance stakes are lower and the volumes are large. A bought, encapsulated blend goes into the nursery diets where the risk is highest and the volumes are small. That is the pattern most successful low-capital producers converge on: buy precision where it matters, grow bulk where it does not.

One caution on palatability. Strong aromatic material can reduce feed intake if you overdo it. Introduce any new plant material gradually over a week, watch the trough, and back off if pigs slow down. Feed intake is worth more than any additive.

Lesson 10.4~12 min

Feeding the Sow So Her Piglets Do Not Need Antibiotics

In this lesson
  • Set late-gestation feeding levels that drive colostrum quality and piglet survival
  • Calculate a lactating sow's daily lysine and energy intake against published targets
  • Adjust sow diets for heat stress using energy density, electrolytes and betaine

The cheapest antibiotic you never buy is the one a well-fed sow makes unnecessary. Piglet survival in the first three days is driven almost entirely by colostrum, and colostrum is driven by how you fed the sow in the last three weeks of pregnancy. Get this right and your scour rate, your pre-weaning mortality and your medication bill all fall together.

Gestation has three distinct feeding stages. Days 0 to 28 and days 28 to 90 both require about 5.9 Mcal of metabolisable energy per day and roughly 8 to 10 g of standardised ileal digestible lysine per day, at a diet level of 0.50 to 0.56 percent SID lysine. Then at day 90 everything changes. From days 90 to 112 the requirement jumps to 8.8 Mcal ME per day and about 12 to 14 g SID lysine per day, with the diet moving to 0.56 to 0.60 percent SID lysine. That jump is not arbitrary: it is mammary development. The sow is building the udder tissue that will produce colostrum and milk. Underfeed here and you get thin colostrum with lower immunoglobulin concentration, and thin colostrum means weak piglets that need medicating.

Colostrum should run at 20 to 25 percent solids compared with 12 to 14 percent for normal milk from day 4 onward, with 4 to 8 percent fat, 5 to 15 percent protein including 5 to 10 percent immunoglobulins, and 1.0 to 1.2 Mcal per kg of energy. Late gestation energy intake drives the solids percentage directly. Note also that gilts and young sows of parity zero to two produce colostrum with fewer immunoglobulins than mature multiparous sows, so first-litter gilts deserve more attention, better vaccination history and, if you practise it, cross-fostering that gives them slightly smaller litters.

Body condition frames all of this. Target a body condition score of 3.5 to 4.0 on the nine-point scale for gilts, because they are still growing their own skeleton. Older sows at parity two and above can sit at 2.5 to 3.5 provided condition is not severely depleted from the previous lactation. Gestating sows tolerate 6 to 8 percent crude fibre, which is useful because bulk keeps a restricted-fed sow satisfied and reduces the aggression and stereotypies that come with hunger.

Lactation is the opposite problem: you cannot feed her enough. Target intake is about 6.4 kg of feed a day on a diet around 3,362 kcal ME per kg, giving roughly 21,500 kcal ME a day. Sixty-five to eighty percent of that energy goes straight into milk. SID lysine target is about 60 g a day, which lands the diet somewhere between 0.77 and 1.34 percent SID lysine depending on how much she actually eats, with crude protein of 14 to 16 percent. Keep crude fibre lower here, at 3 to 4 percent, so you are not displacing energy with bulk. Feed ad libitum with no restriction whatsoever, and give unlimited clean water, because dehydration cuts feed intake before anything else does. Sows eating under about 6.4 kg a day at peak lactation risk poor litter growth and mastitis-metritis-agalactia, and MMA is one of the most common reasons small farms reach for antibiotics.

Heat stress is the African-specific problem. Above about 29 degrees Celsius, lactating sow feed intake falls 15 to 30 percent. She physically cannot eat enough. Three adjustments help: raise energy density from around 3,362 to 3,500 or more kcal ME per kg by adding 2 to 3 percent fat; raise sodium by 0.1 to 0.2 percent and potassium by 0.1 percent to support appetite signalling; and consider betaine at 0.1 to 0.2 percent for osmoregulation. Alongside that, cool her physically with wallows, shade, roof insulation and water.

Worked example. A producer in Ibadan, Nigeria, has a sow eating only 4.8 kg a day in the hot season instead of 6.4 kg. On a 3,362 kcal per kg diet she is getting 4.8 x 3,362 = 16,138 kcal instead of 21,517, a deficit of about 5,379 kcal a day, or 25 percent. If he raises energy density to 3,500 kcal per kg by adding 3 percent palm oil, her 4.8 kg now delivers 4.8 x 3,500 = 16,800 kcal. That recovers only 662 kcal, about 12 percent of the deficit. The lesson is blunt: nutrition alone cannot fix heat stress. He also needs to feed at 05:00 and 19:00 when it is cool, install a wallow, and wet the floor at midday. Feed formulation and cooling must work together.

Also remember linoleic acid at 125 g a day and alpha-linolenic acid at 10 g a day during lactation. The alpha-linolenic acid supports piglet neurological development through colostrum, and both are easily supplied by including an oilseed or vegetable oil source.

Lesson 10.5~12 min

Records, Withdrawal Periods and Passing an Organic or Antibiotic-Free Audit

In this lesson
  • Build a treatment record system that survives a buyer audit using only paper
  • Apply withdrawal periods correctly and identify treated animals reliably
  • List the documentation an organic or antibiotic-free certifier will typically demand

The pork is not antibiotic-free because you say so. It is antibiotic-free because you can prove it. Every scheme, every certifier and every serious buyer will ask for the same things, and a producer who keeps three simple books can pass an audit without any computer at all.

Book one is the treatment register. Every entry needs: date, animal or pen identity, what you observed, the product name, the batch number from the bottle, the dose given, who gave it, the withdrawal period from the label, and the calculated date on which that animal is clear. That last column is the one auditors look at first, and it is the one most farms leave blank. Calculate it at the time of treatment, not later.

Book two is the feed and input register. Every batch of feed you buy or mix: date, supplier or mix date, ingredients and inclusion rates, any additives, and which pens it went to. This is what proves you did not have a medicated premix in the feed. If you buy compounded feed, keep the bag label or take a photograph of it. If a supplier will not tell you in writing that a feed is free of antibiotic growth promoters, assume it is not.

Book three is the movement and sales register: what arrived, from where, what left, to whom, and on what date. This is what lets an auditor trace a specific carcass back to a specific pen and a specific feed batch. Traceability is the whole point.

Withdrawal periods deserve their own discipline. The withdrawal period is the time that must pass between the last dose and slaughter so that residues fall below legal limits. It is printed on every product label and it differs by product and sometimes by route of administration. Two rules keep you safe. First, mark the animal physically at the moment of treatment, with a livestock marker crayon, an ear tag of a distinctive colour, or a spray stripe, and re-mark as it fades. Second, write the clearance date on the pen card in large figures, not just in the book. When a trader arrives unexpectedly with cash and a truck, the pen card is what stops a mistake.

For organic certification the requirements go further and vary by certifier, so obtain the actual standard document from the body you intend to use rather than relying on general advice. Typically you should expect requirements covering: organic or approved feed sourcing with documentation for every ingredient; a conversion or transition period for the land and sometimes for the animals; restrictions on synthetic inputs including many premix components; outdoor access and space allowances; prohibition of routine preventive medication; permitted treatment of sick animals with mandatory record-keeping and often loss of organic status for that animal; and an annual on-farm inspection. Certification carries a real annual fee, so establish that fee and the achievable premium before you begin, not after.

Worked example. A producer in Nakuru, Kenya, treats a sick grower on 3 March with an antibiotic whose label states a 28-day withdrawal. He writes 3 March in the register, adds 28 days, and records 31 March as the clearance date. He sprays a red stripe on the pig's back and writes PIG 44 CLEAR 31 MARCH in large letters on the pen card. On 20 March a trader offers a good price for the whole pen. The card stops him selling pig 44. He sells the other nine and holds pig 44 for eleven more days. That single card protected his buyer contract, which was worth far more than one pig sold eleven days early.

A practical warning about mixed messages. If you keep both medicated and unmedicated feed on the same farm, store them in physically separate places with different coloured sacks or clear painted labels, and never use the same scoop. Cross-contamination in the feed room is one of the most common reasons audits fail, and it is entirely preventable at zero cost.

Finally, keep your records for as long as the scheme requires, usually at least two years, and keep them dry. A rain-damaged book is the same as no book.

Lesson 10.6~12 min

Costing the Change and Pricing the Premium

In this lesson
  • Build a full incremental cost model for switching to antibiotic-free production
  • Calculate the break-even premium per kilogram your farm requires
  • Negotiate and document a premium price agreement with a buyer

Antibiotic-free production only makes sense if somebody pays for it. This lesson turns everything in Module 10 into a single number: the extra cents per kilogram of pork you need, and whether your market will pay it.

Build the cost side in four blocks.

Block one, additive cost. This is the easiest to calculate and usually the smallest. Take your annual feed tonnage by phase, multiply the nursery tonnage by your additive cost per tonne from lesson 2, and add any phytase or enzyme cost across all phases, less the mineral phosphate you no longer buy.

Block two, performance cost. Take your annual feed spend and multiply by your realistic expected performance gap. With good management the published figure is 0 to 3 percent. Be conservative and use 3 percent in year one, then measure your own result and revise.

Block three, management investment. This is the block producers forget and the one that decides success. It covers whatever must change so the 0 to 3 percent gap applies rather than the 5 to 15 percent one: reducing stocking density, fixing drinkers, adding all-in all-out pen scheduling, improving ventilation, raising weaning age, improving the farrowing house. Some of these are capital, some are simply a decision to keep fewer pigs per pen.

Block four, compliance cost. Records, marking materials, any certification fee, and the sales value lost on treated pigs that must be diverted out of the programme.

Worked example. Return to the Arusha producer from lesson 1: 200 pigs a year, 230 kg of feed per pig, 0.45 USD per kg, so 20,700 USD of feed a year and 46,000 kg total feed. Assume nursery is 25 percent of tonnage, so 11.5 tonnes.

  1. Additives: 11.5 tonnes x 45 USD per tonne = 518 USD.
  2. Performance at 3 percent: 0.03 x 20,700 = 621 USD.
  3. Management: he cuts stocking from 12 to 10 pigs per finisher pen, which means building two extra pens. Say 900 USD, depreciated over five years = 180 USD a year, plus 120 USD of drinker repairs and shade netting in year one.
  4. Compliance: 150 USD of record materials, marker crayons and a share of an inspection cost, plus an estimated 5 treated pigs a year sold at 15 USD below programme price = 75 USD.

Total incremental annual cost: 518 + 621 + 180 + 120 + 150 + 75 = 1,664 USD.

Now the output side. Two hundred pigs at 90 kg liveweight is 18,000 kg. Break-even premium = 1,664 / 18,000 = 0.092 USD per kg liveweight, roughly 9 cents. If he sells on a carcass basis at about 72 percent dressing, his 18,000 kg liveweight is roughly 12,960 kg of carcass, and the break-even premium becomes 1,664 / 12,960 = 0.128 USD per kg carcass, about 13 cents. Always state clearly whether your premium is quoted on liveweight or carcass weight, because the difference is nearly 40 percent and this is where negotiations go wrong.

So he needs 13 cents per kilogram of carcass just to break even. If the hotel group he supplies offers 0.30 USD per kg carcass premium, he makes 0.30 x 12,960 = 3,888 USD against 1,664 USD of cost, a gain of 2,224 USD, which is 11.12 USD per pig. That is a real business. If they offer 0.10 USD per kg, he loses money and should either negotiate harder or stay conventional and simply improve his management, which was going to help him anyway.

Negotiating the premium. Bring three things to the meeting: your written records showing you can substantiate the claim, a consistent supply schedule showing how many pigs you can deliver each month, and a sample. Buyers pay premiums for reliability more than for stories. Ask for the premium to be written into a supply agreement with a stated price per kilogram, a stated basis (liveweight or carcass), a stated quality specification, and a stated volume. A verbal premium disappears the first time the market softens.

Finally, revisit the numbers every six months. Input prices in most African markets move fast, and a premium that was generous last year may not cover this year's additive cost. The producers who hold premiums longest are the ones who can show a buyer exactly what their costs are.

Performance cost assumption, year one

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which antibiotic use remains legitimate in essentially all antibiotic-free schemes?

Treating a genuinely sick animal on veterinary diagnosis is legitimate. The treated pig is normally removed from the antibiotic-free programme and sold through another channel.

2. When did the European Union's complete ban on antibiotic growth promoters take effect?

The EU ban has been in force from 2006 onward, permitting only probiotics, phytogenics, organic acids and enzymes as performance additives.

3. A farm with poor hygiene and overcrowding removes antibiotic growth promoters. What performance penalty should it expect?

The published gap widens to 5-15 percent where management is suboptimal, which is why management must be corrected before antibiotics are withdrawn.

4. Which country imposed a complete ban on antibiotic growth promoters in feed in 2020?

China's 2020 complete feed AGP ban was a major global market driver for alternative products.

5. Why does a sow's medication history matter to an antibiotic-free claim on her piglets?

Most programmes define the claim from birth onward, so treatments given to the sow while the litter is suckling can disqualify the piglets.

6. Which group of alternatives has the strongest published evidence base?

Probiotics, organic acids and phytogenics or essential oils are the most extensively studied. Bacteriophages and antimicrobial peptides remain emerging with limited herd-level data.

7. Roughly what proportion of phosphorus in plant-based feeds is phytate-bound and unavailable to the pig?

About 70-80 percent of plant phosphorus is locked in phytate, which is why microbial phytase at 500-2,500 FTU/kg is so widely used.

8. An essential oil blend of carvacrol, thymol and cinnamaldehyde produced what average daily gain response over days 1 to 14 in 2023 work?

The reported response was +15-25 percent ADG over days 1-14, narrowing to +10-18 percent over days 1-42, with 5-10 percent better feed conversion.

9. Why is microencapsulation recommended for essential oils?

Essential oils oxidise quickly. A lipid matrix protects them through the gastrointestinal tract for release in the small intestine, at a 1.5-2x cost premium.

10. What is the most effective cost lever available to smallholders buying feed additives?

Additives are priced steeply against small volumes. Pooling orders across producers cuts unit cost and gives a consistent product for honest comparison.

11. What proportion of oregano essential oil is made up of carvacrol and thymol?

Oregano oil is more than 60 percent carvacrol plus thymol, which is why it is the most commonly used phytogenic base.

12. Why should phytogenic herbs be dried in shade rather than direct sun?

The active compounds are volatile. Heat and UV exposure during drying strip out exactly what you are trying to preserve.

13. What is the main weakness of farm-grown phytogenic material compared with a bought encapsulated product?

Oil content varies with variety, soil, rainfall, harvest timing and drying, and cannot be measured on farm, so precise dosing is impossible.

14. Which strategy do most low-capital producers converge on?

Nursery performance carries the highest risk on small volumes, justifying a bought standardised product. Grower and finisher volumes are large and the stakes lower, suiting home-grown material.

15. What is the first sign that you have included too much aromatic plant material in a ration?

Strong aromatics can suppress palatability. Since feed intake drives everything, introduce new plant material gradually and reduce it if pigs slow at the trough.

16. Why does the sow's energy requirement rise sharply from day 90 of gestation?

Days 90-112 are the mammary development phase. Energy rises from 5.9 to 8.8 Mcal ME per day, and underfeeding here produces thin, low-immunoglobulin colostrum.

17. What is the target daily SID lysine intake for a high-producing lactating sow?

The published target is approximately 60 g of SID lysine per day, which translates to 0.77-1.34 percent of the diet depending on feed intake.

18. What proportion of a lactating sow's energy intake goes into milk production?

Milk production takes 65-80 percent of total energy, with maintenance taking 15-25 percent and body tissue mobilisation making up the rest.

19. Colostrum solids should be in what range, compared with normal milk from day 4?

Colostrum runs at 20-25 percent solids against 12-14 percent for later milk. Late gestation energy intake drives that solids percentage.

20. Above roughly what ambient temperature does lactating sow feed intake begin to fall by 15-30 percent?

Above about 29 degrees Celsius (85 F), intake falls 15-30 percent, creating an energy deficit that requires both higher diet energy density and physical cooling.

21. Which column do auditors look at first in a treatment register?

The clearance date proves the withdrawal period was respected. It should be calculated at the moment of treatment, not reconstructed later.

22. A pig is treated on 3 March with a product carrying a 28-day withdrawal period. When is it clear for slaughter?

3 March plus 28 days is 31 March. Writing this on the pen card as well as in the book prevents an accidental early sale.

23. What is a leading and entirely preventable cause of audit failure on small farms?

Shared scoops and shared storage let medicated feed reach unmedicated pigs. Separate storage and separate scoops cost nothing.

24. What should you do before beginning organic certification?

Certification carries a real annual cost. Confirming both the fee and what buyers will actually pay determines whether the whole exercise is profitable.

25. Why should the withdrawal clearance date be written on the pen card and not only in the register?

Sales decisions get made at the pen, often quickly. The card is what stops a treated animal leaving during its withdrawal period.

26. Which cost block do producers most often forget when costing a switch to antibiotic-free production?

Management investment is what determines whether you face the 0-3 percent performance gap or the 5-15 percent one, and it is routinely left out of the budget.

27. A farm has $1,664 of incremental annual cost and sells 12,960 kg of carcass. What premium does it need to break even?

1,664 divided by 12,960 is about 0.128, or roughly 13 cents per kilogram of carcass weight.

28. Why must a premium always specify liveweight or carcass basis?

With a dressing percentage around 72, the same premium expressed per kilogram means very different money on each basis. Ambiguity here is where negotiations break down.

29. What do premium buyers value most highly from a small producer?

Reliability and provable claims are what let a buyer build a menu or a product line around your pork. Story alone does not survive a stock-out.

30. How often should a producer revisit the premium calculation?

Input prices move quickly in most African markets. Reviewing every six months keeps the premium covering actual cost and gives you evidence for renegotiation.

Module 10 capstone

Design and cost a twelve-month antibiotic-reduction plan for your own herd. Step 1: list every antibiotic you used in the last six months, why you used it, at what cost, and at which pig age. Step 2: classify each use as therapeutic (a sick animal treated on veterinary advice), preventive (routine in-feed medication of healthy pigs), or growth promotion. Step 3: for every preventive and growth-promotion use, propose a specific replacement from this module, priced per tonne of feed using quotes you collect yourself from at least two local suppliers. Step 4: identify the three management changes on your farm (stocking density, water flow rate, all-in all-out pen use, weaning age, ventilation) that must change for the replacements to work, and estimate what each costs. Step 5: interview at least one potential premium buyer, such as a butchery, hotel, restaurant or supermarket, and record in writing what they would pay extra per kilogram for documented antibiotic-free pork and what proof they would demand. Step 6: build a one-page profit-and-loss comparison of your current system against the antibiotic-free system, including the higher feed cost, the expected 0-3 percent performance difference achievable with good management, and the premium price. State clearly whether the change makes money on your farm and what would have to be true for it to do so.
