1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Repair welding is the skill of fixing broken metal parts instead of buying new ones. In Africa, where equipment is expensive and spare parts can take weeks to arrive, this skill saves money and keeps businesses running. Imagine a farmer's plough breaks during planting season. A new one costs 500,000 shillings and takes a month to order. A repair welder can fix it in two hours for 20,000 shillings. That's why repair welding matters.

Repair welding is different from production welding. In production, you weld new, clean metal. In repair, you deal with dirty, rusty, oil-covered, cracked parts. You must clean, gouge, and sometimes preheat. You also need to match the filler metal to the base metal. For example, cast iron needs nickel rods, not mild steel rods. If you use the wrong rod, the repair will crack again.

Common repair jobs include cracked engine blocks, broken gear teeth, worn shafts, leaky tanks, and broken agricultural tools. Each requires a different approach. A cracked water tank needs a watertight weld, while a broken gear tooth needs a strong weld that can handle stress. You must assess the damage before you strike an arc.

Safety is critical. Repair welding often involves grinding, cutting, and welding in awkward positions. Always wear a welding helmet, gloves, and leather apron. Ensure good ventilation, especially when welding galvanized or painted metal. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. In many African workshops, space is tight, so clear the area of flammable materials like petrol, paint, or dry grass.

This module will teach you the tools, methods, and safety practices for repair welding. You will learn to fix common metal parts using arc welding, oxy-acetylene, and brazing. By the end, you will be able to take a broken part, assess it, and repair it to working condition. This skill can earn you a good income, as few welders master repair work.

2. Tools and Materials

To do repair welding, you need more than just a welding machine. Start with a reliable arc welder. A 200-amp inverter welder is good for most repairs. It runs on 220V single phase, common in many African homes and workshops. If you have three-phase power, a 300-amp machine is better for heavy repairs. You also need an oxy-acetylene torch set for heating, cutting, and brazing. This is essential for cast iron repairs and for loosening rusted bolts.

Your tool kit should include an angle grinder with 115mm or 230mm discs. You'll need grinding discs for cleaning and cutting discs for removing cracks. A wire brush for cleaning, chipping hammer for slag, and a set of files for finishing. For measuring and marking, have a steel ruler, center punch, and soapstone. A good vice and clamps are needed to hold parts while you weld. In a typical Kenyan jua kali workshop, you'll find these tools, but you may need to improvise.

Filler metals are crucial. For mild steel, use E6013 or E7018 electrodes. E6013 is easy to strike and good for general repairs. E7018 is stronger and used for critical parts. For cast iron, use nickel-based electrodes like ENi-Cl. For stainless steel, use 308L or 316L rods. For brazing, use silicon bronze or brass rods with flux. Always store electrodes in a dry place. Moisture causes porosity and weak welds.

Safety gear includes a welding helmet with at least shade 10 lens, leather gloves, leather apron, safety boots, and safety glasses for grinding. Ear plugs are good when grinding or hammering. Have a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) and a bucket of sand or water. For cleaning, keep acetone or brake cleaner for removing oil, and a grinder for rust. Never use petrol or diesel to clean parts; they are fire hazards.

Other useful items: a MIG welder for thin sheet metal repairs, a plasma cutter for quick cutting, and a die grinder for hard-to-reach areas. A magnetic welding holder helps position parts. A heat gun or blowtorch for preheating small parts. Keep a notebook to record settings for different jobs. With these tools and materials, you can handle most repair welding tasks.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Assess the damage. Clean the part with a wire brush and grinder to see the full extent of the crack or wear. Use a dye penetrant or simply look closely. Mark the ends of the crack with a center punch to stop it from spreading. If it's a broken piece, check for alignment. Take photos if needed.

Step 2: Prepare the part. Remove all oil, paint, rust, and moisture. Use acetone or brake cleaner for oil. Grind a V-groove along the crack to get full penetration. For a crack in thick metal, grind from both sides if possible. For cast iron, drill a small hole at each end of the crack to relieve stress. Preheat cast iron to 500-600C using an oxy-acetylene torch. This prevents cracking. For mild steel, preheat is not usually needed unless the part is very thick.

Step 3: Set up your welding machine. For mild steel, use E6013 rod at 90-120 amps for 3.2mm rod. For E7018, use 110-140 amps. For cast iron with nickel rod, use 80-100 amps and DC reverse polarity (electrode positive). For brazing, use a neutral flame and heat the part to a dull red (around 800C). Practice on scrap metal first.

Step 4: Weld the repair. For cracks, start welding from the end of the crack towards the center, using a backstep technique to reduce distortion. Use a stringer bead or slight weave. For cast iron, weld short beads (20-30mm) and peen each bead with a chipping hammer while hot to relieve stress. Let it cool slowly. For brazing, heat the part and apply flux, then dip the filler rod. The filler should flow into the joint by capillary action.

Step 5: Inspect and finish. After welding, let the part cool slowly. For cast iron, bury it in sand or wrap it in a heat blanket. Clean the weld with a wire brush and grinder. Check for cracks or porosity. If needed, re-weld. For a watertight repair, test with water or air pressure. For a shaft, check for straightness and machine if necessary. Finally, paint or oil the repaired area to prevent rust. Record what you did for future reference.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Welding dirty or oily metal. This causes porosity, cracks, and weak welds. Fix: Always clean the part with a grinder and acetone before welding. Remove all paint, rust, and oil. For cast iron, bake the part to remove oil.

Mistake 2: Using the wrong filler metal. For example, using mild steel rod on cast iron will crack. Fix: Identify the base metal first. Use a magnet test or spark test. Cast iron sparks are short and red; mild steel sparks are long and yellow. Use nickel rod for cast iron, stainless rod for stainless steel.

Mistake 3: Not preheating cast iron. Cast iron is brittle and cracks when welded cold. Fix: Preheat to 500-600C with a torch. Weld in short passes and cool slowly. If you can't preheat, use nickel rod and peen the weld.

Mistake 4: Welding a crack without grooving. This leads to incomplete penetration. Fix: Grind a V-groove along the entire crack. For thick metal, groove from both sides. Make sure the groove is clean and wide enough for the rod.

Mistake 5: Welding in a draft or cold environment. This causes rapid cooling and cracking. Fix: Shield the work area from wind. Preheat the part if possible. For cast iron, cool slowly by burying in sand or using a heat blanket.

Mistake 6: Not controlling distortion. Welding heats and expands metal, causing warping. Fix: Use clamps and jigs to hold the part. Weld small sections and alternate sides. Use backstep welding. For long cracks, weld from the center outwards.

Mistake 7: Poor penetration on thick metal. Fix: Increase amperage, use a larger rod, or bevel the joint. For 10mm steel, use 4.0mm rod at 140-180 amps. Weld from both sides if possible.

Mistake 8: Ignoring safety. Welding fumes, arc eye, and burns are common. Fix: Wear proper PPE. Use a fume extractor or weld outdoors. Never look at the arc without a helmet. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby.

Mistake 9: Not testing the repair. A weld that looks good may fail under load. Fix: Test with a hammer, water pressure, or load. For critical parts, use non-destructive testing like dye penetrant.

Mistake 10: Rushing the job. Repair welding takes patience. Fix: Plan your steps, prepare properly, and take your time. A good repair lasts years; a bad one fails in days.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in repair welding is not optional. You are working with electricity, fire, and toxic fumes. Start with personal protective equipment (PPE). Wear a welding helmet with a shade 10 to 13 lens, depending on the amperage. Use leather gloves, a leather apron, and safety boots. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts and sticks to skin. Use safety glasses under your helmet for grinding and chipping. Ear plugs protect against grinding noise.

Ventilation is critical. Welding fumes contain metal oxides and gases that can cause lung damage. In a closed workshop, use a fume extractor or open doors and windows. When welding galvanized steel, which is coated with zinc, the fumes cause metal fume fever. Drink milk before and after, and weld outdoors if possible. Never weld in a confined space without forced ventilation.

Fire safety: Repair welding often involves sparks flying up to 10 meters. Clear the area of flammable materials like petrol, paint, thinners, dry grass, and paper. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) and a bucket of sand or water. Wear a leather apron to protect from sparks. After welding, stay in the area for at least 30 minutes to watch for smoldering fires.

Electrical safety: Check your welding cables for damage. Do not weld in wet conditions. Use a dry wooden board to stand on if the floor is wet. Ensure your machine is properly grounded. Never change electrodes with bare hands or wet gloves. Turn off the machine when not in use.

Best practices: Always plan your repair. Assess the damage, choose the right filler, and prepare the surface. Use clamps and jigs to hold parts. Weld in short passes to control heat. For cast iron, preheat and cool slowly. For critical parts, use a qualified welder or get your work inspected. Keep a log of repairs, including settings and results. Learn from each job.

In Africa, many workshops are informal. Set a good example by following safety rules. Train your apprentices. Keep your tools in good condition. A clean, safe workshop is a productive workshop. Remember, a repair that fails can cause injury or death. Never compromise on safety.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will repair a cracked mild steel bracket. You will need: a 200-amp arc welder, E6013 electrodes (3.2mm), an angle grinder with grinding and cutting discs, a wire brush, a chipping hammer, a steel ruler, a center punch, a vice, and safety gear (helmet, gloves, apron, boots).

Step 1: Obtain a cracked bracket. If you don't have one, take a 6mm thick steel plate, 100mm x 100mm, and cut a 50mm crack with a hacksaw or cutting disc. Clean the bracket with a grinder and wire brush. Remove all rust and paint. Use acetone to degrease.

Step 2: Mark the ends of the crack with a center punch. This stops the crack from spreading. Grind a V-groove along the entire crack, about 2mm deep and 3mm wide. Use a cutting disc to open the crack if it's tight.

Step 3: Set up your welder. Set the amperage to 100-120 amps for 3.2mm E6013 rod. Connect the ground clamp to the bracket. Put on your PPE.

Step 4: Weld the crack. Start at one end of the crack. Use a backstep technique: weld from the end towards the center in short beads of about 20mm. Clean each bead with a chipping hammer and wire brush. Continue until the crack is filled. For a 50mm crack, you should use about 3-4 beads. Let the bracket cool between beads to avoid distortion.

Step 5: Inspect the weld. After cooling, clean the weld with a wire brush. Look for cracks, porosity, or undercut. If you find defects, grind them out and re-weld. Use a dye penetrant if available.

Step 6: Test the repair. Clamp the bracket in a vice and try to bend it. If the weld holds, it's good. If it breaks, examine the fracture to see why. Common issues: lack of penetration, slag inclusion, or wrong amperage. Adjust and try again.

Step 7: Finish the repair. Grind the weld smooth if needed. Paint the bracket to prevent rust. Record your settings and observations in a notebook. Repeat the exercise with different crack lengths and positions. Practice on cast iron and stainless steel when you are confident. This exercise builds the skills you need for real repair jobs.

<p style="margin:0; font-size:13px; color:#1a1a1a;">Never weld on galvanised (zinc-coated), lead-painted, or cadmium-plated components without full respiratory protection (P3/FFP3 minimum, ideally supplied-air) and forced ventilation. <h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Identify the base metal first.</strong> Spark test, magnet test, or ask the client. Unknown metal = unknown weld procedure.</li>
<li><strong>Stop-drill crack tips</strong> (35 mm) before grinding to prevent propagation during repair.</li>
<li><strong>Clean to bright metal</strong> 5075 mm either side of the joint. No paint, oil, rust, or moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Preheat cast iron and thick steel</strong> (200300 C for cast iron). Use torch, charcoal bed, or heat blanket.</li>
<li><strong>Match filler to base metal</strong> NiFe for cast iron, ER4043 for aluminium, ER308L for 304 stainless, E7018 for structural steel.</li>
<li><strong>Back-step and stitch</strong> to control heat input and distortion. Never run a continuous bead on a restrained repair.</li>
<li><strong>Peen and slow-cool</strong> cast-iron repairs in dry sand, ash, or vermiculite for at least 4 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Document the repair:</strong> photo, date, filler batch, and client sign-off. This is your warranty trail and your marketing asset.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Business Notes for African Workshops</h3>
<p>Repair welding is a cash-flow business, not a capital business. Your margin comes from three levers: (1) <strong>speed of response</strong> mobile rigs that reach a farm or mine site within 24 hours can charge 23 the shop rate; (2) <strong>consumable discipline</strong> nickel electrodes cost 815 mild steel, so a mis-quoted cast-iron job can wipe out your profit; (3) <strong>repeat clients</strong> mills, dairies, and fleet operators repair the same equipment every 618 months. Build a client register with equipment type, last repair date, and filler used. That register is worth more than your welder.</p>

<p>For RISE AFRICA learners operating with intermittent grid power, invest in an engine-driven stick welder (e.g. 200 A petrol or diesel) before a MIG set. It handles 80% of repair jobs in the field, runs off-site, and is repairable with local parts. Pair it with a 4.5" angle grinder, a 200 A inverter for shop work, and a stock of E6013, E7018, ENiFe-CI, and ER4043. That kit, plus the procedures in this lesson, will win you more repair contracts than any certificate alone.</p>
