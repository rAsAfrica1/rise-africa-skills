1. Step-by-Step Drying of Mango Slices

Choose firm, ripe mangoes that are sweet but not mushy. In Africa, varieties like Kent, Julie, or local mangoes work well. Wash them, peel with a sharp knife or peeler, and slice lengthwise into 1 cm thick slices. Remove the pit. To prevent browning, dip slices in a solution of 1 teaspoon ascorbic acid (or lemon juice) per 1 litre of water for 2 minutes. Drain well.

Pre-treat by blanching: bring water to 95Â°C, drop slices in for 2 minutes, then immediately transfer to cold water. This sets colour and speeds drying. Alternatively, syrup-blanch in a 30% sugar solution (300 g sugar per 1 litre water) for 3 minutes for sweeter, chewier slices.

Arrange slices in a single layer on drying trays. Use a solar dryer or electric dehydrator. Set temperature to 57-60Â°C. If using an oven, set to lowest setting (50-60Â°C) and prop door open 5 cm. Drying takes 6-10 hours. Turn slices every 2 hours. They are ready when leathery, pliable, and no moisture beads appear when squeezed.

Cool completely, then pack in airtight jars or vacuum-sealed bags. Store in a cool, dark place. Properly dried mango slices last 6-12 months. For a 10 kg fresh mango batch, you get about 1.5-2 kg dried slices. Sell in 100 g or 250 g packs. In many African markets, dried mango sells for 3-5 times the fresh price.

2. Making Sweet and Chewy Banana Chips

Use firm, ripe but not overripe bananas. In East Africa, matoke or bogoya varieties are excellent. Peel and slice into 3-5 mm thick rounds or lengthwise strips. To keep them from browning, soak in 1 litre water with 1 tablespoon lemon juice for 10 minutes. Drain and pat dry.

For sweet and chewy chips, prepare a honey or sugar syrup: mix 250 g sugar or 200 ml honey with 500 ml water and 1 teaspoon cinnamon (optional). Bring to a boil, then add banana slices and simmer for 5 minutes. Remove and drain. This syrup infusion adds flavour and extends shelf life.

Dry at 55-60Â°C in a solar dryer or dehydrator for 8-12 hours. If using an oven, keep temperature at 50-60Â°C with door ajar. Turn chips every 2-3 hours. They are done when they bend without breaking and are dry to touch but still chewy. Over-drying makes them brittle.

Cool completely. Store in airtight containers. For 5 kg fresh bananas, you get about 1-1.5 kg chips. In Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria, sweet banana chips are a popular snack. Package in 50 g or 100 g sealed bags. Add a desiccant packet if humidity is high. Sell to schools, supermarkets, and online. Shelf life is 6-9 months if stored properly.

3. Drying Pineapple Rings and Chunks

Select ripe pineapples with a sweet aroma and golden skin. Remove top, base, and skin. Cut into 1 cm thick rings or 2 cm cubes. Remove eyes. For rings, core them. Soak in a solution of 1 teaspoon ascorbic acid per 1 litre water for 5 minutes to prevent browning.

Blanching is optional but recommended: dip in boiling water for 2 minutes, then cold water. This reduces drying time and preserves colour. For sweeter rings, blanch in light syrup (200 g sugar per 1 litre water) for 3 minutes.

Arrange on trays without overlapping. Dry at 55-60Â°C for 8-12 hours. Pineapple has high sugar and moisture, so it takes longer than mango. Turn every 2 hours. Rings are ready when leathery and pliable; chunks when springy but dry. In a solar dryer, it may take 1-2 days.

Cool and pack in airtight jars or vacuum bags. Store in a cool, dark place. From 10 kg fresh pineapple, you get about 1-1.5 kg dried product. Dried pineapple is a premium snack. In South Africa, Ghana, and Kenya, it sells well in health shops and export markets. You can also grind dried pineapple into powder for flavouring. Package in 100 g or 200 g bags. Shelf life is 9-12 months.

4. Drying Papaya, Guava, and Other Tropical Fruits

Papaya: Choose ripe but firm papayas. Peel, cut in half, remove seeds, and slice into 1 cm thick strips or cubes. Papaya can be bland, so soak in light syrup (200 g sugar + 1 teaspoon citric acid per 1 litre water) for 30 minutes. Dry at 55-60Â°C for 6-10 hours until leathery. Papaya dries faster than mango. From 8 kg fresh, get 1-1.5 kg dried.

Guava: Use ripe but firm guavas. Wash, cut ends, and slice into 5 mm thick rounds or halves. Remove seeds if desired. Guava is high in pectin, so it dries chewy. No pre-treatment needed, but a 2-minute blanch helps. Dry at 55-60Â°C for 6-8 hours. From 5 kg fresh, get about 1 kg dried. Guava chips are popular in Kenya and Nigeria.

Other tropical fruits: Pawpaw (same as papaya), soursop (remove seeds, slice 1 cm, dry 8-10 hours), and jackfruit (use ripe pods, dry 10-12 hours). Mango, banana, and pineapple are covered in other lessons. For all, ensure they are dry enough: no moisture when squeezed. Cool completely before packing. Store in airtight containers. Use 100 g or 250 g packs. In African markets, dried tropical fruit mixes are growing in demand. Label with drying date and best before.

5. Drying Berries and Small Fruits Like Strawberries and Mulberries

Berries are delicate and high in moisture. Use fresh, firm berries. Strawberries: hull and slice 5 mm thick. Mulberries: remove stems. Blueberries: blanch for 30 seconds to crack skins. For all, a 10-minute soak in 1 litre water with 1 tablespoon lemon juice helps preserve colour and vitamin C.

Dry at 50-55Â°C, lower than other fruits to prevent case hardening. Spread in a single layer on mesh trays. Drying takes 8-14 hours. Turn gently every 2 hours. They are ready when dry but still pliable, like raisins. Over-drying makes them hard. In humid African regions, use a dehydrator or solar dryer with good ventilation.

For 2 kg fresh strawberries, you get about 200-300 g dried. Mulberries yield similar. Pack in small airtight jars or vacuum bags. Add oxygen absorbers for longer shelf life. Store in cool, dark place. Dried berries are a premium product. Sell in 50 g or 100 g packs to health shops, bakeries, and hotels. In South Africa and Kenya, they fetch high prices. You can also mix with other dried fruits for trail mix. Shelf life is 6-12 months.

6. Flavoring Dried Fruits with Spices and Honey

Flavoring dried fruits adds value and market appeal. Start with fully dried fruit (mango, banana, pineapple, etc.). For honey coating: warm 200 ml honey with 50 ml water and 1 teaspoon cinnamon or ginger. Dip dried fruit pieces for 2 minutes, then drain. Return to dryer at 50Â°C for 1-2 hours to set the coating. This makes them glossy and sweet.

Spice blends: For a savory-sweet mix, combine 2 tablespoons sugar, 1 teaspoon chili powder, 1/2 teaspoon salt, and 1/4 teaspoon citric acid. Toss dried fruit in this mix. For a tropical blend, use 1 tablespoon coconut sugar, 1/2 teaspoon nutmeg, and 1/2 teaspoon vanilla powder. For a tangy coating, mix 1 tablespoon tamarind paste with 1 tablespoon honey and brush on.

Ensure coated fruits are dried again to prevent spoilage. The honey or sugar coating can attract moisture, so pack with desiccant. Label clearly: "Honey-Cinnamon Mango" or "Spicy Pineapple." These value-added products sell for 20-50% more than plain dried fruit. In African markets, spiced dried fruits are popular in urban areas and for export. Experiment with local spices like cloves, cardamom, or hibiscus powder. Always test small batches first. Store in airtight containers in a cool, dark place. Shelf life remains 6-12 months if properly dried and packed.

Flavouring Dried Fruits with Spices and Honey

Flavoured dried fruit is where small African processors differentiate and capture premium margins. The technique is simple: infuse before drying, or coat after drying. For honey-glazed banana chips, dip blanched slices in a 1:1 honey-water solution (pasteurised honey, 60 Â°C) for 10â15 minutes, drain, then dry at 55â60 Â°C. For spiced mango, dust with a blend of chilli, ginger, cinnamon, and a little salt after the fruit reaches 20% moisture, then return to the dryer for 30â60 minutes to set the coating. Always use food-grade spices and declare allergens (honey is not suitable for infants under 12 months â label accordingly).

Honey infusion adds sugars that bind water, so expect a slightly higher final moisture (up to 20%) and a shorter shelf life unless you use airtight packaging with a desiccant or vacuum seal. Test small batches, record your recipe, and keep a sensory log so you can reproduce winning flavours at scale.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Select and grade: Use ripe but firm fruit; reject mouldy, bruised, or over-ripe fruit before peeling.
  • Wash and sanitise: Use potable water; sanitise knives, trays, and drying racks between batches.
  • Pre-treat: Blanch or dip in citric/ascorbic acid to prevent browning and extend shelf life.
  • Uniform sizing: Cut slices to consistent thickness (5â10 mm) so all pieces dry at the same rate.
  • Control temperature: Hold 55â60 Â°C for most fruits; never exceed 70 Â°C or you get case-hardening.
  • Ensure airflow: Load trays no more than 1â2 kg/mÂ² and leave gaps for air movement.
  • Test moisture: Confirm 15â20% (or product-specific target) with a moisture meter or oven method before packing.
  • Cool and pack: Cool fully in a dry, clean room; pack in airtight, food-grade, moisture-barrier bags with a label showing date, batch, and allergens.
