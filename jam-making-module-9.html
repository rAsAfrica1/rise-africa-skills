JAM MAKING · MODULE 9 OF 12

Scaling from Kitchen to Commercial

From 20 jars a week to 500. Equipment, space, staff, production planning, and what changes when jam becomes a business.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Recognise the signs it is time to scale
  • Plan an equipment upgrade path from home kitchen to small commercial unit
  • Decide whether to build, rent, or share a production space
  • Design a production schedule that meets demand without burning you out
  • Decide when and how to hire your first staff
  • Cost a scaling step before you commit to it
Lesson 9.1

When to scale — and when not to

Scaling too early kills businesses. Scaling too late loses opportunities. This lesson is about reading the signs.

Signs it is time to scale

  • You are consistently selling out. Every batch sells within a week. You are turning customers away.
  • You have a waiting list or repeat requests from shops, hotels, or individuals you cannot supply.
  • You are regularly working past midnight to meet orders — the business is eating your life.
  • Your margins are healthy. You are making a real profit per jar, and the profit is enough to reinvest.
  • You have a reliable fruit supply that can support 2–5× your current volume.
  • You have 3–6 months of costs in reserve. Scaling requires upfront investment in equipment, stock, and staff — before the revenue catches up.

Signs you should NOT scale yet

  • You have not proven the product sells consistently. A few good months is not a pattern. Look for six months of steady sales.
  • Your margins are marginal. If you are barely profitable at 100 jars, you will not be profitable at 500.
  • You cannot maintain quality at higher volume. If batches already vary, more batches means more variation.
  • You do not have the cash reserve. Scaling eats cash before it produces cash. A business that scales without reserve often fails from cash flow, not from lack of demand.
  • You have not solved your basic bottlenecks. If you are always short of jars, or always waiting for fruit, those problems multiply when you scale.

The cash-flow trap of scaling

You buy jars, lids, labels, and ingredients for 500 jars. You produce them. You deliver to shops. The shops pay you in 30–60 days. Meanwhile you have paid all your costs upfront and your cash is locked in inventory and receivables. This is where businesses fail — not from lack of sales, but from running out of cash while waiting to be paid.

Before scaling, know your payment terms and have enough reserve to cover 3 months of operating costs.

Lesson 9.2

Equipment upgrades

The home kitchen works up to about 100 jars a month. Beyond that, you hit equipment walls. These are the upgrades, in order of priority.

The upgrade ladder

The order matters

Do not buy a labelling machine before you have a big enough pot. Do not rent commercial kitchen space before you have proven demand. Work the ladder in order — each upgrade solves the next bottleneck.

The realistic progression

  1. Home kitchen (0–100 jars/month) — 5L pot, domestic stove, hand filling. Works fine.
  2. Home kitchen with upgrades (100–300 jars/month) — 15L pot, second burner, chest freezer, better filling tools. Still home-based.
  3. Small commercial unit (300–1,000 jars/month) — rented kitchen, commercial stove, jar filler, water bath canner, first staff member.
  4. Small factory (1,000–5,000 jars/month) — purpose-built or long-term-rented kitchen, multiple burners, semi-automated filling, 3–10 staff, dedicated storage.

Each step roughly doubles your costs but triples your output. The trick is to move up only when the demand and margins support the higher cost.

Lesson 9.3

Space — build, rent, or share

Home kitchens are limited by food-safety regulations for commercial use. As you scale, you will eventually need a dedicated production space. There are three routes.

Route 1 — Build your own

Convert a shed, build a small kitchen, or construct a purpose-built unit.

ProsCons
Full control over layout and complianceHigh upfront capital ($3,000–15,000+)
No monthly rentBuilding permits, inspections, time
Can be expandedYou own the maintenance

Best for: established producers with clear 3–5 year growth plans and available capital or land.

Route 2 — Rent a commercial kitchen

Some towns have shared commercial kitchens, church kitchens, restaurant off-hours, or municipal food hubs available to rent by the hour or day.

ProsCons
No upfront capitalMonthly cost ($100–1,000)
Usually already compliant with food-safety regulationsScheduling conflicts with other users
Can scale up or downYou cannot customise the space

Best for: producers scaling from 200 to 800 jars/month, testing whether commercial volume works for them before committing to a lease.

Route 3 — Shared kitchen with other producers

Three or four small food businesses jointly rent a kitchen. Schedule rotations so each producer has specific days or hours.

ProsCons
Lower cost per producerCoordination effort
Shared equipment and compliance costsDisagreements over cleaning standards, storage, schedule
Community of producersLegal complexity if one member causes a food safety issue

Best for: producers in a market with a strong local food producer community and clear agreements on shared responsibilities.

The compliance question comes before the cost question

Before choosing any route, check with your local public health authority what is required for the volume you are planning. Some jurisdictions cap home-based production at a low number of units per month. Others require a separate licensed kitchen above a certain volume. Getting this wrong costs far more than the difference between renting and building.

Lesson 9.4

Production planning and scheduling

As volume grows, production shifts from "make jam when you feel like it" to "make jam to a schedule." This is where many small food businesses fail — they scale up sales before scaling up planning, and they miss deliveries, run out of stock, or over-produce and waste.

The production calendar

Plan in three horizons:

  1. Annual — how much will you sell this year, by month, by product? Base this on last year's sales plus a growth target.
  2. Monthly — how many jars of each flavour do you need this month? Back-calculate fruit, sugar, jars, labels.
  3. Weekly — what specific batches will you make this week? Which days?

A worked example

Target: 600 jars a month, in three flavours (strawberry, mango, mixed berry), 200 jars each.

The production day

A well-organised commercial cook day looks like this:

  1. Morning: prep. Wash, peel, cut fruit. Set up sterilisation. Get jars, lids, labels ready.
  2. Mid-morning: first batch. Cook batch 1 to set. Fill, seal, water bath.
  3. Afternoon: second batch. While batch 1 cools, prepare and cook batch 2.
  4. Late afternoon: filling and sealing. Fill all jars from both batches. Water bath. Cool.
  5. Evening: cleanup and records. Wash equipment. Write batch records. Update inventory. Prepare orders for delivery next day.

Two batches a day is a good rhythm. Trying to do three batches a day without assistance leads to mistakes and fatigue.

Batch discipline is what makes consistency possible

Commercial jam makers do not improvise. They use the same recipe, same equipment, same timing, same temperature for every batch. The record from batch 1 tells them what batch 2 should look like. Consistency is what keeps wholesale customers — hotels, shops, restaurants — buying from you.

Lesson 9.5

Hiring your first staff member

You will know it is time to hire when you are regularly working past midnight and turning away business, and the additional revenue from doing more would comfortably pay another person.

What to hire first

Your first hire should be someone who takes the repetitive work off your plate so you can focus on the higher-value work — sales, relationships, product development, planning.

RoleWhen to hireCost estimate
Production assistantWhen you are producing more than you can cook aloneMinimum wage + benefits
Delivery driverWhen deliveries eat more than 3 hours a dayPart-time wages + fuel
Sales repWhen you cannot visit buyers yourselfBase + commission
Bookkeeper (part-time)When you spend more than 4 hours a week on recordsFixed monthly fee

Most small jam businesses hire a production assistant first.

What to look for

  • Reliability — someone who shows up on time, every day, matters more than speed
  • Attention to detail — jam has no room for shortcuts in sterilisation or timing
  • Food safety awareness — even basic awareness is a huge advantage
  • Willingness to learn — you are teaching them your process
  • Physical stamina — jam making is standing work, lifting fruit, moving jars

What to pay

Pay fairly. The savings from underpaying staff are trivial compared to the cost of high turnover. A trained assistant who stays for two years is worth far more than three who rotate through every six months.

Pay at least the local minimum wage, ideally above it. Add anything that makes the job attractive:

  • Lunch provided
  • Transport allowance
  • End-of-year bonus based on production
  • Profit share on sales beyond a threshold

Training is not optional

When you hire your first staff member, they need to learn your process. This means:

  • Written recipes and procedures they can follow without you
  • Side-by-side training for at least 2 weeks before they work alone
  • Clear quality standards — what "correct" looks like, what "wrong" looks like
  • Permission to stop and ask when something is uncertain

An untrained assistant making jam to their own understanding is a food safety risk and a brand risk. Write the process down. Train properly. Check their work regularly for the first month.

Lesson 9.6

Costing a scaling step

Before committing to any scaling move — a new pot, a rented kitchen, a hire — model the numbers. This is what separates growth that works from growth that kills the business.

The scaling calculation

  1. Current state: how many jars per month, and at what margin?
  2. Target state: how many jars per month will you sell, at what price?
  3. Additional revenue: (new volume × retail price) − (current volume × retail price)
  4. Additional cost: new equipment, rent, staff, ingredients, packaging for the extra volume
  5. Net gain per month: additional revenue − additional cost
  6. Payback period: upfront investment ÷ net gain per month

When scaling does NOT pay

Watch for these scenarios

  • Your margin is under $2/jar. The additional volume required to pay for a scaling step is larger than the local market can absorb.
  • You cannot sell the extra volume. If demand is not there, producing more just means more unsold jars.
  • Your payment terms are long. If wholesale buyers pay in 60 days and you need the cash for the next batch, scaling eats your reserves.
  • You have no reserve. If scaling requires every dollar you have, one slow month kills the business.

Practical assignment — Module 9

Plan your next scaling step.

  1. Where are you now? State your current monthly production, your true cost per jar, your retail price, and your monthly gross profit.
  2. What is your bottleneck? Is it equipment, time, space, or demand? Be specific. "Not enough hours" is not specific. "Only one 5L pot, so I can only make 50 jars a day" is specific.
  3. What scaling step solves the bottleneck? Name the specific investment: a bigger pot, a hired assistant, a rented kitchen, a chest freezer.
  4. Model the numbers. Use the tables in Lesson 9.6. What is the upfront cost? What is the additional monthly revenue? What is the additional monthly cost? What is the payback period?
  5. Check your market. Can you actually sell the extra volume? Name two specific buyers who would take the additional jars, or state clearly that you need to find them first.
  6. Check your reserve. If you need 3 months of operating costs as reserve, how much is that, and do you have it?
  7. Decide. Is this scaling step worth doing now, or should it wait?

Points 4 and 5 carry the most marks. Point 4 tests whether you can model a business decision. Point 5 tests whether you are honest about your market.

Module 9 knowledge check

1. Which is the most important equipment upgrade when scaling from home production?

  1. A labelling machine
  2. A larger, heavier pot with a thick base
  3. A second freezer
  4. A commercial food processor
Show answer

b. The pot is the bottleneck. A larger, heavier pot enables bigger batches and gives better heat distribution. Other upgrades are useful but the pot comes first.

2. Why is the cash-flow trap the most common scaling failure?

  1. Because costs go up
  2. Because you pay for materials, equipment, and staff upfront, but wholesale buyers pay in 30–60 days — the money is locked in inventory and receivables while your costs are already paid
  3. Because staff are expensive
  4. Because scaling always loses money
Show answer

b. Scaling eats cash before it produces cash. You buy materials, hire staff, and pay for space upfront, then wait to be paid. Without a reserve to cover this gap, the business fails from lack of cash even with strong sales.

3. What should your first hire be in a small jam business?

  1. A marketing consultant
  2. A production assistant to take repetitive work off your plate
  3. A bookkeeper
  4. A delivery driver
Show answer

b. Your first hire should take the repetitive production work off your plate so you can focus on higher-value work — sales, buyer relationships, planning, product development. Hire delivery drivers, bookkeepers, or sales reps later.

4. Before scaling up, what is the most important thing to check?

  1. Whether you have a nice logo
  2. Whether the market can absorb the additional volume, and whether you have the reserve to fund the scaling step
  3. Whether you can afford new equipment
  4. Whether your competitors are scaling too
Show answer

b. Scaling only works if you can actually sell the extra volume and can fund the cash-flow gap. Both must be true. Equipment, logos, and competitor activity are secondary.

5. Why must training be written down, not just shown once?

  1. It is required by law
  2. Written procedures let your staff make consistent product without you, reduce food safety risk, and give new hires a reference they can return to
  3. It is faster
  4. You have to for insurance
Show answer

b. Written procedures are the only way to maintain consistency as you scale. Staff change, memory is unreliable, and food safety depends on the process being followed exactly. Write your recipes and methods down before you hire.
