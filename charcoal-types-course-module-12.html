1. Fixing and Protecting Charcoal Drawings

Charcoal smudges easily, so fixing your drawing is the first step to protect your work. You need a workable fixative spray, such as Krylon Workable Fixatif or a local equivalent like Bostik fixative. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Hold the can 30 cm from the drawing and spray in light, even passesleft to right, then top to bottom. Apply 2-3 thin coats, waiting 10 minutes between coats. Do not overspray, or the charcoal will darken and lose its texture. For a cheaper alternative, you can use hairspray, but test it first on a scrap piece because some hairsprays yellow over time.

After fixing, let the drawing dry for at least 30 minutes. To further protect it, place a sheet of glassine paper or acid-free tissue over the surface before storing or framing. Glassine is available at art supply shops in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. If you cannot find glassine, use a clean sheet of newsprint, but avoid newspaper because the ink can transfer.

For drawings that will be handled often, consider mounting them on a rigid board. Use acid-free double-sided tape or wheat starch paste to attach the drawing to a piece of acid-free mat board. This prevents creases and tears. If you plan to sell your work, always fix and mount it before delivery. A well-protected drawing shows professionalism and increases its value.

Store fixed drawings flat in a portfolio or between two pieces of cardboard. Keep them away from moisture and direct sunlight. In humid regions like coastal West Africa, add silica gel packets to your storage box to prevent mold. Check your drawings every few months for signs of smudging or foxing (brown spots). If you see smudges, you can lightly erase them with a kneaded eraser before re-fixing. Remember, fixing is not permanent; it can be reactivated by moisture, so handle with care.

2. Matting and Framing Charcoal Art

Matting and framing protect your charcoal drawing and make it ready to hang or sell. You will need a mat board (acid-free, 4-ply), a backing board, a frame, and glass or acrylic glazing. For a standard 30x40 cm drawing, cut your mat board to 40x50 cm. The opening (window) should be 2 cm smaller than your drawing on each side, so the mat overlaps the edges. Use a utility knife and a metal ruler to cut the mat. Practice on scrap first.

Place your drawing face up on the backing board. Center it, then place the mat over it. Use acid-free linen tape to hinge the drawing to the backing board at the top edge only. This allows the paper to expand and contract with humidity. Do not tape all sides, or the paper will buckle. For a professional look, add a second mat (a double mat) with a contrasting color.

Choose glazing carefully. Glass is scratch-resistant but heavy and breakable. Acrylic (Plexiglas) is lighter and safer for shipping, but it scratches easily and can build static that attracts charcoal dust. If using glass, make sure it does not touch the drawinguse a mat or spacers to create a gap. For charcoal, always use glazing; without it, the drawing will smudge. In dusty environments, seal the back of the frame with kraft paper or framing tape to keep out insects and dust.

Frames can be bought ready-made or custom-made by local carpenters. In many African cities, you can find affordable wooden frames at markets like Balogun in Lagos or Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam. Paint or stain the frame to match your artwork. For a rustic look, use reclaimed wood. Attach hanging hardwareD-rings and wireto the back of the frame. Use two D-rings, one on each side, about one-third from the top. String the wire between them, leaving enough slack to hang on a hook. Finally, clean the glazing with a soft cloth and glass cleaner before final assembly. Your framed charcoal art is now ready to display.

3. Photographing Charcoal Art

Good photographs help you sell your charcoal art online or submit to exhibitions. You do not need an expensive camera; a smartphone with at least 12 megapixels works. Use natural daylightshoot outdoors on a cloudy day or in a room with a large window, but avoid direct sunlight because it creates harsh shadows. Place your drawing on a neutral background, like a white or grey wall or a large sheet of paper. If the drawing is framed behind glass, remove it from the frame to avoid reflections. If you must shoot through glass, tilt the frame slightly and position yourself so reflections fall away.

Set your camera to the highest resolution and use a grid or level to keep the drawing straight. Fill the frame with the artwork, leaving a small border. Use a tripod or stack of books to steady your phone. For sharp focus, tap the screen on the drawing. If your phone has a manual mode, set ISO to 100 and shutter speed to 1/125 second. Use a remote shutter or the timer to avoid shake. Take multiple shotsat least 5and bracket exposures (one normal, one brighter, one darker).

After shooting, edit the photos on your phone using free apps like Snapseed or Lightroom Mobile. Adjust brightness, contrast, and white balance to match the original. Crop to straighten the edges. Do not over-edit; charcoal has subtle tones. Save as high-quality JPEG or PNG. For online sales, resize to 1500 pixels on the longest side and 72 dpi for web, but keep a high-resolution version (300 dpi) for printing. Name your files clearly, e.g., 'charcoal_baobab_2024.jpg'.

If you plan to print reproductions, consider scanning your drawing with a flatbed scanner at 300-600 dpi. Scanners capture fine details without glare. Many print shops in African cities offer scanning services for a small fee. For video, record a slow pan across the drawing to show texture. Use a tripod and good lighting. Post your photos on social media with relevant hashtags like #AfricanArt #CharcoalDrawing. Good photography can increase your sales by 50% or more.

4. Selling and Exhibiting Charcoal Art

To sell your charcoal art, you need to find the right audience. Start locally: cafes, hotels, and art galleries in your city often display local artists. In Nairobi, try the GoDown Arts Centre; in Accra, the Nubuke Foundation; in Cape Town, the Zeitz MOCAA. Approach them with a portfolio of 5-10 framed pieces. Offer to hang your work on consignmentthey take 30-50% commission. Set a fair price: calculate your materials (charcoal, paper, frame) plus 3-5 times your hourly rate. For a 30x40 cm framed drawing, a typical price in Africa ranges from $50 to $300, depending on your reputation.

Online platforms expand your reach. Use Instagram, Facebook Marketplace, or Etsy. Post high-quality photos and write a short story about each piece. Include dimensions, materials, and price. For shipping, use rigid tubes or flat boxes with bubble wrap. Charge shipping separately. In Africa, courier services like DHL or local buses can deliver. Always insure valuable pieces.

Exhibitions are powerful. Apply to group shows at community centers, art fairs, and biennials. Prepare a artist statement (100 words) and a price list. At the exhibition, bring business cards and a guest book. Talk to visitors about your process. Offer prints or postcards as affordable alternatives. If you cannot afford a solo show, organize a pop-up in a friend's shop or at a market. In Lagos, the Lekki Arts & Crafts Market is a good starting point.

When selling, provide a certificate of authenticity. Include your name, title, date, medium, and dimensions. Package the artwork professionally. For local sales, hand-deliver if possible. For online sales, communicate clearly and ship promptly. Ask buyers for feedback and testimonials. Repeat customers and referrals are key. Finally, keep records of all sales for tax and inventory. With consistent effort, you can build a sustainable income from your charcoal art.

5. Archival Storage

Archival storage keeps your charcoal drawings safe for decades. The enemies are light, heat, moisture, insects, and acid. Store your drawings in a cool, dark place with stable temperature (18-22C) and humidity (45-55%). In tropical Africa, use a dehumidifier or silica gel packets in storage boxes. Avoid attics, basements, and garages. A closet in a living space is often better.

Use acid-free materials. Buy archival boxes, folders, or portfolios made of polypropylene or polyethylene. Do not use PVC plastic, which releases harmful gases. Interleave drawings with acid-free glassine or tissue paper. If you cannot find glassine, use unprinted newsprint, but change it every 6 months. Stack drawings flat, not rolled, to prevent curling. For large drawings, use a flat file cabinet with shallow drawers. Label each folder with the title, date, and medium.

Handle drawings with clean, dry hands or cotton gloves. Oils from your skin can damage charcoal. Support the drawing from underneath when moving it. Never touch the surface. If a drawing gets dusty, gently brush it with a soft, clean paintbrush. Do not use water or solvents. For framed pieces, keep them hanging away from direct sun and heat sources. Rotate your display every 3-6 months to limit light exposure.

Check your stored art every 3 months. Look for signs of mold, foxing, or insect damage. If you see mold, isolate the drawing and consult a conservator. For minor insect damage, freeze the drawing in a sealed plastic bag for 72 hours (if the drawing is not framed). This kills larvae. After freezing, let it return to room temperature before opening. Keep a inventory list with photos and locations. Digitize your collection by scanning or photographing each piece. Store digital copies on a cloud drive or external hard drive. This ensures your legacy even if physical pieces are lost.

6. Final Project: Complete Charcoal Artwork

For your final project, you will create a finished charcoal artwork from start to finish, applying all the skills from this course. Choose a subject that reflects your African heritagea market scene, a wildlife portrait, a traditional mask, or a landscape. Work on A3 (30x42 cm) or larger paper. Use a mix of charcoal types: willow for sketching, compressed for dark tones, and white charcoal for highlights. Plan your composition with a light pencil sketch, then build up layers of charcoal. Remember to use fixative between layers to prevent smudging.

Once the drawing is complete, fix it with 2-3 coats of workable fixative. Let it dry completely. Then mat and frame it using acid-free materials. Cut a mat with a 2 cm border around the drawing. Use a simple wooden frame that complements the artwork. Attach hanging hardware. Photograph the finished piece under natural light, using a tripod and editing for accuracy. Write a short artist statement (100 words) describing your inspiration and process.

Present your artwork to the class or online. Prepare a 5-minute talk: explain your subject, the charcoal types you used, and the challenges you faced. Show your before-and-after photos. Offer the piece for sale or display it in a local venue. Price it based on materials plus your time. For example, if you spent 10 hours at $10/hour and materials cost $20, price at $120. Include a certificate of authenticity.

Finally, store a digital copy and a physical portfolio of your project. Reflect on what you learned: fixing, framing, photographing, selling, and storing. This project is your portfolio piece to show potential clients or galleries. Take pride in your workcharcoal art from Africa is unique and valuable. With these skills, you can turn your passion into a sustainable craft. Submit your project with photos, artist statement, and a short report on your process. Congratulations on completing the course!

<h2>Practical Reference: Finishing and Presenting Charcoal Art</h2>
<p>In the African charcoal art market from the busy craft stalls of Nairobi's Maasai Market to gallery walls in Lagos, Accra, and Cape Town the difference between a drawing that sells for KES 3,000 and one that commands KES 30,000 often comes down to finishing. Collectors and corporate buyers pay for artwork that looks professional, travels safely, and survives the humidity, dust, and heat of the continent. This practical reference covers the complete finishing chain: fixing and protecting charcoal drawings, matting and framing, photographing work for online sales, exhibiting and selling, archival storage, and a final project to consolidate your skills.</p>

<h3>1. Fixing and Protecting Charcoal Drawings</h3>
<p>Charcoal is one of the most fragile drawing media. Without fixing, a finished drawing can smudge with a single brush of a hand. The standard approach is a workable fixative applied in thin, even coats from 2530 cm away, in a well-ventilated space, ideally outdoors or near an open window. Two to three light coats allowing each to dry fully lock the charcoal without flattening the depth that makes charcoal attractive to buyers. Avoid cheap hairspray: it yellows over time and is an instant red flag for serious collectors.</p>
<p>For African conditions, humidity is the enemy. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, and Durban, drawings should be fixed and then stored with silica gel packets in sealed portfolios. In dusty environments like northern Nigeria or the Sahel, always store drawings face-up inside a portfolio rather than loose on a table.</p>

<h3>2. Matting and Framing Charcoal Art</h3>
<p>Matting does two jobs: it keeps the glass off the drawing surface (charcoal will transfer to glass if it touches) and it adds a professional border that raises perceived value. Use acid-free mat board buffered or 100% cotton rag and attach the drawing to the backing with Japanese paper hinges and wheat starch paste, or with acid-free linen tape. Never use masking tape or superglue; both will stain and destroy the artwork within a few years.</p>
<p>For framing, UV-filtering glass or acrylic is worth the extra cost in sunny African markets. In Lagos or Nairobi, a framed charcoal drawing hung near a window without UV protection can fade visibly within 18 months. Metal or wooden frames with a sealed backing board also keep out dust and insects a real concern in many workshops.</p>

<h3>3. Photographing Charcoal Art</h3>
<p>Most African charcoal artists now sell through Instagram, WhatsApp Business, and platforms like Etsy or local marketplaces. A poor photograph kills a sale. The rules are simple: shoot in diffused daylight (not direct sun), never use flash, keep the camera parallel to the artwork, and use a tripod or a stack of books to steady the phone. Place the drawing on a neutral grey or white surface. Charcoal's deep blacks and soft greys are notoriously hard to capture underexposing slightly and lifting shadows in editing is better than blowing out highlights.</p>
<p>Include a scale reference (a ruler or hand) in at least one shot, and photograph the framed piece as well as the flat artwork. Buyers want to see how it will look on a wall.</p>

<h3>4. Selling and Exhibiting Charcoal Art</h3>
<p>Charcoal art sells well in three African contexts: tourist and craft markets (volume, lower price), corporate and hospitality commissions (hotels, lodges, offices higher price), and online galleries (international buyers). For corporate work, present a professional portfolio with framed samples, clear pricing, and a written care guide for the buyer. For markets, display framed work at eye level, with a small sign explaining the medium and the artist's story storytelling adds 2040% to perceived value.</p>
<p>Exhibitions at local art centres (e.g., the GoDown Arts Centre in Nairobi, Alliance Franaise in Accra or Lagos) build credibility. Always carry a guest book, collect emails, and follow up within 48 hours.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Aerosol fixatives contain solvents (acetone, toluene, xylene) that are harmful to lungs, eyes, and skin. Always spray outdoors or in a room with cross-ventilation. Wear a NIOSH-approved respirator (N95 minimum) and safety goggles. Never spray near open flames, cooking fires, or generators. Keep away from children. In shared workshops, spray in a dedicated area away from other artists. If you feel dizzy, leave the area immediately and seek fresh air.</p>

<h3>5. Archival Storage</h3>
<p>Unsold or commissioned work awaiting delivery must be stored archivally. Use acid-free tissue interleaving between drawings, flat files or rigid portfolios, and store in a cool, dry room away from direct sunlight. In humid regions, add silica gel and check monthly. Never roll charcoal drawings tightly the charcoal will crack and flake. If you must roll, roll with the drawing facing outward around a large (10 cm+) acid-free tube.</p>

<h3>6. Final Project: Complete Charcoal Artwork</h3>
<p>Your final project is to produce one finished charcoal artwork from start to finish, documented at every stage. The deliverable includes: (a) the original drawing, fixed and matted; (b) a framed presentation piece; (c) three high-quality photographs (flat, framed, and in-situ); (d) a one-page artist statement and care guide; and (e) a pricing sheet with three tiers (print, original, commissioned). Present it as if to a corporate buyer in Nairobi or a gallery in Cape Town. This mirrors real market expectations and gives you a portfolio-ready package.</p>
