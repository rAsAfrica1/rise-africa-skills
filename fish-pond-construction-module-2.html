Lessons

1. Conducting a Topographic Survey

A topographic survey shows the shape and slope of your land. You need it to design a pond that fills and drains by gravity, avoiding pumps. Start by walking the site at the driest time of year. Use a dumpy level, tripod, and levelling staff. If you don't have these, a water-filled hose and two clear plastic pipes work as a water level. For a 20m x 30m pond, take readings every 5 metres along grid lines. Record elevations in a notebook.

Set up your level where you can see the whole area. Take a backsight on a known point (like a concrete benchmark or a large rock). Then take foresights at each grid point. Subtract foresight from backsight to get the height difference. If the difference is positive, the ground is lower; negative, higher. Write these numbers down. For example, if backsight is 1.5m and foresight is 2.0m, the ground is 0.5m lower than your level.

After surveying, draw a contour map. Contours are lines connecting points of equal elevation. Use a 0.25m contour interval for flat land, 0.5m for hilly. This map shows where water will flow. You want a slope of 1-2% for a pond bottom, so water moves to the drain. If the slope is more than 5%, you'll need to cut and fill more soil, which costs money.

In Africa, many sites are uneven. If you find a natural depression, use it. Otherwise, plan to move soil from the high side to the low side. Calculate the volume: area x average depth difference. For a 600m pond with 0.5m average difference, you need to move 300m of soil. That's about 30 trips with a 10m tipper truck. Always mark your survey points with wooden pegs and flagging tape so you don't lose them.

Finally, check for underground rocks or old tree stumps. A simple probe (a metal rod) pushed into the ground can find them. If you hit rock, you may need to blast or relocate. A good survey saves you from building a pond that won't drain or hold water.

2. Soil Testing for Pond Suitability

The best soil for a fish pond has at least 20% clay. Clay holds water; sand and gravel leak. To test, dig a hole 50cm deep. Take a handful of soil from the bottom. Squeeze it in your fist. If it forms a ribbon that hangs together for 5cm or more, it's good clay. If it crumbles, it's too sandy. Do this test in at least five spots across the site.

Next, do a jar test. Fill a clear 1-litre jar one-third with soil, add water to the top, and shake hard for 3 minutes. Let it settle for 24 hours. Sand settles first, then silt, then clay. Measure each layer with a ruler. If clay is less than 20% of the total, you need to import clay or use a plastic liner. For a 20m x 30m pond, you'd need about 40 tonnes of clay to mix into the top 30cm.

Check for soil pH. Use a cheap pH test kit from an agro-vet shop. Ideal pH is 6.5 to 8.5. If below 6.0, add agricultural lime at 2 tonnes per hectare. If above 9.0, add gypsum. Also test for salinity: if you're near the coast, use an EC meter. Fish like tilapia tolerate up to 2,000 S/cm, but catfish prefer below 1,000 S/cm.

In many parts of Africa, termites and earthworms indicate good soil. If you see many termite mounds, the soil is often clay-rich. Avoid black cotton soil that cracks when dryit leaks when wet. Also avoid soils with high organic matter (like peat) because they decompose and cause oxygen problems.

If your soil fails, don't give up. You can compact it with a sheepsfoot roller, add bentonite clay (10kg per m), or line the pond with HDPE geomembrane (0.5mm thick). The liner costs about $3 per m, so for a 600m pond, that's $1,800. Testing first saves you from spending that money unnecessarily.

3. Water Source Evaluation and Testing

You need a reliable water source that provides at least 5% of your pond volume daily to replace evaporation and seepage. For a 600m pond, that's 30,000 litres per day. Measure your source: stream flow, borehole yield, or rainwater. For a stream, use a bucket and stopwatch. If a 20-litre bucket fills in 10 seconds, flow is 2 litres per second, or 172,800 litres per daymore than enough.

Test water quality before you commit. Use a test kit for dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, temperature, and ammonia. DO should be above 5 mg/L. If below 3 mg/L, fish will gasp at the surface. pH should be 6.5-8.5. Ammonia should be below 0.5 mg/L. In Africa, many boreholes have high iron or sulphur. If water smells like rotten eggs, it has hydrogen sulphidetoxic to fish. Aerate it by splashing into a storage tank before use.

Check for pesticides and herbicides if you're near farmland. These kill fish. A simple bioassay: put 10 small tilapia in a bucket of source water. If more than 2 die in 24 hours, the water is unsafe. Also test for heavy metals if near mining areasuse a lab test, which costs about $50.

Rainwater is the cheapest source. A roof of 100m in a region with 800mm annual rainfall collects 80,000 litres per year. That's 219 litres per dayenough for a small pond. But you need storage. A 10,000-litre tank costs $500. For groundwater, drill a borehole (cost $3,000-$8,000) and test yield with a pump test. You want at least 1 litre per second.

Always have a backup source. If your stream dries up, you need a borehole or stored water. In dry season, many African ponds fail because the source disappears. Plan for the worst month, not the best.

4. Assessing Drainage and Flood Risks

A pond must drain completely for harvest and maintenance. You need a low point with a drain pipe. If your site is flat, create a 1% slope toward the drain. For a 30m long pond, that's a 30cm drop from inlet to outlet. Use a water level to check. If the land slopes the wrong way, you'll need to cut a channel or install a pumpexpensive.

Flood risk is critical. In Africa, many ponds are lost to seasonal floods. Check the high-water mark: look for debris lines on trees, silt deposits, or ask elders. If your site floods more than once every 5 years, don't build there. Alternatively, build a dyke (embankment) at least 1m above the highest flood level. For a 600m pond, a 1m high dyke with 2:1 side slopes needs about 200m of soil.

Assess drainage away from the pond. You don't want overflow to erode your dyke or flood downstream neighbours. Dig a diversion channel 1m deep and 1m wide around the uphill side. This channel should slope at 0.5% to carry water away. If the channel is 50m long, it drops 25cm. Use a line level and string to check.

In heavy rain, your pond may overflow. Install a spillwaya concrete or grassed channel that carries excess water safely. Size it for a 1-in-10-year storm. For a 1-hectare catchment, that's about 0.5m per second. A spillway 2m wide and 0.3m deep can handle that. Never let water flow over the dyke; it will breach.

Also check groundwater. If the water table is within 0.5m of the surface in dry season, you may not need a liner. But if it's high in wet season, the pond may float if emptied. In that case, keep the pond full or install a pressure relief valve. Simple test: dig a hole 1m deep. If it fills with water within 24 hours, the water table is high.

5. Marking Pond Boundaries and Levels

Once you've chosen the pond location, mark it clearly. Use wooden pegs (30cm long) and bright flagging tape. For a rectangular pond, drive pegs at all four corners. For irregular shapes, use a rope to outline the edge. Check square corners with the 3-4-5 method: measure 3m along one side, 4m along the other, and the diagonal should be 5m. Adjust until it is.

Mark the pond bottom level. The bottom should be flat or slope 1% toward the drain. Use a dumpy level or water level to set grade stakes. Drive a stake at the outlet (lowest point) and mark the desired bottom elevation. Then drive stakes every 5m along the pond length. Use a string line between stakes to show the bottom grade. For a 30m pond with 1% slope, the inlet end is 30cm higher than the outlet.

Mark the top of the dyke. The dyke should be 0.5m above the water level. For a 1.5m deep pond, the dyke top is 2.0m above the bottom. Use a level to mark this on corner pegs. Also mark the inside and outside toes of the dyke. The inside toe is where the dyke meets the pond bottom; the outside toe is where it meets the original ground. For a 1m high dyke with 2:1 slopes, the base is 1m + 2*(1m) = 3m wide on each side? Actually, for a 1m high dyke, top width 1m, side slopes 2:1, the base width = 1m + 2*2*1m = 5m. Mark these with pegs.

Use a permanent marker: concrete monuments or steel pipes at corners. Write the elevation on them. In Africa, cattle and people may knock over wooden pegs. So after marking, take GPS coordinates of each corner. Record them in a notebook. If pegs are lost, you can re-establish them with GPS.

Finally, mark the inlet and outlet. The inlet should be at the highest point, with a pipe or channel. The outlet (drain) at the lowest point, with a monk or standpipe. Mark these with different coloured tape. Check that water will flow by gravity from inlet to outlet. If not, adjust your levels before digging.

6. Using GPS and Drones for Site Mapping

A handheld GPS (like Garmin eTrex) costs about $100 and is accurate to 3-5 metres. Walk the pond boundary and mark waypoints at each corner. Record the coordinates (latitude, longitude, elevation). Download them to a computer using free software like QGIS. This gives you a map with area and perimeter. For a 20m x 30m pond, you should get 600m. If your GPS says 550m, check your walking line.

For better accuracy, use a GPS with external antenna or a smartphone app like GPS Fields Area Measure. These can be accurate to 1-2 metres. Mark the four corners, then use the app to calculate area. Also mark the water source, inlet, outlet, and any obstacles (trees, rocks). Take photos with geotags.

Drones are becoming affordable. A basic drone like the DJI Mini 2 costs $400. Fly at 50m height with 70% overlap between photos. Use free software like OpenDroneMap to stitch images into an orthomosaic map. This map shows every detail: slopes, vegetation, wet spots. You can measure distances and elevations. For a 1-hectare site, a drone flight takes 10 minutes and processes in 1 hour.

In Africa, drone rules vary. In Kenya, you need a permit from the Kenya Civil Aviation Authority. In Nigeria, register with NCAA. Always fly in open areas, away from airports and people. If you can't afford a drone, hire a surveyor with one. A drone survey for a small pond costs $200-$500, but it saves days of manual surveying.

Use GPS and drone data to plan your pond. Import the map into QGIS, draw the pond shape, and calculate cut and fill volumes. For example, if the drone map shows a 0.5m elevation difference across the site, you know you need to move 300m of soil. Also use the map to plan drainage channels and access roads. Share the map with your digger operatorthey can see exactly where to dig. This reduces errors and saves fuel.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Site Assessment and Surveying for Fish Pond Construction

In African fish farming, the difference between a pond that yields 4 tonnes of tilapia per cycle and one that drains your capital often comes down to work done before the first scoop of soil is moved. Site assessment and surveying is not a technical formality it is your cheapest risk-management tool. A poorly sited pond fails through seepage, flooding, drought, or poor access to markets. This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into field-ready procedures, decision tables, and checklists you can use with a farm manager, an extension officer, or a hired surveyor.

Throughout, the assumption is commercial: you are building ponds to sell fish, not to demonstrate technology. Every measurement should answer a business question Will this site hold water? Can I fill and drain it cheaply? Can I reach buyers year-round? Will a flood wipe out a season's investment?

1. Conducting a Topographic Survey

A topographic survey establishes elevation differences across the site. For pond construction you need slope, not just area. The ideal range for a gravity-fed, drainable pond system in most African contexts is a gentle slope of 13% (a 13 m fall per 100 m). Flatter sites require embankment construction on all sides; steeper sites raise earthworks costs and erosion risk.

Field method (low-cost): Use a dumpy level or a water level (hose level) with a staff. Establish a benchmark (a permanent peg at a known elevation, ideally tied to a GPS point), then take spot heights on a 10 m 10 m grid for small ponds (under 0.5 ha) or 20 m 20 m for larger sites. Record readings in a field book and reduce them to a contour map.

Business decisions from the survey:

  • Cut-and-fill balance: aim to balance excavation and embankment to avoid importing or exporting soil.
  • Pond layout: align ponds along the contour so each can be filled and drained independently.
  • Access roads and pump pads: identify the flattest route for vehicle access during harvest.

2. Soil Testing for Pond Suitability

The critical soil property is the ability to hold water. A soil with 2035% clay content, when well compacted, generally forms a watertight pond bottom. Sandy soils leak; heavy shrink-swell clays crack in the dry season. Test before you excavate.

Field tests (no laboratory required):

  • Jar/ribbon test: Roll moist soil into a ribbon. A ribbon 35 cm long before breaking indicates adequate clay.
  • Jar sedimentation test: Fill a clear jar one-third with soil, add water and a pinch of salt, shake, and let settle 24 hours. Measure sand, silt, and clay layers as percentages.
  • Percolation test: Dig a 30 cm hole, fill with water, refill, and measure the drop over 24 hours. A drop of less than 2 cm/day is suitable; more than 5 cm/day indicates serious seepage risk.

3. Water Source Evaluation and Testing

Water must be available in sufficient quantity year-round and of acceptable quality. For a 1,000 m pond averaging 1 m depth, you need roughly 1,000 m to fill, plus 25% daily replacement to offset seepage and evaporation approximately 2050 m/day. In the dry season, this is where most African farms fail.

Quantity: Measure flow rate of streams or springs (time to fill a 20 L bucket). For boreholes, confirm yield in litres per hour and pump duty cycle. Never assume a source will sustain peak dry-season demand.

Quality: Test for pH, dissolved oxygen, temperature, turbidity, salinity, and pesticide/agricultural runoff contamination. In areas with cotton, horticulture, or mining upstream, test for agrochemicals and heavy metals before committing capital.

4. Assessing Drainage and Flood Risks

Two failure modes dominate African pond losses: inability to drain (making harvest inefficient and disease-prone) and flooding (washing out stock and eroding embankments).

Drainage assessment: Confirm that the pond bottom sits above the outlet ditch, and that the ditch leads to a safe discharge point that does not flood back. A fall of at least 0.5 m from pond bottom to the ditch is desirable.

Flood risk: Identify the 10-year and 50-year flood levels. In many African catchments, historical flood marks, local knowledge, and national meteorological or water authority records are the best available data. Avoid siting ponds on floodplains below the 50-year level unless protected by a diversion bund and elevated embankments.

SAFETY WARNING Excavation and Earthworks

Pond excavation involves deep trenches, heavy machinery, and unstable soil. Before any digging: (1) Never enter an unsupported trench deeper than 1.2 m collapse can be fatal. (2) Locate all buried services (water mains, cables, pipelines) through the relevant utility authority before digging. (3) Keep all personnel at least 5 m from excavator swing radius. (4) Do not work alone near open water or deep pits; ensure a colleague can raise an alarm. (5) In malaria-endemic areas, drain or treat standing water in borrow pits within 48 hours to prevent mosquito breeding. (6) Provide and enforce the use of life jackets when working from boats or on floating pumps.

5. Marking Pond Boundaries and Levels

Once the design is fixed, translate it to the ground with pegs, string lines, and level marks. Errors here are expensive: a pond that is 20 cm too shallow at one end cannot be drained properly.

  • Corner pegs: Use durable stakes (hardwood or steel) at each corner, tied with string to mark the top of embankment and the pond bottom level.
  • Level marks: Paint or notch the staff/pole at the design water level, freeboard level (typically 0.30.5 m above water), and bottom level. Transfer these marks to all corners.
  • Reference benchmark: Keep one permanent benchmark outside the construction area so future repairs and expansions can be tied back to the original levels.
  • Documentation: Photograph pegs and marks before excavation; contractors and equipment operators change, and records prevent disputes.

6. Using GPS and Drones for Site Mapping

Modern tools reduce survey time and improve accuracy, but they are only as good as the ground-truthing behind them.

  • Handheld GPS (sub-5 m accuracy): Ideal for marking boundaries, water source points, and access routes. Record waypoints and export to Google Earth or QGIS for a farm map.
  • RTK-GNSS (centimetre accuracy): Required for cut-and-fill volume calculations and precise level transfer. Many African survey firms now offer RTK drone services at accessible rates.
  • Drones: Photogrammetry from a drone flight can produce a digital elevation model (DEM) and orthomosaic in hours, covering 1050 ha per flight. Use for site selection, progress monitoring, and flood-risk modelling.
  • Business case: A drone survey costing a few hundred USD can prevent a six-figure earthworks error. For farms above 2 ha, the payback is immediate.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Slope measured and confirmed between 1% and 3% across the pond footprint.
  2. Soil ribbon test and 24-hour percolation test completed; clay content 2035%.
  3. Dry-season water quantity measured in litres per day; at least 2050 m/day available for a 1,000 m pond.
  4. Water quality tested for pH, DO, temperature, turbidity, and upstream contamination.
  5. Flood level (10-year and 50-year) identified; ponds sited above it or protected by a diversion bund.
  6. Independent drain route confirmed with at least 0.5 m fall to a safe discharge point.
  7. Corner pegs, level marks, and a permanent benchmark established and photographed.
  8. GPS/drone map produced, with boundaries, water sources, and access roads recorded.
