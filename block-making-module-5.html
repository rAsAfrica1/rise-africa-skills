Foam Concrete Block (CLC)

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Medium difficulty

1 Foam concrete vs AAC

Foam concrete (also called CLC — Cellular Lightweight Concrete) is often confused with AAC, but they're different processes:

AAC: Uses aluminium powder + lime to create gas bubbles inside the mix. Requires autoclave (pressure steam) curing. Factory-scale only.

Foam concrete: Uses a foam generator to pre-make air bubbles, which are then mixed into a cement slurry. No autoclave needed — air curing is enough. Medium-scale possible.

Key point: foam concrete can be made in a small workshop. You don't need a $250K autoclave. You do need a foam generator ($1,500–$8,000) and a mixer.

Density: 400–1,600 kg/m³ depending on how much foam you add. Blocks are usually 600–1,000 kg/m³.

2 Foaming agents explained

The foam is made from a foaming agent — a surfactant that creates stable bubbles when whipped with water and air.

Types of foaming agents:

  • Protein-based (keratin): From animal protein. Strongest, most stable bubbles. Best for structural foam concrete. £3–£6 per litre.
  • Synthetic (SLS, AOS): Cheaper, but weaker bubbles. Suitable for non-structural insulation. £1.50–£3 per litre.
  • Composite: Blend of protein and synthetic. Mid-price, good performance. £2–£4 per litre.

Dose: 1 litre of foaming agent makes 40–80 litres of foam, depending on dilution ratio (typically 1:30 to 1:60 with water).

For block making, use protein-based. The stronger bubbles hold up during mixing and give more consistent density.

💡 Buy foaming agent from specialty suppliers. Dish soap will foam, but the bubbles collapse in minutes — no good for concrete.

3 Making foam on site

The foam generator is a simple but essential machine:

  1. Water and foaming agent are mixed in a tank (ratio per supplier — usually 1:30 to 1:60)
  2. A pump pushes the solution through a foam generator chamber
  3. Compressed air is injected into the chamber, creating the foam
  4. Foam exits as a dense, shaving-cream-like mass through a hose

Foam density target: 60–90 g per litre. Too light: foam collapses quickly. Too dense: no volume gain.

Test: fill a 1-litre container with foam and weigh. 70 g is ideal.

Small foam generators ($1,500–$3,500) handle 100–500 L/min of foam. Enough for a small block workshop making 200–800 blocks a day.

4 Foam-to-slurry ratios

The target block density determines the foam ratio:

Block Density (kg/m³)UseFoam Ratio
600Insulation only1:1.5 (very high foam)
800Non-load walls1:1
1,000Partition walls1:0.6
1,200Load-bearing (light)1:0.4
1,600Structural1:0.2

"1:1.5" means 1.5 parts foam per 1 part slurry by volume. Add foam slowly while the mixer runs. Stop when target density reached (test by filling and weighing a 1-litre cup).

Higher foam = lower strength. Standard wall block: 800–1,000 kg/m³.

5 Pouring into moulds

Foam concrete is a pourable liquid, not a stiff mix. It flows into moulds by gravity.

  1. Prepare moulds on a flat, level surface (foam concrete won't self-level across 5°+)
  2. Lightly oil moulds for release
  3. Pour the foamed mix slowly — don't splash, as splashing breaks the bubbles
  4. Fill to the top of the mould
  5. Do NOT vibrate — vibration collapses the foam
  6. Level the top with a straight edge (gentle)

Because foam concrete is self-compacting (the bubbles push the mix into every corner), you don't get honeycomb like dense blocks.

Fresh foam concrete rises a small amount as it sits. Fill moulds to 95% and leave the natural rise to complete.

⚠ Never vibrate foam concrete. It destroys the air structure — you'd end up with a lump of dense concrete at 1,600 kg/m³.

6 No autoclave needed

This is the biggest advantage of foam concrete over AAC. You air-cure it, like normal concrete.

Curing steps:

  1. Leave in the mould for 24–48 hours (foam concrete sets slower than dense concrete)
  2. Demould and stack on pallets
  3. Cover with plastic or damp cloth
  4. Keep damp for 7 days
  5. Full strength at 28 days

Strength after 28 days: 2–8 N/mm² depending on density. At 800 kg/m³, expect 2–3 N/mm². At 1,200 kg/m³, expect 6–8 N/mm².

For non-load-bearing and partition walls, this is enough. For load-bearing, you need 1,200+ kg/m³ foam concrete (weaker than dense concrete, but acceptable for small buildings).

7 Curing foam concrete

Foam concrete cures like normal concrete but is more sensitive to drying out.

Curing rules:

  • Keep covered for 7 days — plastic sheet or damp cloth
  • Don't dry out — foam concrete loses strength if it dries fast (more so than dense concrete)
  • Avoid direct sun — move to shade if possible
  • Protect from rain — too much water can wash out the foam structure

Blocks are ready to sell at 7 days (60–70% strength), full strength at 28 days. Same as dense blocks.

Handling: because foam concrete is fragile, handle gently during curing. Stack only 6–8 blocks high.

8 CLC for warm climates

Foam concrete is ideal for hot African climates:

  • Excellent insulation — λ value 0.10–0.30 W/m·K (similar to AAC)
  • Lightweight — 40–60% lighter than dense concrete, so easier to transport and lay
  • Pourable and self-compacting — no vibration equipment needed
  • Made in small workshops — no $250K autoclave
  • Can be made on-site — some contractors bring a foam generator to the build site and pour directly

Where to use CLC blocks:

  • Interior partition walls
  • Non-load-bearing external walls in hot climates
  • Second-storey infill on framed buildings
  • Roof insulation fills
  • Floor screeds (as a lightweight layer)

Where NOT to use:

  • Foundations below ground (moisture + low strength)
  • High-rise load-bearing (unless density >1,200 kg/m³ and engineer-approved)
  • Areas with high mechanical stress
💰 Foam concrete is the sweet spot for African small-scale producers: light, insulating, and manufacturable in a workshop. Equipment cost is $8,000–$25,000 to start.
