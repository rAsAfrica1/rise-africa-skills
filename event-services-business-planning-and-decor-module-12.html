Lessons

1. Separating personal and business money

As an event planner, you might be tempted to use the same wallet for business and personal needs. But mixing money is the fastest way to kill your business. When you pay for your child's school fees from the same account that holds a client's deposit, you won't know if your business is actually profitable. Worse, you might spend money that belongs to a vendor or for materials, leaving you in debt.

Start by opening a separate bank account for your business. In many African countries, you can open a simple current or savings account with a small deposit. For example, in Nigeria, GTBank or Access Bank allow you to open a business account with as low as 10,000. In Kenya, Equity Bank or KCB have similar options. If you can't open a bank account yet, use a mobile money wallet exclusively for business, like M-Pesa in Kenya or MTN MoMo in Ghana. But a bank account is better because it gives you a record.

Next, pay yourself a fixed salary. Decide on a monthly amount that covers your personal needs, and transfer it from your business account to your personal account. For instance, if your business earns 200,000 in a month, you might pay yourself 50,000 and leave the rest for business expenses and savings. This discipline forces you to live within your means and shows you if your business can afford you.

Also, keep every receipt. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Excel to record every business expense, even small ones like transport to a client meeting. At the end of the month, compare your business income and expenses to see if you're making a profit. If you're not, you need to adjust your prices or cut costs.

Finally, never use business money for personal emergencies. Instead, build a personal emergency fund separately. This separation will save you from confusion and help your business grow sustainably.

2. Saving for slow seasons and emergencies

Event planning is seasonal. In many African countries, the busiest times are December, January, and the wedding season (often from April to September). But from February to March or October to November, bookings can dry up. If you don't save during the busy months, you'll struggle during the slow ones.

Start by calculating your monthly living expenses. For example, if you need 80,000 per month for rent, food, and transport, you must save at least three months' worth, which is 240,000. This is your emergency fund. Aim to save 10-20% of every payment you receive. If a client pays you 150,000 for a wedding, immediately transfer 30,000 to a separate savings account. In Kenya, you can use M-Shwari or a fixed deposit account with your bank. In Nigeria, try PiggyVest or a target savings account.

Also, save for slow seasons. If you know that February is always slow, set aside money from December and January to cover your bills in February. For instance, if your business expenses are 50,000 per month, save an extra 100,000 by the end of January to cover February and March. This way, you won't panic or take loans with high interest.

Another strategy is to create a 'rainy day' jar or mobile money savings. Every time you get paid, put a small percentage aside. Even 5% adds up. For example, on a 200,000 event, save 10,000. After 10 events, you have 100,000. That's enough to cover a month of expenses.

Don't touch this savings for personal wants. It's for business survival. If you have a slow month, you can pay your rent and keep your business running. Over time, this habit will make your business stable and less stressful.

3. Reinvesting profits into decor inventory and marketing

To grow your event business, you must reinvest some of your profits. If you spend all your profit on personal needs, you'll stay small. Reinvesting means using money to buy better equipment, improve your marketing, and take on bigger jobs.

Start with decor inventory. If you currently rent chairs, tables, and drapes for every event, consider buying your own. For example, a set of 50 chairs might cost 100,000 in Nigeria. If you rent them for 500 each per event, you spend 25,000 per event. After 4 events, you've recovered your cost. Then, you can rent them out to other planners or use them for your own events, saving money and earning extra. Similarly, invest in quality backdrops, centerpieces, and lighting. Buy durable items that can be reused many times.

But don't buy everything at once. Start with the items you use most often. For instance, if you do many weddings, invest in elegant chair covers and table linens. If you do corporate events, invest in branded banners and stage decor. Allocate at least 30% of your profit to inventory. If you make 100,000 profit, put 30,000 into new items.

The other key area is marketing. You can't grow if no one knows about you. Use part of your profit to promote your business. For example, boost your Instagram and Facebook posts for as little as $5 per day to reach more potential clients. Print business cards and flyers to hand out at events. Create a simple website or a WhatsApp Business catalog. In Africa, word-of-mouth is powerful, so also invest in small thank-you gifts for clients who refer others.

Track your return on investment. If you spend 20,000 on ads and get two bookings worth 200,000, that's a good return. If not, try a different approach. Reinvesting wisely will help you build a sustainable business that grows year after year.

4. Tracking income and expenses monthly

If you don't track your money, you're running blind. Many event planners in Africa don't know if they're making a profit until they're broke. Tracking income and expenses monthly gives you control and helps you make smart decisions.

Start by recording every transaction. Use a simple notebook or a free spreadsheet like Google Sheets or Excel. For each event, write down the client's name, date, total income, and all expenses: transport, materials, labor, venue fees, etc. For example, if you plan a wedding and receive 500,000, but spend 300,000 on decor, 50,000 on transport, and 50,000 on assistants, your profit is 100,000. If you don't write this down, you might think you made 500,000 and spend it all.

At the end of each month, add up all income and all expenses. Subtract expenses from income to get your profit or loss. If you have a loss, find out why. Maybe you underpriced your services or overspent on materials. If you have a profit, decide how much to save, reinvest, and pay yourself.

Use mobile apps to make it easier. In Kenya, you can use M-Pesa statements to track business transactions. In Nigeria, apps like Kippa or Bumpa help small businesses record sales and expenses. They also send reminders and generate reports. If you have a smartphone, these tools are low-cost or free.

Also, separate your business and personal expenses. If you use the same account, you'll mix things up. Have a dedicated business account or wallet. At the end of the month, review your records. Are your expenses higher than last month? Why? Are your prices covering your costs? Adjust as needed.

Tracking monthly helps you spot trends. For example, you might notice that you spend more on transport during the rainy season. You can then plan ahead. It also makes tax filing easier and helps you apply for loans or grants. Make it a habit, and your business will be more stable.

5. Planning for taxes and licenses

Many event planners in Africa ignore taxes and licenses until they get a letter from the government. But paying taxes and getting the right licenses protects your business and helps you grow. It also makes you look professional to clients and banks.

First, register your business. In Nigeria, you can register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) for about 10,000. In Kenya, register with the Registrar of Companies under the Companies Act. In Ghana, register with the Registrar General's Department. Registration gives you a legal identity and allows you to open a business bank account. It also makes it easier to get contracts from corporate clients who require a registered business.

Next, understand your tax obligations. In many countries, small businesses with turnover below a certain threshold pay a simplified tax. For example, in Nigeria, if your turnover is less than 25 million, you may qualify for a 0% CIT for small companies. But you still need to file returns. In Kenya, there's a turnover tax for businesses with turnover below KSh 5 million. Check with your local tax authority (FIRS in Nigeria, KRA in Kenya, GRA in Ghana) to know your obligations.

Keep records of all income and expenses. You'll need them to file your taxes. If you don't, you might overpay or face penalties. Consider hiring a part-time accountant or use a tax app like Taxify (Nigeria) or iTax (Kenya) to file online. Many African countries now have online portals for tax filing.

Also, get any local licenses you need. For event planning, you might need a permit from the local government to operate, especially if you have a physical office or warehouse. If you serve alcohol at events, you need a liquor license. If you play music, you might need a copyright license from the relevant body (e.g., MCSK in Kenya).

Budget for taxes and licenses. Set aside 10-15% of your profit for taxes. For example, if you make 100,000 profit, save 15,000 for tax. This way, you won't be caught off guard. Paying taxes on time builds your creditworthiness and avoids fines. It's part of being a sustainable business.

6. Setting long-term financial goals for your event business

Without goals, your business will drift. Long-term financial goals give you direction and motivate you to keep going. As an event planner, you might want to buy a van, open a showroom, or employ a team. Whatever your dream, you need a plan.

Start by setting specific, measurable goals. For example, instead of saying 'I want to make more money,' say 'I want to earn 5 million in revenue by December 2025.' Then break it down. How many events do you need to do? If your average event earns 250,000, you need 20 events. That's about 2 events per month. Now you can plan your marketing and pricing to achieve that.

Next, set financial milestones. For instance, by the end of year one, save 500,000 in an emergency fund. By year two, buy a used van for 2 million to transport decor. By year three, hire a full-time assistant. Write these down and review them every month. Put them where you can see them, like on your phone's notes or a vision board.

To reach these goals, you need to manage your money well. That means budgeting, saving, and reinvesting as we've discussed. Also, increase your income by raising prices as you gain experience. For example, if you start charging 150,000 per event, aim to charge 300,000 within two years by improving your quality and portfolio. Don't be afraid to target higher-paying clients, like corporate events or destination weddings.

Another goal might be to diversify. You could start renting out decor to other planners, or add a new service like catering or photography. This creates multiple income streams.

Finally, track your progress. At the end of each quarter, compare your actual income and savings to your goals. If you're behind, adjust your plan. Celebrate small wins to stay motivated. Long-term goals turn your business from a hustle into a legacy. With discipline and focus, you can build an event business that supports you and your family for years.

<h2>Practical Reference: Managing Money and Building a Sustainable Event Business</h2>

<p>In the African event services industrywhether you're draping a wedding tent in Kumasi, renting out chiavari chairs in Nairobi, or supplying floral installations for corporate galas in Lagosthe difference between a business that survives five years and one that folds after two seasons almost always comes down to money management. Decor and event planning is a cash-intensive, seasonal, trust-driven trade. Clients pay deposits, suppliers demand upfront payment, and your peak months (typically NovemberJanuary and AprilJune) can generate 6070% of annual revenue. This module gives you the practical financial systems to separate personal and business money, survive slow seasons, reinvest intelligently, and build a business that outlasts you.</p>

<h3>1. Separating Personal and Business Money</h3>
<p>The single most common failure point for African event businesses is commingling funds. When your business revenue sits in the same mobile money wallet or bank account you use for school fees and rent, you cannot know whether you are profitable, and you will spend working capital on personal needs without realising it.</p>
<p><strong>Practical steps:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Open a dedicated business bank account (many African banks now offer zero-maintenance accounts for SMEse.g., GTBank SME accounts in Nigeria, Equity Bank Business accounts in Kenya, Absa Business in South Africa).</li>
<li>Register a separate mobile money merchant line (MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, M-Pesa Business) for client payments.</li>
<li>Pay yourself a fixed monthly salaryeven if modestrather than drawing randomly. This makes your personal budget predictable and your business accounts clean.</li>
<li>Use accounting software: <strong>Wave</strong> (free), <strong>Zoho Books</strong>, or <strong>QuickBooks Simple Start</strong>. For mobile-first operators, <strong>Kippa</strong> (Nigeria) and <strong>Hisa</strong> (Kenya) offer bookkeeping tailored to African SMEs.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Saving for Slow Seasons and Emergencies</h3>
<p>Event work is seasonal. In much of West Africa, the rains (JuneSeptember) slow outdoor events; in East Africa, certain months align with school terms and lower disposable spending. You must build a "lean season fund."</p>
<p><strong>The 3-Bucket System:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Operations bucket (50% of revenue):</strong> covers materials, transport, labour, venue deposits.</li>
<li><strong>Lean season reserve (20%):</strong> set aside in a separate savings account or money market fund. Target 36 months of operating expenses.</li>
<li><strong>Growth & tax bucket (30%):</strong> reinvestment, tax obligations, licenses, and profit.</li>
</ol>
<p>Instruments to consider: Treasury bills (Nigeria: FGN savings bonds; Kenya: CBK T-bills via DhowCSD; Ghana: 91-day T-bills), money market funds (Stanbic, Sanlam, Etica Capital), or fixed deposit accounts. Avoid locking all reserves in illiquid assetsyou need access within 48 hours for emergencies.</p>

<h3>3. Reinvesting Profits into Decor Inventory and Marketing</h3>
<p>Decor businesses scale through asset accumulationreusable inventory that earns repeatedly. A 200,000 investment in 100 chiavari chairs that rent at 500 each per event can generate 50,000 per event, paying back in 45 events.</p>
<p><strong>Reinvestment priorities (in order):</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>High-utilisation inventory</strong> (chairs, tables, linens, backdrops, lighting) target 3040% of profit.</li>
<li><strong>Marketing that generates leads</strong> Instagram/TikTok content, Google Business Profile, WhatsApp Business catalogue. Target 1015% of revenue.</li>
<li><strong>Skills and certifications</strong> floral design, rigging safety, project management.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment maintenance and storage</strong> a dry, secure storage unit prevents 2030% annual inventory loss to mould, theft, and damage.</li>
</ol>

<h3>6. Setting Long-Term Financial Goals</h3>
<p>Use the <strong>SMART</strong> framework and set goals across three horizons:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Short-term (012 months):</strong> Build a 3-month emergency fund; register business and TIN; acquire 50 chairs and 10 tables.</li>
<li><strong>Medium-term (13 years):</strong> Hire one full-time staff member; purchase a delivery van; reach 25% net profit margin; open a dedicated business account with a savings product.</li>
<li><strong>Long-term (310 years):</strong> Own a storage warehouse; expand to two cities; build a brand with recurring corporate clients; establish a retirement/pension plan (e.g., NSSF in Kenya, RSA in Nigeria, SSNIT in Ghana, or a private scheme).</li>
</ul>
