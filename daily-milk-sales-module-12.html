1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Selling milk every day is the backbone of a profitable dairy herd. If you milk 10 cows and each gives 10 litres, you have 100 litres daily. At 50 KES per litre, that's 5,000 KES per day or 150,000 KES per month. But that money only comes if you sell all the milk. Miss a day, and you lose 5,000 KES. Worse, milk spoils in hours without cooling. So you must have reliable routes to market.

In Africa, most milk is sold informally: neighbours, roadside kiosks, local hotels, or milk bars. Formal channels include processors like Brookside or dairy cooperatives. Each route has different prices, payment terms, and volumes. For example, a processor may pay 40 KES per litre but collect daily, while a neighbour pays 50 KES but only buys 2 litres. Your job is to mix routes to sell all your milk at the best average price.

Why does this matter? Because dairy farming has fixed costs: feed, labour, vet care. Every litre unsold is a direct loss. Also, daily cash flow lets you buy feed, pay workers, and reinvest. Without daily sales, you borrow money or sell cows. Many small dairies fail not because cows produce little, but because they cannot sell what they produce.

This module shows you how to find, secure, and manage multiple routes to market. You will learn tools like milk cans, coolers, and record books. You will follow a step-by-step method to plan your daily sales. You will avoid common mistakes like selling on credit or delivering late. And you will practise a real sales route. By the end, you will have a system to sell every drop, every day, at a fair price.

2. Tools and Materials

To sell milk daily, you need the right tools. First, milk cans: 20-litre or 50-litre stainless steel or aluminium cans with tight lids. Plastic jerrycans are cheaper but crack and taint milk. A 20-litre can costs about 3,000 KES and lasts years. You need at least two: one for morning milk, one for evening. Second, a cooler box or refrigerator. If you sell to processors, they require milk below 4C. A 100-litre chest freezer converted to a cooler costs 40,000 KES, but a simple insulated box with ice packs works for short trips.

Third, a lactometer and alcohol test kit. Processors test milk for water and antibiotics. A lactometer (1,500 KES) checks density; alcohol test (500 KES) checks acidity. Without these, you may be rejected. Fourth, a record book or smartphone app. Record daily milk volume, buyer, price, and payment status. A simple notebook (100 KES) is fine. Fifth, a bicycle, motorcycle, or donkey cart for delivery. A used motorcycle costs 80,000 KES, but if you deliver 100 litres daily, it pays back in months.

Sixth, clean buckets, a strainer, and a thermometer. Strain milk through a clean cloth to remove hair and dirt. A dairy thermometer (1,000 KES) ensures you cool milk quickly. Seventh, a mobile phone for orders and payments. M-Pesa or Airtel Money is standard. Eighth, a weighing scale if you sell by weight, though most sell by volume. Ninth, a receipt book for credit sales. Tenth, a uniform or apron for hygiene.

You do not need everything at once. Start with two cans, a strainer, a record book, and a phone. Add a cooler when you supply hotels or processors. Buy tools from agro-vet shops or dairy cooperatives. Keep them clean: wash cans with hot water and detergent after each use, then sun-dry. Dirty tools cause milk to spoil and buyers to reject. Invest in quality; cheap tools cost more in the long run.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Assess your daily milk volume. For one week, measure morning and evening milk from all cows. Suppose you get 80 litres daily. Step 2: Identify potential buyers within 5 km. Walk or ride around. List neighbours, shops, hotels, schools, and processors. Note what they buy and at what price. Step 3: Choose a mix of routes. Aim to sell 60% to a reliable buyer (processor or hotel) and 40% to neighbours or kiosks. This balances price and security. Step 4: Set a price. Check local rates: in Kenya, farmgate price is 35-50 KES per litre. Add delivery cost if you deliver. For neighbours, price slightly lower than shop milk.

Step 5: Make a daily route plan. For example: 6:00 AM milk cows, 6:30 strain and cool, 7:00 deliver to hotel (20 L), 7:30 deliver to kiosk (10 L), 8:00 sell to neighbours (10 L). Evening: 5:00 PM milk, 5:30 deliver to processor (40 L). Step 6: Secure agreements. For hotels, agree on daily volume, price, and payment (cash on delivery or weekly). For processors, sign a contract if possible. Step 7: Deliver on time. Late milk is rejected. Use a cooler box with ice packs for long trips. Step 8: Record every sale. In your book, write date, buyer, litres, price, total, and paid/unpaid. Step 9: Collect payments. For credit, set a limit (e.g., 1,000 KES) and follow up weekly. Step 10: Review weekly. Which route pays best? Which is most reliable? Adjust your plan.

Step 11: Scale up. When you add cows, find new buyers before milk increases. Step 12: Keep buyers happy. Ask for feedback. If they complain about taste or temperature, fix it. For example, if milk spoils, cool it faster. If they want smaller quantities, pack in 1-litre bottles. This method ensures you sell all milk daily and build a stable income.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Selling only to one buyer. If that buyer stops, you lose all income. Fix: Always have at least three buyers. For example, a processor, a hotel, and neighbours. Mistake 2: Selling on credit without records. You forget who owes what. Fix: Use a receipt book. Write name, date, litres, amount, and balance. Set a credit limit of 500 KES per person. Mistake 3: Delivering late. Hotels need milk by 6 AM. If you arrive at 8 AM, they reject it. Fix: Plan your route and leave early. Use a cooler to keep milk fresh if delayed.

Mistake 4: Not cooling milk. In hot weather, milk spoils in 2 hours. Fix: Cool milk to below 4C immediately after milking. Use a fridge or ice packs in a cooler box. Mistake 5: Dirty cans and hands. Buyers reject milk with dirt or smell. Fix: Wash cans with hot water and soap, rinse with clean water, and sun-dry. Wash hands before milking. Mistake 6: Pricing too low. You work hard but earn little. Fix: Know your cost per litre. If feed, labour, and vet cost 30 KES per litre, sell at least 40 KES. Mistake 7: Ignoring buyer feedback. If a hotel says milk is watery, test your cows for mastitis or check for water addition. Fix: Test milk with a lactometer.

Mistake 8: Not having a backup plan. If your motorcycle breaks, you cannot deliver. Fix: Have a bicycle or arrange with a neighbour. Mistake 9: Overpromising. If you promise 50 litres but only have 30, you lose trust. Fix: Underpromise and overdeliver. Say 30 litres, then bring 35 if possible. Mistake 10: Mixing morning and evening milk without cooling. This speeds spoilage. Fix: Keep batches separate and cool both. Mistake 11: Selling to strangers without payment. Fix: For new buyers, ask for cash on first delivery. Mistake 12: Not keeping records. You cannot tell if you are profitable. Fix: Record every litre and every shilling. Review weekly.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety starts with clean milk. Bacteria from dirty hands, udders, or cans cause disease and spoilage. Always wash your hands with soap before milking. Clean the udder with warm water and dry with a clean cloth. Use a strip cup to check for mastitis (clots or blood). Discard milk from sick cows or cows on antibiotics. Antibiotics in milk can harm people and cause processors to reject your whole batch. Withdraw milk for the required period (usually 3-5 days) after treatment.

Keep milk cold. After milking, strain it into a clean can and cool immediately. If you have a fridge, set it to 4C. If not, use a cooler box with ice packs. Never leave milk in the sun. Transport in a shaded box. For long distances, use a insulated container. Test milk before selling: use a lactometer to check density (should be 1.026-1.032) and an alcohol test to check acidity. If milk fails, do not sell it; find out why.

Practice hygiene at the point of sale. Use clean containers, not dirty jerrycans. Do not touch the inside of cans. Pour milk through a strainer. If selling to neighbours, use a clean jug and funnel. Wash hands before handling money and milk; better, have someone else handle money. Keep records safe. Use a book or phone app. Back up phone data. For payments, use M-Pesa to avoid cash theft. If you must carry cash, vary your route and time.

Best practice: build relationships. Greet buyers, ask about their needs, and deliver on time. Offer a small discount for bulk or prompt payment. For processors, follow their quality standards exactly. For hotels, package milk in clean 1-litre bottles if they prefer. Train your workers on hygiene and safety. Have a first aid kit for cuts. Wear closed shoes when handling cows. Finally, insure your cows and equipment if possible. Safety and best practice protect your health, your buyers, and your income.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will plan and execute a daily milk sales route for one week. You need: a notebook, a pen, a measuring can, a cooler box with ice packs, and a phone. If you have a bicycle or motorcycle, use it; otherwise, walk. First, measure your daily milk volume for three days. Record morning and evening litres. Suppose you get 60 litres daily. Second, list at least five potential buyers within 3 km. Include their name, type (neighbour, shop, hotel, processor), location, and current price they pay. Visit them and ask: How much milk do you need daily? What price? When do you need it? How will you pay?

Third, design a route. For example: 6:00 AM milk, 6:30 cool and pack, 7:00 deliver 10 L to Mama Njeri (neighbour), 7:20 deliver 15 L to Ushindi Hotel, 7:50 deliver 20 L to Kiosk, 8:10 return home. Evening: 5:00 PM milk, 5:30 deliver 15 L to processor. Adjust based on buyer needs. Fourth, set prices. For neighbours, 50 KES/L; hotel, 55 KES/L; kiosk, 45 KES/L; processor, 40 KES/L. Calculate expected daily income. For 60 L: 10*50 + 15*55 + 20*45 + 15*40 = 500 + 825 + 900 + 600 = 2,825 KES.

Fifth, execute for five days. Each day, record actual litres sold, buyer, price, amount, and payment status. Note any problems: late delivery, rejection, spoilage. Sixth, at the end of the week, calculate total income and total litres sold. Compare with your plan. Identify which route was most profitable and which was most reliable. Seventh, adjust your plan for next week. For example, if the hotel pays better but demands early delivery, buy a cooler box. If a neighbour often pays late, reduce credit or ask for cash. Write a one-page report: your route, your income, your challenges, and your improvements. This exercise gives you a real system to sell every day.

<h2>Practical Reference: Selling Every Day Routes to Market for Daily Milk Sales</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working companion to <strong>Selling Every Day: Routes to Market</strong>, part of the RISE AFRICA skills course <em>Daily Milk Sales: Dairy Herd System</em>. It is written for smallholder dairy producers, milk traders, cooperative field officers, and agripreneurs who need to move fresh milk from the cow to a paying customer every single day reliably, profitably, and safely.</p>

<p>The core business principle is simple: <strong>milk is a perishable daily cash-flow asset</strong>. A litre not sold today is a litre lost tomorrow. Your route to market the physical and commercial pathway your milk travels determines your price, your volume, your wastage, and your reputation. Choosing and managing that route is a business decision, not an afterthought.</p>

<h3>Why Routes to Market Matter</h3>

<p>In most African dairy systems, four dominant routes exist: (1) direct farm-gate or household sales, (2) informal trader or bicycle/motorcycle collection, (3) cooperative or processor collection, and (4) direct-to-institution contracts (schools, hotels, cafs). Each carries a different price per litre, a different payment cycle, and a different level of risk. A producer selling 20 litres per day at KES 60 farm-gate earns KES 1,200 daily; the same 20 litres at KES 50 to a processor earns KES 1,000 but with guaranteed daily collection and no spoilage. The "best" route is the one that maximises net margin after transport, spoilage, and time costs, while matching your herd's daily output.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Method: Building Your Daily Route</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Measure your daily output.</strong> Record morning and evening milk for 7 consecutive days. You cannot plan a route without knowing your true volume.</li>
<li><strong>Map your buyers within 5 km.</strong> List households, shops, hotels, schools, and traders. Note their daily demand and price offered.</li>
<li><strong>Test milk quality.</strong> Use the alcohol test and lactometer. Buyers reject adulterated or high-acid milk quality is your passport.</li>
<li><strong>Cool immediately.</strong> Milk above 4C loses 1020% value per hour in tropical heat. Use a charcoal cooler, fridge, or chilled water bath.</li>
<li><strong>Choose your primary route.</strong> Match route to volume using Table 2. Start with one route; add a second only when volume exceeds primary demand.</li>
<li><strong>Set a price and payment term.</strong> Write it down. For informal buyers, insist on cash on delivery. For contracts, agree on a 15-day or 30-day cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Deliver on time, every day.</strong> Reliability beats price. A buyer who trusts your 6 a.m. delivery will not switch for a 5-cent discount.</li>
<li><strong>Track daily.</strong> Record litres sold, price, buyer, and payment received. Review weekly; adjust route mix monthly.</li>
</ol>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c"> SAFETY WARNING BRUCELLOSIS &amp; MILK-BORNE DISEASE</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Raw (unpasteurised) milk can transmit <em>Brucella</em>, <em>Mycobacterium bovis</em> (TB), <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>, and <em>Listeria</em>. In African smallholder systems where pasteurisation is not always available, the following are <strong>non-negotiable</strong>:</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px">
<li>Never sell milk from an animal showing signs of disease, abortion, or mastitis.</li>
<li>Boil or pasteurise (63C for 30 min or 72C for 15 sec) all milk not consumed within 2 hours.</li>
<li>Wash hands, udder, and equipment with clean water and disinfectant before and after milking.</li>
<li>Use food-grade stainless steel or aluminium cans never plastic jerrycans previously used for fuel or chemicals.</li>
<li>Keep cold chain below 4C from milking to sale. Reject any milk with off-smell, clots, or abnormal colour.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Record daily milk volume (AM + PM) for 7 days before choosing a route.</li>
<li> Cool milk to below 4C within 2 hours of milking.</li>
<li> Test every batch with the alcohol test before sale.</li>
<li> Deliver at the same time every day reliability sells.</li>
<li> Insist on cash for informal sales; written terms for contracts.</li>
<li> Keep at least two routes active to reduce buyer risk.</li>
<li> Log litres, price, buyer, and payment daily in a simple notebook.</li>
<li> Review route profitability monthly and adjust the mix.</li>
</ul>
