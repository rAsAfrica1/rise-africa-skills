1. Overview of Pork Primal Cuts

Pork primal cuts are the large sections a butcher creates when breaking down a whole pig. In Africa, common primals include the shoulder (often called Boston butt or picnic), loin, belly, leg (ham), and sometimes the head and jowl. Each primal yields specific retail cuts. The shoulder is tough but flavorful, ideal for slow cooking or sausage. The loin runs along the back and gives chops, roasts, and tenderloin. The belly is fatty and used for bacon. The leg is lean and used for fresh hams or cured ham. Understanding these primals helps you plan how to cut a carcass efficiently. A typical market-weight pig in Africa weighs 60â90 kg dressed. From that, expect about 20% shoulder, 20% loin, 15% belly, 25% leg, and the rest in offal, fat, and bone. Tools needed: a sharp breaking knife (8â10 inch), a bone saw, a meat hook, and a clean cutting table. Always work in a cool room (below 10Â°C) if possible. Start by splitting the carcass down the spine. Then remove the head, feet, and internal fat. Next, separate the shoulder from the loin by cutting between the 4th and 5th ribs. Remove the loin from the leg by cutting at the hip joint. Finally, remove the belly from the loin by cutting along the rib ends. Label and store each primal properly. This overview sets the foundation for the next lessons.

2. Breaking Down a Whole Pork Carcass

Breaking down a whole pork carcass is heavy work but follows a logical sequence. You need a sturdy rail or table, a meat saw, a breaking knife, a boning knife, a sharpening steel, and a clean apron. Start with a carcass that has been chilled for 24 hours at 0â4Â°C. First, place the carcass on its back. Remove the head by cutting through the atlas joint (between skull and first neck vertebra). Save the head for head cheese or stock. Remove the feet at the hock joint using a saw. Next, split the carcass lengthwise down the spine using a saw or cleaver. You now have two sides. For each side, remove the leaf fat (kidney fat) and set aside for lard. Remove the kidneys and liver if still present. Now separate the shoulder: cut between the 4th and 5th ribs, then through the shoulder joint. The shoulder includes the picnic and Boston butt. Next, remove the loin by cutting along the rib ends from the shoulder to the hip. The loin runs from the last rib to the hip bone. Then remove the belly by cutting just below the ribs, leaving the loin intact. Finally, separate the leg (ham) from the loin by cutting through the hip joint. You now have primal cuts: shoulder, loin, belly, leg, and miscellaneous parts like ribs, jowl, and hocks. Weigh each primal to track yield. A 80 kg carcass should yield about 16 kg shoulder, 16 kg loin, 12 kg belly, 20 kg leg, and 16 kg of bones, fat, and offal. Clean your tools and table with hot soapy water after. This breakdown is the core skill of pork fabrication.

3. Cutting Pork Chops and Roasts

Pork chops come from the loin. After removing the loin primal, you have a long muscle with a bone (the rib or spine) on one side. Decide whether to keep it bone-in or boneless. Bone-in chops are more flavorful and common in African markets. To cut chops, first trim the loin of excess fat, leaving about 1 cm of fat cap. Remove the tenderloin if still attached (it sits on the inner side). For bone-in chops, use a meat saw to cut perpendicular to the spine, about 2.5â3 cm thick. For boneless chops, run your knife along the bones to remove the entire rack, then cut the eye of meat into 2.5 cm slices. A standard chop weighs 150â200 g. For roasts, cut larger sections: a bone-in loin roast is 4â6 ribs long (about 1.5â2 kg). A boneless loin roast is the eye of meat tied with butcher twine at 2 cm intervals. Season or marinate as desired. In Africa, popular dry rubs include paprika, garlic, and chili. For a simple roast, rub with salt, pepper, and crushed garlic, then roast at 180Â°C for 25 minutes per 500 g until internal temperature reaches 63Â°C. Let rest 10 minutes before slicing. Chops can be grilled, pan-fried, or braised. For tough chops, brine in salt water (50 g salt per liter) for 2 hours before cooking. Always cut against the grain for tenderness. Store chops and roasts wrapped in butcher paper or vacuum-sealed at 0â4Â°C for up to 5 days, or freeze for 3 months.

4. Producing Bacon and Ham from the Belly and Leg

Bacon comes from the pork belly. Start with a fresh belly, skin on or off. For dry-cured bacon, mix 25 g curing salt (containing 6.25% sodium nitrite) with 250 g sea salt, 100 g sugar, and 10 g black pepper for a 5 kg belly. Rub the mixture all over the belly. Place in a food-grade plastic bag or non-reactive container. Refrigerate at 2â4Â°C for 7 days, flipping daily. The belly will release liquid; that's normal. After curing, rinse well and pat dry. For smoked bacon, cold smoke at 20â25Â°C for 6â8 hours using wood chips (hickory or apple). If you don't have a smoker, you can oven-bake at 90Â°C for 2 hours to set the cure, then slice. Slice bacon 2â3 mm thick. For ham, use the leg primal. You can make fresh ham (roast) or cured ham. For a wet-cured ham, prepare a brine: 1.5 kg salt, 500 g sugar, 50 g curing salt, and 10 liters water for a 5 kg leg. Inject brine into the leg along the bone and around joints. Submerge the leg in brine and keep at 2â4Â°C for 5 days per kg (so a 5 kg leg cures for 25 days). Then rinse and smoke at 75â85Â°C until internal temperature reaches 68Â°C. In Africa, many artisans use local spices like cloves, bay leaves, and chili in the brine. Always use food-grade curing salts; never use industrial nitrites. After curing, ham can be sliced or left whole. Store bacon and ham in the fridge for 2 weeks or freeze for 6 months.

5. Preparing Ribs and Tenderloin

Pork ribs come from the belly side of the loin. There are two main types: baby back ribs (from the loin) and spare ribs (from the belly). After removing the loin, you'll find the rib rack attached to the belly. To prepare spare ribs, trim the belly to leave a rectangular rack of ribs with about 2 cm of meat on top. Remove the membrane on the bone side by slipping a knife under it and pulling it off. This makes the ribs more tender. Cut the rack into individual portions or leave whole. For baby back ribs, they are the curved ribs attached to the loin. Remove the loin meat first, then separate the ribs. A typical rack has 10â13 ribs. Season with a dry rub (paprika, brown sugar, salt, pepper, garlic powder) and let sit for 30 minutes. Cook low and slow: smoke at 110Â°C for 4â5 hours, or bake at 150Â°C for 2.5 hours, until meat pulls back from the bone. For tenderloin, it's the long, lean muscle on the inside of the loin. Remove it by pulling it away from the loin with your fingers, then trim off silver skin. A whole tenderloin weighs 300â500 g. Cut into medallions 2 cm thick, or leave whole for roasting. Marinate in soy sauce, garlic, and ginger for 2 hours, then grill or pan-sear for 3â4 minutes per side. Tenderloin is very lean, so don't overcook. In Africa, serve with rice, plantains, or chapati. Store ribs and tenderloin at 0â4Â°C for up to 3 days.

6. Utilizing Pork Offal and By-Products

Pork offal includes liver, heart, kidneys, tongue, head, feet, intestines, and blood. These are nutritious and can be profitable. Start with the liver: soak in milk or salt water for 1 hour to remove bitterness. Slice 1 cm thick, dredge in flour, and pan-fry with onions and chili. Heart and tongue: simmer in salted water for 2 hours until tender, then slice for sandwiches or stews. Kidneys: remove the white core, slice, and soak in vinegar water for 30 minutes, then fry with garlic. Feet and hocks: rich in collagen, perfect for slow-cooked soups or pickled feet. Clean thoroughly by scrubbing with salt and vinegar, then boil for 3 hours. Head: make head cheese by boiling the head with spices, picking the meat, and setting in gelatinous broth. Intestines: clean by turning inside out, scraping, and soaking in salt water; use as sausage casings. Blood: collect fresh, stir to prevent clotting, and make blood sausage (black pudding) with onions, fat, and spices. Fat: render leaf fat and back fat into lard by slowly heating at 100Â°C for 2 hours; strain and store. Lard is used for cooking or soap making. Bones: simmer for 6â12 hours to make pork stock. In Africa, offal is often sold at lower prices but can be value-added into ready-to-eat products. Always handle offal with strict hygiene: keep at 0â4Â°C, cook thoroughly to 71Â°C, and never mix with cooked meat. Properly used, offal can increase your carcass profit by 15â20%.

Practical Reference: Pork Fabrication for African Butcheries

Pork fabrication is the process of breaking a whole pig carcass into primal cuts, sub-primals, retail cuts, and value-added products. In an African butchery context, mastery of pork fabrication directly affects your margins: a skilled butcher can extract 8â15% more saleable yield from the same carcass compared to an untrained operator. This practical reference consolidates the core knowledge from the six lessons in this module into a single working document for butchers, shop owners, and meat processors operating in African markets.

Whether you are supplying wet markets in Nairobi, hotels in Lagos, or retail packs in Lusaka, the principles remain constant: understand the anatomy, control the temperature, minimise waste, and convert every part of the carcass into revenue. Pork is one of the fastest-growing meat categories in sub-Saharan Africa, driven by urbanisation, rising incomes, and the expansion of commercial pig production in countries such as Uganda, Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa. This reference is designed to help you capture that demand profitably.

Cutting Pork Chops and Roasts

The loin is the highest-value primal in most African butcheries. To cut consistent chops, first remove the loin from the carcass, trim the fat cover to 5â8 mm, and square the ends. Chops should be cut 2â3 cm thick for retail and 3â4 cm for restaurant supply. For roasts, cut 8â12 cm sections from the rib end (rib roast) or the sirloin end (loin roast).

Bone-in chops command a premium in most markets because customers associate them with flavour and value. However, boneless loin cuts are preferred by hotels and catering operations. Decide your cut profile based on your customer base, not on habit.

Producing Bacon and Ham

Bacon is produced from the belly through curing (wet or dry), optional smoking, and slicing. Ham is produced from the leg through curing, smoking, or cooking. Both are high-margin products that extend shelf life and reduce waste. In African markets, bacon and ham are increasingly demanded by urban supermarkets, hotels, and fast-food outlets.

Preparing Ribs and Tenderloin

Ribs are cut from the belly side of the loin (spare ribs) or from the loin itself (baby back ribs). Remove the membrane from the bone side, trim excess fat, and cut into uniform racks. Tenderloin is removed from the loin and trimmed of silver skin. It is the most tender cut on the pig and should be sold whole or as medallions to premium customers.

In African markets, ribs are increasingly popular for grilling and braai. Tenderloin is a hotel and restaurant cut. Price both according to their premium positioning, not as generic pork.

Utilising Offal and By-Products

Offal â liver, heart, kidney, tongue, trotters, head, and blood â is a significant revenue stream in most African butcheries. In many communities, these products are culturally valued and command good prices. Do not treat them as waste. Clean, chill, and display them properly. Trotters and head can be sold for soups and stews; liver and kidney for grills and stews; blood for traditional dishes.

Pork must be kept at or below 4Â°C from slaughter to sale. Above 4Â°C, Salmonella, Listeria, and Yersinia multiply rapidly. Never leave carcasses or cuts at ambient temperature for more than 2 hours.
