Cold Chain & Storage

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 What the cold chain is

The cold chain is an unbroken series of temperature-controlled steps from harvest to customer:

Farm → Transport → Factory → Storage → Transport → Shop → Home

Every step must be at the right temperature. One break — a truck without refrigeration, a fridge left open, a long power cut — spoils the whole chain. Bacteria multiply fast in warm conditions: at 30°C, E. coli doubles every 20 minutes.

Two temperatures matter most: chilled (0–4°C) and frozen (−18°C or below).

⚠ Power cuts are the biggest threat in Africa. Budget for a backup generator or solar system from day one.

2 Fridge vs freezer vs cool room

Domestic fridge: 0–4°C. Good for small quantities. Not reliable for business.

Commercial fridge: 0–4°C. Larger capacity, more reliable. $500–$3,000 for a good unit.

Freezer: −18°C or below. Long-term storage. $800–$5,000 depending on size.

Cool room: Large walk-in room at 2–8°C. For bulk storage. $5,000–$20,000 to install. Best ROI when volume grows.

Blast freezer: −35°C. Freezes products fast (hours not days). Essential for IQF fruit and vegetables. $10,000–$40,000.

Start with commercial fridges and freezers. Add a cool room when you regularly fill 3+ fridges.

3 Temperature monitoring and logs

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every cold store needs:

  • Thermometer in each fridge/freezer (simple dial or digital)
  • Continuous data logger (records temperatures 24/7 — $30–$200)
  • Alarm system for out-of-range temperatures
  • Written log — check twice daily, sign and date

Standard temperatures to log:

  • Chilled storage: 0–4°C
  • Frozen storage: −18°C or below
  • Delivery vehicle: 0–4°C (chilled) or −18°C (frozen)

Review logs weekly. Any deviation triggers an investigation — what happened, was the product affected, what corrective action was taken?

4 Insulated transport boxes

Getting product from factory to customer without breaking the cold chain requires insulated transport.

Options:

  • Insulated boxes with ice packs — for small deliveries, 4–8 hours
  • Insulated bags — for short local deliveries
  • Refrigerated vans — for regular city deliveries ($15,000–$40,000 used)
  • Refrigerated trucks — for long distance ($50,000+)

Pre-cool the box before loading. Pack frozen products tightly — air gaps waste cold. Use dry ice for extended journeys.

5 Ice packs and dry ice

Ice packs (gel): Freeze overnight, last 8–24 hours in a good insulated box. Cheap, reusable, ideal for local delivery. $1–$3 each.

Dry ice (solid CO₂): −78°C. Lasts 24–72 hours depending on insulation. Used for long-distance frozen shipments. Cost: $2–$5 per kg. Requires ventilation — CO₂ is dangerous in closed spaces.

Eutectic plates: Specialised freezer plates that hold temperature longer than gel packs. Used in professional cold transport.

⚠ Never seal dry ice in an airtight container — it will explode as it sublimates.

6 Cold chain breaks and how to handle them

Break in cold chain = product at risk. What to do:

  1. Record the event (what, when, how long, what temperature reached)
  2. Assess the product — how long was it above safe temperature?
  3. If under 2 hours at moderate temp: product may be safe — test with thermometer
  4. If over 4 hours or reached 15°C+: discard the batch
  5. Document everything
  6. Find the root cause and fix it

Never sell product you are unsure about. One bad batch kills your reputation.

Common causes: power cut, fridge door left open, overloaded fridge, blocked airflow, delivery vehicle breakdown.

7 Dry storage for shelf-stable products

Not everything needs cold storage. Shelf-stable products (dried, canned, jarred) need dry storage instead.

Dry storage conditions:

  • Temperature: 10–20°C (cool, stable)
  • Humidity: 50–60% (dry)
  • Darkness: protect from light
  • Air circulation: not sealed tight
  • Off the floor: pallets or shelving
  • Away from walls: 10 cm gap
  • Away from chemicals: no cleaning products nearby

Store products by expiry date, with oldest at front (FIFO — First In, First Out). Monthly stock counts prevent waste.

8 Stock rotation (FIFO)

FIFO (First In, First Out): Oldest stock sold first. Simple rule, huge impact.

How to implement:

  • Label every batch with the date it was made
  • Stack new stock behind old stock
  • Use oldest stock first when fulfilling orders
  • Weekly check for expired or near-expiry items
  • Discount near-expiry to clear

Benefits: Less waste, safer product, fresher taste for customers, better cash flow (no dead stock).

Train every worker on FIFO. It's a habit, not a policy.

💰 The cold chain and storage are where most food businesses lose money — spoiled product, wasted power, broken equipment. Get this right and your margins improve dramatically.
