1. Common Rabbit Diseases and Parasites

Rabbits are vulnerable to several diseases that can wipe out your herd if you don't act fast. The most common in Africa include coccidiosis, mange, pasteurellosis (snuffles), and enteritis (diarrhoea). Coccidiosis is caused by a parasite in the gut or liver, often from dirty cages. Signs: bloody or watery diarrhoea, weight loss, and sudden death in young rabbits. Treat with amprolium or sulphonamides in drinking water for 5-7 days. Always follow dosage on the label.

Mange is caused by mites that burrow into the skin, especially on the ears and nose. You'll see crusty scabs, hair loss, and the rabbit scratching constantly. Treat with ivermectin injections (0.2 mg per kg body weight) under the skin, repeated after 10 days. Also clean the cage with a flame or strong disinfectant.

Pasteurellosis, or snuffles, is a bacterial infection that causes sneezing, nasal discharge, and matted fur on the front paws. It spreads quickly through sneezing. Isolate sick rabbits immediately. Use antibiotics like enrofloxacin or oxytetracycline, but only under vet advice. There is no vaccine in most African countries, so prevention through good ventilation is key.

Enteritis is often caused by sudden feed changes, dirty water, or stress. Signs include watery or jelly-like droppings, loss of appetite, and a hunched posture. Withdraw feed for 12 hours, provide fresh water with electrolytes, and give a small amount of hay. If it persists, use a broad-spectrum antibiotic. Always introduce new feed gradually over 7 days.

Parasites like fleas, ticks, and ear mites are also common. Use a cat-safe flea powder or spray, and dust the cage bedding. For ear mites, clean the ears with a cotton bud dipped in vegetable oil, then apply a vet-recommended miticide. Check your rabbits weekly: look at ears, eyes, nose, feet, and droppings. Early detection saves money and lives. Keep records of any treatments and dates. Remember, a clean cage, clean water, and clean feed prevent 80% of these problems.

2. Vaccination and Deworming Protocols

Vaccination in rabbits is not as routine as in chickens or cattle, but two diseases are serious enough to warrant it: rabbit haemorrhagic disease (RHD) and myxomatosis. In many African countries, these vaccines are not always available. Check with your local vet or agricultural office. If available, RHD vaccine is given at 10-12 weeks of age, with a booster every 6-12 months. Myxomatosis vaccine is given at 6-8 weeks, with annual boosters. Never vaccinate sick rabbits; wait until they are healthy.

Deworming is more commonly practised. Internal parasites like tapeworms and roundworms can affect rabbits, especially if they graze on contaminated pasture. Use fenbendazole or albendazole. The typical dose is 20 mg per kg body weight, given orally once, then repeated after 10-14 days. For example, a 2 kg rabbit gets 40 mg. Many dewormers come as a paste or liquid; use a syringe without a needle to squirt it into the mouth. Always weigh your rabbit first using a kitchen scale.

External parasites like mites and fleas are treated with ivermectin. The dose is 0.2 mg per kg body weight, injected under the skin or given orally. Repeat every 10 days for three treatments to kill all life stages. For ear mites, you can also use a topical ivermectin drop. Do not use frontline (fipronil) on rabbits; it can be fatal.

A simple schedule: Deworm all rabbits every 3-4 months. Treat for mites only when you see signs, but if you have an outbreak, treat the whole herd. Pregnant does should not be dewormed in the first 2 weeks of pregnancy. Kits (baby rabbits) can be dewormed at 8 weeks. Always consult a vet before using any drug. Keep a record: date, drug name, dose, and which rabbits. This helps you track effectiveness and avoid resistance. In hot, humid African climates, parasites thrive, so be more frequent with checks and treatments.

3. Biosecurity Measures in Rabbitry

Biosecurity means keeping germs out of your rabbitry. It is the cheapest and most effective way to prevent disease. Start with a perimeter: fence your rabbit house to keep out wild rabbits, rodents, and stray dogs. These animals carry diseases like RHD and parasites. Use wire mesh with holes no bigger than 1 cm. Keep grass short around the structure to deny rodents shelter.

Control who enters. Only you and essential workers should go in. Have a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite or quaternary ammonium) at the entrance. Change it every 2-3 days. Wear dedicated boots and overalls inside the rabbitry; do not wear them outside. If you visit another farm or market, change clothes and shower before entering your rabbitry. This is critical because viruses can travel on your clothes.

All-in, all-out management is ideal: keep rabbits in groups by age, and when you sell or cull a group, clean and disinfect the empty cages before bringing in new rabbits. If you cannot do all-in all-out, at least quarantine new rabbits for 30 days in a separate cage, away from the main herd. Watch for signs of illness during quarantine. Only after 30 days of good health should you introduce them.

Clean and disinfect regularly. Remove droppings daily. Scrub feeders and waterers with soap and water weekly, then disinfect. Use a disinfectant that works against viruses and bacteria: 1 part bleach to 10 parts water (0.5% sodium hypochlorite) is cheap and effective. Let it sit for 10 minutes before rinsing. For cages, a flame torch can be used on metal parts to kill mites and eggs. Bedding should be changed at least twice a week, or daily if wet.

Feed and water hygiene: store feed in sealed containers off the floor. Do not feed mouldy hay or spoiled greens. Water should be clean and fresh; use nipple drinkers rather than open bowls to prevent contamination. Keep a closed herd: breed your own replacements rather than buying from unknown sources. If you must buy, buy from a reputable breeder with a health record. Finally, control flies and mosquitoes with screens or traps; they can spread diseases mechanically. A simple fly trap made from a plastic bottle and sugar water works well.

4. Recognizing Signs of Illness

Rabbits hide illness until they are very sick. As a farmer, you must observe your rabbits daily. Spend 10 minutes each morning and evening just watching. Healthy rabbits are alert, have bright eyes, clean noses, and eat and drink normally. They hop around and groom themselves. Any change from this is a warning sign.

First, look at behaviour. A sick rabbit may sit hunched in a corner, grind its teeth (sign of pain), or be reluctant to move. It may stop eating or drinking. If a rabbit that usually runs to the feeder ignores food, check it immediately. Also watch for aggression or unusual vocalisation.

Second, check the body. Eyes should be clear, not cloudy or weeping. Ears should be clean inside, without crusts or brown wax. Nose should be dry; any white or yellow discharge means snuffles. Front paws should not be matted with fur or discharge, which happens when a rabbit wipes its nose. Teeth should be aligned; overgrown teeth cause drooling and weight loss. Feel the belly: it should be soft, not bloated or hard. Check the rear end for diarrhoea or soiled fur, which attracts flies and causes flystrike.

Third, examine droppings. Healthy rabbit droppings are round, firm, and brown, like small marbles. You may also see soft, shiny caecotrophs that rabbits eat directly from the anusâthis is normal. Diarrhoea, jelly-like droppings, or no droppings at all are emergencies. Blood in droppings suggests coccidiosis. Also check urine: it can be cloudy or reddish, which is often normal, but bloody urine is a sign of infection.

Fourth, monitor weight. Weigh your rabbits monthly, especially breeding does and growing kits. A sudden weight loss of 10% or more is a red flag. Use a simple kitchen scale. Keep a notebook for each rabbit or cage. Record any signs, dates, and actions. If you see any of these signs, isolate the rabbit immediately and consult a vet. Early treatment is cheaper and more successful. Remember: one sick rabbit can infect the whole herd, so act fast.

5. First Aid for Rabbits

First aid can save a rabbit's life before the vet arrives. Keep a basic kit: antiseptic solution (e.g., diluted povidone-iodine), clean gauze, bandages, scissors, tweezers, a digital thermometer, a syringe without a needle, electrolyte powder, and a small bottle of vegetable oil. Also have a carrier or box for transport.

For cuts and wounds: clean with antiseptic, apply an antibiotic ointment (not one with steroids), and cover with a light bandage if needed. If bleeding heavily, apply pressure with gauze for 2 minutes. For minor cuts, just keep clean. If a wound is deep or won't stop bleeding, see a vet.

For heat stroke: rabbits suffer above 28Â°C. Signs: panting, drooling, lying stretched out, and hot ears. Move the rabbit to a cool, shaded area. Wet its ears and feet with cool water (not cold), and offer water with electrolytes. Do not submerge the rabbit in water. Use a fan if available. If it doesn't improve in 30 minutes, get to a vet.

For diarrhoea: withdraw all feed for 12 hours, but keep fresh water with electrolytes available. Give 1 ml of kaolin-pectin (available at pharmacies) orally twice a day. After 12 hours, offer small amounts of hay and plain pellets. If diarrhoea persists beyond 24 hours, see a vet.

For flystrike: this is when flies lay eggs on soiled fur, and maggots hatch. It is an emergency. Clip the fur around the affected area, remove maggots with tweezers, and flush the wound with diluted antiseptic. Apply an antibiotic ointment. Keep the rabbit indoors and treat with ivermectin. Consult a vet for severe cases.

For broken bones or fractures: do not try to splint yourself unless trained. Muzzle the rabbit with a towel to prevent biting, and place it in a small carrier with limited movement. Go to the vet immediately.

For poisoning: if you suspect the rabbit ate a toxic plant (e.g., avocado leaves, rhubarb), give 5 ml of activated charcoal mixed with water orally, and rush to the vet. Always know your vet's phone number and the nearest clinic. In remote areas, keep a contact for a veterinary officer who can advise by phone. Remember, first aid is not a substitute for professional care; it buys time.

6. Working with a Veterinarian

A good veterinarian is your partner in rabbit farming. In many African countries, vets are scarce, especially those who know rabbits. Start by finding one before you have an emergency. Ask other rabbit farmers, the local agricultural office, or a university veterinary school. If you cannot find a rabbit-savvy vet, find one willing to learn with you. Share books or online resources.

Establish a relationship early. Invite the vet to your farm for a routine visit. Show them your setup, discuss your health records, and ask for advice on vaccination and deworming schedules. A vet can also help you design a biosecurity plan. Expect to pay for visits, but many vets offer phone consultations at a lower fee. Keep their number saved.

When you call a vet, be prepared. Describe the signs: how many rabbits are affected, their ages, what you see (e.g., diarrhoea, sneezing), and what you have already tried. Have your records ready. If the vet needs to see a rabbit, bring the sick one and a fresh faecal sample in a clean container. For post-mortem, bring the whole carcass in a plastic bag on ice if possible.

Vets can prescribe antibiotics and other drugs that are not available over the counter. Never use human antibiotics without vet advice; wrong dosage can cause resistance or kill your rabbits. A vet can also perform surgery, such as for abscesses or broken bones. If you need to euthanize a rabbit, a vet can do it humanely.

Cost is a concern. Ask about fees upfront. Some vets offer package deals for herd health visits. You can also form a cooperative with other rabbit farmers to share the cost of a vet visit. In some areas, government veterinary services are free or subsidised for livestock, including rabbits. Check with your local department of agriculture.

Finally, learn from your vet. Ask them to show you how to give injections, clip teeth, or treat wounds. The more you can do yourself, the less you rely on costly visits. But never hesitate to call for serious problems. A good vet will respect you for seeking help early. Keep a file with all prescriptions, diagnoses, and outcomes. This history helps the vet treat your herd better in future.

Practical Reference: Health Management & Disease Prevention in African Rabbitries

In African rabbit farming, disease is rarely just a veterinary problem â it is a business problem. A coccidiosis outbreak in a 40-doe unit can wipe out an entire kindling cycle, delay sales by eight weeks, and destroy the working capital you set aside for feed. Because rabbits are small, prolific, and fast-maturing, they can be one of the most profitable livestock enterprises on a smallholding â but only if mortality stays under control. This practical reference consolidates the disease, vaccination, biosecurity, first-aid, and veterinary information you need to protect your stock, your cash flow, and your reputation with buyers.

Use this module as a field manual. Keep a printed copy in your rabbitry, record every observation, and treat every death as a data point. Healthy rabbits convert feed at 4:1; sick rabbits convert your profits into losses.

Vaccine availability varies widely across Africa. In South Africa, RHD and myxomatosis vaccines are registered and widely used. In Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, and Ghana, RHD vaccine is available through veterinary importers but often at premium prices. Where vaccines are unavailable, biosecurity and culling become your primary tools.

3. Biosecurity Measures in Your Rabbitry

Biosecurity is the cheapest medicine you will ever buy. In practice, it means controlling three things: what comes in, what goes out, and who moves between them.

  • Quarantine every new rabbit for 30 days â separate hutch, separate tools, handled last each day.
  • Footbath at the entrance â a simple basin of disinfectant (e.g., Virkon or bleach solution) changed daily.
  • All-in/all-out where possible â avoid mixing age groups in the same hutch.
  • Control vectors â mosquito nets, fly traps, and rodent control reduce myxomatosis and Eimeria spread.
  • Feed and water hygiene â store feed off the floor, use nipple drinkers where affordable, clean troughs daily.
  • Visitor log â buyers, vets, and extension officers should enter only the dirty zone unless suited up.
  • Dead stock disposal â bury at least 1 m deep, away from water sources, or incinerate.
  • Record keeping â date, hutch, animal ID, observation, action. This is your early-warning system.

4. Recognising Signs of Illness Early

Rabbits are prey animals â they hide illness until it is advanced. Your job is to notice the small changes. A rabbit that stops eating for 12 hours is already in trouble. Train yourself to do a daily 60-second check: eyes, nose, ears, breathing, droppings, posture, and feed intake.

Red flags: grinding teeth (pain), hunched posture, laboured breathing, head tilt, diarrhoea or no droppings, sudden weight loss, abscesses, or a doe that abandons her litter. Any of these justifies isolation and a vet call.

5. First Aid for Rabbits

First aid buys time â it does not replace veterinary care. Keep a basic kit: gloves, antiseptic (povidone-iodine), sterile gauze, vet wrap, syringes (1 ml and 5 ml), electrolyte solution, and a heat source for shock.

  • Wounds: clean with saline, apply antiseptic, cover, isolate.
  • Heat stress: move to shade, wet ears, offer cool water with electrolytes.
  • Diarrhoea: remove greens, offer hay and water, isolate; suspect coccidiosis.
  • Abscess: do not squeeze; let a vet lance and drain.
  • Fractures: immobilise, reduce stress, seek vet â most fractures in rabbits are not economically repairable.

6. Working with a Veterinarian

Find a vet before you need one. Ask about rabbit experience, vaccine access, and drug availability. A good working relationship means faster diagnosis, legal drug access, and better prices on bulk treatments. In many African countries, the local veterinary office or agricultural extension service can also advise on notifiable diseases such as RHD.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Quarantine all new rabbits for 30 days in a separate hutch.
  2. Record daily: feed intake, droppings, behaviour, and any deaths.
  3. Use footbath and gloves; wash hands before and after handling.
  4. Keep weaners separate from adults and from other age groups.
  5. Vaccinate against RHD and myxomatosis where available; deworm strategically.
  6. Control mosquitoes, flies, and rodents â they carry disease.
  7. Dispose of dead stock by deep burial or incineration, away from water.
  8. Call your vet early â do not wait for the whole herd to show signs.
