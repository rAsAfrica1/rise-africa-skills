1. Developing a Farm Biosecurity Plan

A biosecurity plan is a written set of rules and routines that keep diseases off your farm. Without one, a single visitor, shared feed sack, or wild bird can wipe out your entire flock in days. Start by drawing a simple map of your farm. Mark the gate, feed store, broiler houses, water source, and dead bird pit. Then draw three zones: outside (public road), transition (parking and footbath), and inside (the broiler house). Only you and your workers enter the inside zone. Everyone else stops at the transition zone.

Next, list every way disease can enter: new chicks, feed, water, equipment, people, rodents, insects, wild birds, and dead birds. For each, write one action you will take. For example: 'All chicks must come from a certified hatchery with a vaccination record.' 'Feed sacks must be stored on pallets in a closed room.' 'Visitors must sign a log and wear farm boots.' 'Dead birds are picked up twice daily and put in a sealed pit.' Keep this list on one page and post it at the farm gate.

Train your workers on the plan. Hold a 30-minute meeting every Monday to review. Use pictures if some workers cannot read. Assign one person as the biosecurity officer. That person checks footbaths daily, refills disinfectant, and records any sick birds. Give them a notebook and a pen. Every week, walk the farm and look for breaks: holes in walls, spilled feed, standing water, or a missing footbath. Fix them immediately.

Your plan must be practical. If you cannot afford a concrete footbath, use a plastic basin with a sponge and 2% bleach solution. If you have no fence, use a rope and sign that says 'No Entry Biosecurity Area.' Review the plan every three months. After any disease outbreak, update it. A good plan is one you actually follow, not one that sits in a drawer. Start small, be consistent, and your flock will reward you with fewer sick birds and more profit.

2. Cleaning and Disinfection Procedures

Cleaning removes dirt and organic matter; disinfection kills germs. You must clean first, because disinfectants do not work on dirty surfaces. Between flocks, follow this five-step process: dry clean, wash, rinse, disinfect, and dry. First, remove all feed, litter, and equipment. Scrape manure off floors, walls, and feeders. Sweep ceilings and vents. Burn or compost the old litter far from the house.

Next, wash everything with warm water and detergent. Use a high-pressure sprayer if available; otherwise a stiff brush and buckets. Pay attention to corners, cracks, fan blades, and water lines. Flush water lines with a 10% bleach solution, then rinse. For floors and walls, use a detergent like washing powder (1 cup per 10 liters of water). Scrub until the surface is visibly clean. Rinse with clean water.

Then disinfect. Common disinfectants in Africa include bleach (sodium hypochlorite), quaternary ammonium, and glutaraldehyde. For bleach, mix 1 part bleach to 9 parts water (about 0.5% solution). Make fresh solution every day because bleach loses strength. Apply with a knapsack sprayer or a mop. Cover all surfaces: floors, walls, feeders, drinkers, and equipment. Leave for 10 minutes, then let dry. For footbaths, use a strong solution (2% bleach) and change it daily or when dirty.

After disinfection, allow the house to dry completely. Sunlight helps kill germs. If possible, leave the house empty for 7 to 14 days before bringing in new chicks. This is called downtime. During downtime, repair holes, fix leaks, and clean the feed store. Always wear boots and gloves when handling chemicals. Store disinfectants in a locked cabinet away from feed. Keep a record of what you used and when. Proper cleaning and disinfection can reduce disease by 80% or more. It costs little but saves a lot.

3. Traffic Control and Visitor Policies

Every person, vehicle, or animal that enters your farm can carry disease. Traffic control means limiting and directing movement. Start at the gate. Put a sign: 'Biosecurity Area Authorized Persons Only.' Keep the gate locked. If you have a fence, maintain it. If not, use a rope barrier. All vehicles must park outside the fence. Only essential vehicles (feed truck, vet) enter, and their wheels must be sprayed with disinfectant (2% bleach) or driven through a footbath mat.

For people, create a visitor policy. No visitor enters the broiler house without permission. All visitors must sign a log: name, date, time, purpose, and last poultry contact. They must wear clean farm boots and a disposable overall or clean lab coat. Provide a footbath at the entrance of each house: a basin with 2% bleach solution and a brush. Visitors step in, scrub soles, and step on a dry mat. Change footbath solution daily. If a visitor has been on another poultry farm in the last 48 hours, do not allow entry.

Workers must follow the same rules. Keep a set of boots and overalls for each worker. They should not wear these clothes home. If they keep their own chickens at home, they must change clothes and shower before entering your farm. Ideally, workers should not keep backyard poultry. If they must, their birds should be vaccinated and kept away from your farm.

Deliveries: Feed and chicks should be received at the gate. Use your own trolley to move them inside. Do not let delivery drivers enter the houses. For dead bird pickup, have a designated spot at the fence. The rendering truck never enters. Keep a log of all traffic. Review it weekly. If you see disease, you can trace back who came in. Remember, a single contaminated boot can infect 5,000 birds. Traffic control is cheap insurance.

4. Rodent and Insect Control Programs

Rodents and insects carry diseases like Salmonella, E. coli, and fowl pox. They also eat feed and stress birds. A good control program has three parts: exclude, eliminate, and monitor. Start with exclusion. Repair holes in walls, roofs, and floors. Use wire mesh (1 cm holes) on vents and windows. Keep grass and weeds cut short around houses (less than 10 cm). Remove junk piles, old equipment, and spilled feed. Store feed in sealed bins or on pallets at least 30 cm off the floor. Fix leaking taps and drain standing water.

Next, eliminate. For rodents, use bait stations with anticoagulant rodenticides (like brodifacoum). Place stations along walls, not in open areas. Secure them so birds cannot access. Check daily and refill. For every rodent you see, there are 10 more hidden. Traps (snap traps or glue boards) work for small numbers. For insects, use insecticides approved for poultry houses. Common ones include permethrin and cypermethrin. Spray walls, floors, and cracks. For flies, use fly bait or sticky ribbons. For darkling beetles (lesser mealworms), clean out old litter and apply insecticide between flocks.

Monitor. Keep a simple record: date, location, number of rodents caught or insects seen. Use tracking powder or chew marks to find rodent runways. If you see many flies, increase cleaning frequency. For mosquitoes, remove standing water and use larvicide in drains. Always follow label instructions. Never use rat poison in feed areas. Store chemicals away from birds. Wear gloves and a mask when spraying.

Integrate with your biosecurity plan. A clean farm has fewer pests. After each flock, do a deep clean and apply residual insecticide. Keep a pest control calendar: monthly bait check, weekly fly count, quarterly deep spray. If you have a serious infestation, call a professional pest control company. But most farms can manage with consistent effort. Healthy birds need a pest-free environment.

5. Dead Bird Disposal Methods

Dead birds are a major disease risk. They attract flies, rodents, and wild birds. They can contaminate feed and water. You must remove them immediately. Collect dead birds at least twice a day, once early morning and once late afternoon. Wear gloves and use a dedicated bucket or wheelbarrow that stays outside the house. Never carry dead birds through the feed store. After collection, wash your hands and change gloves.

The best disposal methods are incineration, composting, and deep burial. Incineration: use a small incinerator or a 200-liter drum with a grate. Burn dead birds with wood or diesel until only ash remains. This kills all pathogens. It is expensive but effective for small flocks. Composting: build a two-bin compost system with a roof. Layer dead birds with litter, straw, or sawdust. Add water to keep it moist. Turn every 2 weeks. After 3 months, the compost is safe to use. This method is cheap and good for medium flocks. Deep burial: dig a pit 2 meters deep, at least 100 meters from wells and 30 meters from houses. Place birds in, cover with lime (1 kg per bird), then fill with soil. Mark the pit. Never bury near water sources.

Never feed dead birds to dogs, pigs, or other animals. Never sell them. Never throw them in a river or bush. That spreads disease to other farms. If you have a disease outbreak, use incineration or deep burial with extra lime. For routine deaths (less than 1% per week), composting works well. Keep a record: date, number of dead birds, and method used. If deaths increase suddenly, call a vet.

On small farms, a simple pit with a lid works. Cover it with a heavy stone. Add a little lime each time. Keep children and animals away. Always wash your hands and boots after handling dead birds. Proper disposal protects your flock, your neighbors, and your community.

6. Quarantine and Isolation Protocols

Quarantine means separating new or sick birds from the healthy flock. Isolation means keeping sick birds away from healthy ones. Both are critical to stop disease spread. When you bring in new chicks, they should come from a certified hatchery and be placed in a clean, disinfected house. If you bring in older birds (for example, from a market), quarantine them for at least 30 days in a separate house at least 100 meters from your main flock. Use separate boots, clothes, and equipment. Feed and water them last. If they show no signs of disease after 30 days, you can introduce them.

For sick birds, act immediately. Look for signs: ruffled feathers, watery droppings, coughing, sneezing, swollen eyes, or sudden death. Isolate sick birds in a separate pen or house. Wear dedicated boots and gloves. Do not move between sick and healthy birds without changing clothes and washing hands. Use a footbath at the entrance of the isolation area. If many birds are sick, cull the affected group. Bury or incinerate carcasses. Disinfect the area.

During an outbreak, stop all movement. No birds in or out. No visitors. Cancel deliveries. Increase cleaning and disinfection. Use a separate set of tools for the sick house. After the outbreak, wait at least 21 days after the last death or recovery before restocking. Then clean and disinfect thoroughly, and leave the house empty for 14 days.

Quarantine also applies to you and your workers. If you visit another poultry farm, market, or live bird market, change clothes and shower before entering your farm. Keep a set of farm-only clothes and boots. If you have backyard chickens, keep them away from the broiler houses. Vaccinate your flock according to the schedule. Common vaccines for broilers include Newcastle, Infectious Bronchitis, and Gumboro. A good quarantine and isolation protocol can save your whole flock. Be strict, be consistent.

<h2>Practical Reference: Biosecurity &amp; Disease Prevention in African Broiler Farming</h2>

<p>Biosecurity is the cheapest insurance policy available to a broiler producer. In Africa, where veterinary infrastructure is often thin, diagnostic turnaround is slow, and the nearest vaccine cold chain may be hours away, prevention is not a technical nicety it is the difference between a profitable cycle and a total loss. This practical reference consolidates the core operating standards from across this module into one working document you can print, laminate, and post at the farm gate and in the control room.</p>

<p>Treat every item below as a business control, not a formality. A single disease outbreak on a 5,000-bird farm can wipe out 36 months of margin, destroy buyer relationships, and trigger lender scrutiny. The procedures here are designed to be implemented with locally available materials and at low cost.</p>

<h2>1. Developing a Farm Biosecurity Plan</h2>
<p>Your plan must be written, dated, signed by the farm manager, and reviewed at least twice a year. It should identify: (a) all entry points to the farm; (b) who and what may cross each zone; (c) the cleaning and disinfection routine per house; (d) the source and health status of every batch of chicks; (e) feed and water suppliers; and (f) the actions to take if mortality exceeds 1% per day for two consecutive days. Keep a copy at the gate and one in the office. Train every worker against it during induction and re-train quarterly.</p>

<h2>2. Cleaning and Disinfection Procedures</h2>
<p>Effective C&amp;D follows a fixed sequence: dry clean wash disinfect dry rest. Never skip the dry clean; organic matter inactivates most disinfectants. Use a two-bucket system, dedicated brushes per house, and rotate disinfectant active ingredients (e.g., quaternary ammonium glutaraldehyde sodium hypochlorite) to prevent resistance. Allow at least 714 days downtime between flocks.</p>

<h2>3. Traffic Control and Visitor Policies</h2>
<p>Every vehicle and person entering the farm is a potential vector. Establish a single controlled entry point with a vehicle wheel bath, a pedestrian footbath, and a visitor log recording name, date, last poultry contact, and purpose. No visitor enters the broiler house without clean coveralls, dedicated boots, and a 48-hour gap from any other poultry premises. Delivery trucks must stop at the boundary; feed is offloaded into a store, not driven to the house.</p>

<h2>4. Rodent and Insect Control Programs</h2>
<p>Rats and darkling beetles carry <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>, and <em>Pasteurella</em>; flies mechanically transmit <em>Newcastle disease</em> and <em>avian influenza</em> virus. Run a baiting map with numbered stations checked weekly, keep a 1-metre vegetation-free gravel strip around each house, and store feed in sealed, elevated bins. Insect control combines sanitation (no spilled feed, no wet litter) with targeted insecticides rotated by mode of action.</p>

<h2>5. Dead Bird Disposal Methods</h2>
<p>Never sell, feed, or leave dead birds exposed. Options, in order of preference for African conditions: (1) incineration in a purpose-built incinerator; (2) deep burial at least 1.5 m deep, 2 m above the water table, with lime, sited away from wells and boreholes; (3) composting in a sealed bin for small daily mortality. Record every disposal with date, house, and number. Sudden spikes in mortality must trigger an immediate veterinary call and a movement freeze on the farm.</p>

Always wear gloves, a mask, and dedicated clothing when handling sick or dead birds, and wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds afterwards. Never eat, drink, or smoke in the poultry house. Disinfectants are corrosive: store them locked, away from children and feed, and follow label PPE requirements. If you develop fever or respiratory symptoms after contact with sick birds, seek medical attention immediately and mention the poultry exposure.</p>

<h2>6. Quarantine and Isolation Protocols</h2>
<p>New birds and returning workers are the two most common routes of introduction. Quarantine any new batch in a separate house at least 50 m from the main flock for 21 days, with dedicated equipment and a dedicated caretaker who tends quarantined birds last. Isolate any bird showing respiratory distress, diarrhoea, or lameness immediately. If disease is confirmed, declare a farm-wide standstill: no birds in or out, no visitors, and notify the district veterinary officer within 24 hours.</p>

<ul>
<li> Written biosecurity plan signed, dated, and posted at the gate; reviewed every 6 months.</li>
<li> Single controlled entry with vehicle wheel bath, footbath, and visitor log in use.</li>
<li> All-in/all-out placement with 714 days downtime between flocks.</li>
<li> C&amp;D sequence (dry clean wash disinfect dry rest) documented per house.</li>
<li> Numbered rodent bait stations checked weekly; 1 m gravel strip clear around houses.</li>
<li> Dead birds disposed of by incineration, deep burial, or sealed composting logged daily.</li>
<li> New birds quarantined 21 days; sick birds isolated and vet notified within 24 hours.</li>
<li> Feed stored in sealed, elevated bins; no spillage; no wild bird access.</li>
</ul>
