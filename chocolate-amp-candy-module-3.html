Lessons

1. Roasting Cocoa Beans in a Clay Oven or Pan

Roasting develops the chocolate flavor and loosens the bean shells. Start with 2 kg of dried cocoa beans. If using a clay oven, light a wood fire and let it burn down to embers. Spread the beans in a single layer on a metal tray or clay pot. Place the tray over the embers, about 15 cm above the heat. Stir every 2 minutes with a wooden spoon. Roast for 20-30 minutes. The beans are ready when they smell like dark chocolate and the shells crack easily. If using a pan on a stove, use medium heat and stir constantly. You will hear popping sounds. Do not let the beans burn. For a light roast, stop when the beans are light brown. For a dark roast, wait until they are dark brown but not black. After roasting, pour the beans onto a clean cloth or mat to cool for 10 minutes. Rub them with your hands to remove the shells. This is called winnowing. You can also winnow by putting beans in a basket and tossing them in the wind. The shells are lighter and blow away. You should get about 1.6 kg of nibs from 2 kg of beans. Store the nibs in a dry container. Roasting is the most important step for flavor. Practice with small batches until you can judge by smell and sound.

2. Winnowing and Grinding Cocoa Nibs with a Mortar and Pestle

After roasting and cooling, you need to separate the nibs from the shells. This is winnowing. Put the roasted beans in a clean cloth and rub them vigorously. The shells will flake off. Then, use a basket or a bowl. Toss the beans in the air. The wind blows the light shells away while the heavier nibs fall back. Do this on a windy day or use a fan. Repeat until you have mostly nibs. For 2 kg of beans, you should get about 1.6 kg of nibs. Now grind the nibs. Use a large mortar and pestle, preferably made of wood or stone. Put 1 cup of nibs in the mortar. Grind with a twisting motion, pressing down hard. After 10 minutes, the nibs turn into a thick paste. Keep grinding for 20-30 minutes. The paste becomes smoother and releases cocoa butter. You want a smooth, shiny liquid. This is called cocoa mass or cocoa liquor. It will be warm from friction. If it gets too thick, add a little cocoa butter or warm it slightly. For a finer grind, you can pass the mass through a sieve and grind again. This manual grinding is hard work but gives you pure chocolate. You can also use a meat grinder or a grain mill if available, but the mortar and pestle is traditional and effective. Store the cocoa mass in a cool place.

3. Refining Cocoa Mass with Sugar and Cocoa Butter

Now you turn cocoa mass into chocolate. You need sugar and cocoa butter. For a dark chocolate, use 60% cocoa mass, 30% sugar, and 10% cocoa butter. For milk chocolate, use 40% cocoa mass, 40% sugar, 15% milk powder, and 5% cocoa butter. Weigh your ingredients with a scale. Sugar should be fine and dry. If your sugar is coarse, grind it in a mortar first. Warm the cocoa mass gently in a pot over low heat until it is liquid, about 45-50C. Do not overheat. Add the sugar and cocoa butter. Stir well with a wooden spoon. The mixture will be gritty. Now refine it. You can use a mortar and pestle again, or a stone grinder. Work in small batches. Grind the mixture for 30-60 minutes. The particles should become very small, less than 30 microns. You can test by rubbing a drop between your fingers; it should feel smooth, not gritty. This step is called refining. It takes time and effort. If you have a mechanical grinder, use it. Otherwise, grind in a mortar, then pass through a fine sieve and grind again. The chocolate will become thicker as you refine. Keep it warm but not hot. After refining, you have a smooth paste. Now it is ready for conching. Refining is crucial for texture. Poorly refined chocolate feels sandy. Aim for a smooth, glossy paste.

4. Conching Chocolate by Hand for Smooth Texture

Conching removes bitterness and makes chocolate smooth. After refining, the chocolate is still gritty and acidic. Conching means stirring and aerating the chocolate for a long time. Traditionally, this is done by hand with a wooden spoon or a stone roller. Put your refined chocolate in a warm bowl or a stone metate. Keep it at 45-50C. Stir continuously with a wooden spoon. Every 10 minutes, scrape the sides and fold the chocolate over. This exposes it to air. Do this for 6-12 hours. You can do it in shifts. The longer you conch, the smoother and milder the chocolate. For a small batch of 1 kg, conch for at least 6 hours. If you have a mechanical mixer, use it on low speed. But hand conching is possible. You will notice the chocolate becomes less acidic and more shiny. It will also thicken slightly. If it gets too thick, add a little warm cocoa butter. After conching, the chocolate is ready for tempering. Conching is tiring but essential for good quality. You can conch overnight while doing other tasks. Some makers conch for 24 hours or more. For a tropical climate, conching also helps reduce moisture. Keep the area clean and dry. The final chocolate should taste smooth, not bitter or gritty.

5. Tempering and Pouring Chocolate into Bars

Tempering gives chocolate a snap and shine. It involves heating and cooling to specific temperatures. You need a thermometer. Chop your conched chocolate into small pieces. Put two-thirds in a bowl over warm water (not boiling). Heat to 45-50C, stirring until melted. Remove from heat. Add the remaining one-third of chocolate. Stir until melted. This cools the chocolate. Keep stirring and check the temperature. For dark chocolate, cool to 27-28C. For milk chocolate, cool to 26-27C. Then, warm gently back to 31-32C for dark, or 29-30C for milk. This is the working temperature. If you don't have a thermometer, test by dipping a knife tip in the chocolate. It should set in 5 minutes at room temperature (around 25C). Now pour the chocolate into molds. Use silicone molds or clean plastic trays. Fill each mold and tap gently to remove air bubbles. Scrape off excess with a spatula. Let set at room temperature, not in the fridge. In tropical heat, you may need a cool box with ice packs. It should set in 20-30 minutes. If it doesn't set, the temper was off. You can re-melt and try again. Once set, pop the bars out. They should be glossy and snap when broken. Store in a cool, dry place.

6. Curing and Storing Chocolate in Tropical Conditions

After making bars, you need to cure and store them properly. Curing means letting the chocolate rest for a few days to develop flavor. Wrap each bar tightly in foil or plastic. Put them in an airtight container. Keep at 18-20C for 5-7 days. In tropical climates, this is challenging. Use a cool box with ice packs, or a cellar. Avoid temperature swings. If it's too hot, chocolate can bloom. Bloom is when cocoa butter rises to the surface, making white streaks. It's safe to eat but looks bad. To prevent bloom, store at a steady temperature below 25C. Humidity should be below 60%. If humidity is high, add a desiccant like silica gel to the container. Do not refrigerate unless you wrap very tightly; condensation can ruin chocolate. For long-term storage, keep in a dark, cool place. Properly stored chocolate lasts 6-12 months. If you see bloom, you can re-temper and re-mold. For selling, package in clean wrappers with labels. In Africa, many makers use local materials like banana leaves or paper. But for longer shelf life, use foil and plastic. Always handle with clean, dry hands. Curing improves flavor and texture. Taste after 5 days; it should be smoother and less harsh. Store away from strong smells like spices or onions.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Chocolate Making from Bean to Bar (Africa Context)

This practical reference is designed for African agribusinesses, cooperatives, and small-scale processors who want to move from selling raw cocoa beans to capturing higher margins through bean-to-bar chocolate production. It focuses on low-capital, locally adapted methodsclay ovens, mortar and pestle, hand conching, and tropical storagewhile highlighting business-critical control points. Whether you are in Ghana, Cte dIvoire, Nigeria, Cameroon, or Uganda, the principles are the same: control the process, protect the quality, and sell a finished product that commands premium prices in local and export markets.

Step 1: Roasting Cocoa Beans in a Clay Oven or Pan

Roasting develops flavor precursors and loosens nibs from shells. In Africa, clay ovens or heavy pans over firewood/charcoal are common. Target 110140C for 2040 minutes, stirring every 23 minutes. Light roast preserves fruity notes (good for premium single-origin bars); dark roast gives robust, chocolatey flavor (better for milk chocolate or blending).

Business tip: Record roast time and temperature for every batch. Consistency is what allows you to sell to hotels, supermarkets, and export buyers. A $10 thermometer saves thousands in rejected batches.

Step 2: Winnowing and Grinding Cocoa Nibs with a Mortar and Pestle

After roasting, cool beans and crack them. Winnowing separates light shells from heavier nibs. Traditional methods: pour cracked beans between two bowls in a breeze, or use a woven basket. In a mortar and pestle, grind nibs into a paste (cocoa mass). This is labor-intensive but requires almost no capital.

Yield data: 100 kg roasted beans yield roughly 7080 kg nibs. The 2030 kg shell waste can be sold as animal feed or compostturning waste into revenue.

Step 3: Refining Cocoa Mass with Sugar and Cocoa Butter

Refining reduces particle size and blends ingredients. For dark chocolate: 70% cocoa mass, 25% sugar, 5% cocoa butter. For milk chocolate: 40% cocoa mass, 40% sugar, 15% milk powder, 5% cocoa butter. Grind in mortar or stone mill until smooth (particles <30 microns).

Business tip: Source sugar and milk powder locally to reduce costs. In Ghana, Nigeria, and Kenya, local sugar is available; milk powder can be sourced from dairy cooperatives.

Step 4: Conching Chocolate by Hand for Smooth Texture

Conching removes volatile acids and develops smooth mouthfeel. Hand conching: stir and knead chocolate mass in a warm bowl (4555C) for 624 hours. Traditional stone conches (like those used in Mexico and India) can be adapted locally. Longer conching = smoother texture = higher price.

Business tip: Conching is where you differentiate. A 12-hour conch can command 2030% higher price than a 4-hour conch. Market your conching time as a quality signal.

Step 5: Tempering and Pouring Chocolate into Bars

Tempering stabilizes cocoa butter crystals. Method: heat chocolate to 45C, cool to 27C, reheat to 3132C. Pour into molds, tap to remove bubbles, cool at 1822C. In tropical climates, work in the coolest part of the day (early morning) or use a cool box.

Business tip: Tempered chocolate has snap, shine, and longer shelf life. Untempered chocolate blooms (white streaks) and sells at discount. Train one staff member as tempering lead.

Step 6: Curing and Storing Chocolate in Tropical Conditions

Cure bars at 1822C and 60% relative humidity for 27 days. In tropical Africa, this often means a cool room, cellar, or insulated box with a dehumidifier. Store away from sunlight, strong odors, and pests. Properly cured and stored chocolate lasts 612 months.

Business tip: Invest in a small cool room or repurposed refrigerator with temperature controller. This single investment protects your entire inventory and enables export-grade quality.

SAFETY WARNING: Fire, Heat, and Hygiene

Roasting and conching involve open flames, hot surfaces, and prolonged heat exposure. Always use firewood/charcoal in well-ventilated areas to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. Wear heat-resistant gloves and aprons. Never leave hot oil or chocolate unattended. Ensure all equipment is food-grade and cleaned daily to prevent salmonella and moldespecially in humid tropical conditions. Workers handling cocoa should wash hands and wear clean clothing. If you feel dizzy near the fire, move to fresh air immediately and seek help.

Quick Reference Checklist (68 Points)

  1. Source quality beans: Buy from farmers who ferment and dry properly. Poor beans = poor chocolate, no matter the process.
  2. Calibrate your roast: Use a thermometer. Record time and temperature for every batch. Aim for consistency, not guesswork.
  3. Winnow efficiently: Separate shells from nibs completely. Shells cause gritty texture and off-flavors.
  4. Grind fine: Target particle size below 30 microns. Rub a small sample between fingersshould feel smooth, not sandy.
  5. Conch long enough: Minimum 6 hours, ideally 1224 hours. This is your quality differentiator.
  6. Temper precisely: 45C 27C 3132C. Work in cool conditions. Test on a knife tipshould set shiny and snap.
  7. Cure and store right: 1822C, 60% humidity, away from light and odors. Use a cool box or controlled room.
  8. Track your numbers: Record yields, costs, and selling prices. Know your profit per batch. Reinvest in better equipment and training.
