1. Understanding Layer Breeds for African Production Systems

When you decide to produce eggs for sale, the single most important decision you will make is which breed of layer to keep. In Africa, this decision is shaped by climate, feed availability, disease pressure, market demand, and the amount of capital you can afford to tie up in a flock. A breed that performs beautifully in a temperate climate may collapse under the heat and humidity of the Sahel or the coastal tropics. A breed that lays large brown eggs may not be accepted in a market that prefers white eggs. A breed that needs high-protein commercial feed may not be profitable where maize and soy prices swing wildly. This lesson gives you a practical framework for choosing layer breeds that fit African conditions and your business model. We will cover the major categories of layers, the specific breeds and hybrids commonly available on the continent, and the trade-offs you must weigh before you buy a single chick. The first category is the commercial hybrid layer. These are birds produced by primary breeding companies and sold through hatcheries and distributors. They are not a breed in the strict sense; they are crosses that combine high egg numbers, feed efficiency, and predictable performance. The most common commercial layers in Africa include the Lohmann Brown, the Hy-Line Brown and Hy-Line W-36, the ISA Brown, the Bovans Brown, the Shaver Brown, and the Dekalb White. These birds typically start laying between 18 and 20 weeks of age, peak at over 90 percent production, and produce 300 to 330 eggs in a 72 to 80 week cycle. They convert feed efficiently, often needing only 1.8 to 2.2 kilograms of feed per kilogram of eggs. Their weakness is that they are expensive to buy, they need consistent high-quality feed, and they are less tolerant of neglect than indigenous birds. If you have reliable feed supply, good housing, and a market that pays a premium for uniform brown or white eggs, a commercial hybrid is usually the most profitable choice. The second category is the dual-purpose breed. These birds are kept for both eggs and meat. Examples include the Rhode Island Red, the New Hampshire Red, the Plymouth Rock, the Sussex, and the Australorp. They lay fewer eggs than commercial hybrids, often 180 to 250 per year, and they eat more feed per egg produced. However, they are hardier, they can forage, and they have better resale value as meat. In many African villages and peri-urban farms, dual-purpose birds are the backbone of smallholder poultry because they tolerate scavenging, they brood their own chicks, and they do not require the same level of purchased inputs. If your market is local and your feed is partly home-grown or scavenged, dual-purpose breeds can be a smart business choice. The third category is the indigenous or local breed. Every African region has its own landraces: the Nigerian indigenous chicken, the Ethiopian Horro, the Kenyan Kienyeji, the South African Venda and Naked Neck, the Egyptian Baladi, and many others. These birds are not high producers. A local hen may lay 40 to 100 small eggs per year. But they are extremely hardy, they resist many local diseases better than exotic birds, they hatch and raise their own chicks, and they thrive on kitchen waste, insects, and crop residues. Their eggs and meat often command a premium in urban markets because consumers believe they taste better and are more natural. For a business, indigenous birds are best used in a free-range or semi-intensive system where input costs are very low and the product is sold as a specialty. The fourth category is the improved indigenous or crossbreed. These are birds developed by crossing local breeds with improved exotic breeds to combine hardiness with better production. Examples include the Kuroiler, the Sasso, the Rainbow Rooster, the Improved Kienyeji, and the Fulani ecotype improvements. These birds typically lay 150 to 220 eggs per year, they forage well, they tolerate heat and poor housing better than commercial hybrids, and they can be raised with less purchased feed. They are increasingly popular across East and West Africa because they offer a middle path between the low output of local birds and the high input demands of commercial hybrids. When choosing a breed, you must match it to your production system. Intensive systems with cages or deep litter and full commercial feed favor commercial hybrids. Semi-intensive systems with outdoor access and partial feed favor improved indigenous or dual-purpose birds. Free-range and scavenging systems favor indigenous and improved indigenous birds. You must also match the breed to your market. Brown eggs are preferred in many East and Southern African markets, while white eggs dominate in parts of West Africa and North Africa. Some buyers pay more for large eggs; others buy by number and do not care about size. You must match the breed to your climate. Commercial hybrids are more sensitive to heat stress. In hot regions, you need breeds with good heat tolerance, and you need housing with good ventilation, shade, and cool water. You must match the breed to your feed budget. A commercial hybrid that lays 320 eggs will only do so if it eats a balanced ration with 16 to 18 percent protein, adequate calcium, and proper energy. If you cannot supply that, the bird will lay fewer eggs and you will have paid a high price for genetic potential you cannot use. You must match the breed to your disease environment. Newcastle disease, Gumboro, fowl typhoid, coccidiosis, and Marek's disease are common in many African countries. Some breeds and hybrids have better resistance or better response to vaccination. You must match the breed to your labor and management capacity. Commercial hybrids need precise feeding, lighting, and health management. If you cannot provide that, a hardier breed may give you a better return even if its peak production is lower. Finally, you must match the breed to your financing. Commercial hybrids cost more per chick, they need more feed, and they need better housing. If you are starting with limited capital, a dual-purpose or improved indigenous flock may allow you to start smaller and grow gradually. A practical way to decide is to write down your answers to these questions: What is my market and what does it pay for? What feed can I reliably buy or produce? What is my climate and housing? What diseases are common in my area? How much can I invest per bird? How much labor can I provide? Then compare the breeds available from local hatcheries and suppliers against those answers. Do not buy a breed because a neighbor has it or because a salesperson praises it. Buy it because it fits your business. Remember that genetics only set the ceiling. Nutrition, housing, health, and management determine how close you get to that ceiling. A good breed in poor conditions will fail. A modest breed in excellent conditions can be profitable. In the next lessons we will look at how to select pullets, how to evaluate chick quality, how to plan your flock replacement, and how to manage the rearing period so that your chosen breed reaches its potential. Your breed choice is the foundation. Get it right and everything else becomes easier.

2. Selecting Healthy Pullets: What to Look For and What to Avoid

Pullets are young female chickens that have not yet started laying, usually between day-old and about 18 weeks of age. The quality of the pullets you buy or raise determines how well your flock will lay and how much profit you will make. A poor pullet may never lay well, may die early, or may bring disease into your farm. A good pullet will grow evenly, start laying on time, and produce consistently for a full cycle. This lesson teaches you how to select healthy pullets using practical, low-cost methods that work in African conditions. We will cover selection at day-old, at point of lay, and at intermediate ages, plus the records and questions you must ask any supplier. Start with day-old chicks. If you are buying day-old pullets, you must inspect them before you pay. A healthy day-old chick is alert, active, and stands upright. It has bright, open eyes. Its navel is completely closed and clean, with no yolk sac protruding and no redness or discharge. Its legs are straight and firm. Its down is dry, fluffy, and uniform in color for the breed. It should weigh what the breed standard says, usually 35 to 45 grams depending on breed and hatchery. It should not be panting, huddled, or spread out limply. It should not have a pasted vent, which is when dried droppings block the opening. It should not have a crooked beak, twisted neck, or splayed legs. Reject any chick that is small, weak, or deformed. Also reject chicks from a batch where many are dead or dying. Ask the hatchery for the hatch date, the vaccination record, and the parent flock age. Chicks from young or old parent flocks may be weaker. Chicks that have been transported long distances without proper temperature control may be stressed. If you can, visit the hatchery or supplier before buying. Look at the brooders, the water, the feed, and the general cleanliness. A dirty hatchery is a source of disease. Now consider pullets at 6 to 12 weeks. At this stage you are looking for uniform growth. A good batch of pullets is even in size. If some birds are much larger or much smaller, there is a problem with feed access, disease, or genetics. Weigh a sample of birds and compare to the breed standard. For commercial hybrids, the target body weight at 6 weeks is often around 450 to 500 grams, at 12 weeks around 1.0 to 1.2 kilograms, depending on breed. If the average is below target, the birds have been underfed or sick. If the average is above target, they may be too fat, which can reduce later laying. Look at the birds individually. A healthy pullet has bright eyes, clean feathers, and a red, well-developed comb and wattles if it is close to laying age. Its legs are clean and straight. Its vent is moist and pink, not dry or crusted. It is active and curious. It eats and drinks normally. It does not have discharge from the eyes or nostrils. It does not cough or sneeze. It does not have diarrhea. It does not have external parasites like lice, mites, or fleas. It does not have scaly leg mites, which show as rough, crusty scales on the legs. It does not have a swollen head or face. It does not have paralysis or twisted neck. It does not have a bloated abdomen. Reject any bird with these signs. Also look at the flock as a whole. Is there any bird that is isolated, hunched, or ruffled? Is there any bird that is panting when others are not? Is there any bird with a pale comb or shrunken comb? These can be signs of disease or poor management. Check the litter. Wet, caked litter smells of ammonia and can cause respiratory disease. Check the feeders and drinkers. Are they clean and adequate in number? Are there birds that cannot reach feed or water? Check the ventilation. Is there a draft? Is it too hot? Is it too stuffy? All of these affect pullet quality. Now consider pullets at point of lay, usually 16 to 18 weeks. At this stage you want birds that are ready to lay soon. A good point-of-lay pullet has a well-developed, red comb and wattles. Its vent is large, moist, and pink. Its pelvic bones are wide apart, usually two to three fingers. Its abdomen is soft and full. Its eyes are bright. Its feathers are smooth. It should weigh close to the breed standard, often 1.4 to 1.8 kilograms for commercial brown layers. It should be vaccinated according to the schedule used in your area. Ask for the vaccination record. Common vaccines include Newcastle disease, Gumboro, fowl pox, fowl typhoid, and Marek's disease. If the supplier cannot show a vaccination record, do not buy. You are buying risk. Also ask about the feed the pullets have been eating. If they have been on a different feed, you need to transition them slowly to your feed to avoid stress. Ask about the lighting program. If they have been on long days, they may start laying too early. If they have been on short days, they may be delayed. Ask about the history of disease on the farm. If there has been an outbreak of Newcastle or Gumboro, the birds may be carriers or may have been exposed. Ask about mortality. A good pullet rearer will have less than 5 percent mortality from day-old to point of lay. If mortality is higher, there is a problem. Ask about the source of the chicks. If they come from a reputable hatchery, they are more likely to be healthy. If they come from a market or a trader, they may be mixed, unvaccinated, and stressed. When you buy pullets, always buy from a reputable supplier. It is better to pay more for healthy pullets than to save money on poor ones. Poor pullets will cost you more in feed, medicine, and lost production. They may also infect your other birds. If you are raising your own pullets, you must select them yourself. Remove any bird that is small, weak, deformed, or sick. Remove any bird that is not growing at the same rate as the others. Remove any bird that has a pasted vent, diarrhea, or respiratory signs. Remove any bird that is being bullied. Keep records of body weight, feed intake, mortality, and vaccination. These records will tell you if your pullets are on track. If they are not, you can correct the problem before it is too late. A simple way to select is to use a scoring system. Score each bird on alertness, body weight, feathering, comb development, and vent condition. Keep the top birds. Cull the bottom birds. This is especially important if you are keeping a dual-purpose or indigenous flock where variation is high. Remember that pullet selection is not a one-time event. You should monitor your pullets throughout the rearing period. Weigh them every two weeks. Check their feed and water consumption. Watch for signs of disease. Adjust feed and management as needed. The goal is to have a uniform flock of healthy pullets that start laying at the right age and reach peak production quickly. A uniform flock is easier to manage, easier to feed, and more profitable. In the next lesson we will look at how to plan your flock replacement so that you always have pullets coming into lay when your old hens are going out of production. This is the key to a steady egg supply and a steady income.

3. Chick Quality Assessment and Hatchery Selection in Africa

The quality of the chicks you start with determines the quality of the flock you end with. In Africa, where hatcheries vary widely in standards and where transport can be long and hot, chick quality assessment is a critical business skill. This lesson teaches you how to assess chick quality at the hatchery and on arrival at your farm, how to choose a hatchery, and how to handle chicks so that they get off to a strong start. We will focus on practical steps that any farmer or farm manager can use, even without a laboratory. Start with hatchery selection. Not all hatcheries are equal. A good hatchery has healthy parent stock, a clean and well-managed incubator, a reliable vaccination program, and a good record-keeping system. Visit the hatchery if you can. Look at the parent flock. Are the birds healthy and well-fed? Are they housed at the right density? Are they vaccinated? Look at the incubator room. Is it clean? Is the temperature and humidity controlled? Are the eggs clean and properly stored? Look at the chick handling area. Are the chicks kept warm and dry? Are they vaccinated before sale? Ask for the hatch date, the parent flock age, and the vaccination record. Ask about the hatch rate. A good hatch rate is above 80 percent of fertile eggs. Ask about the chick yield, which is the percentage of eggs set that produce saleable chicks. A good yield is above 75 percent. Ask about the mortality in the first week. A good hatchery will have less than 1 percent mortality in the first week. If the hatchery cannot answer these questions, look elsewhere. Also consider the distance from the hatchery to your farm. Long transport is stressful for chicks. If the hatchery is far, make sure the chicks are transported in a ventilated vehicle with adequate space and temperature control. Avoid transporting chicks in the heat of the day. Transport them early in the morning or late in the evening. Make sure they have access to water and feed immediately on arrival. Now consider chick quality assessment at the hatchery. You should inspect the chicks before you accept them. A good chick is alert and active. It stands upright and moves around. It has bright, open eyes. Its navel is completely closed and clean. Its legs are straight and firm. Its down is dry and fluffy. It is uniform in size and color with the rest of the batch. It weighs what the breed standard says. It does not have a pasted vent. It does not have a crooked beak or twisted neck. It does not have splayed legs. It does not have a swollen head or watery eyes. It does not have a distended abdomen. It does not have a bad smell. Reject any chick that is weak, small, deformed, or sick. Also reject any batch where more than 2 percent of chicks are dead or dying. If you are buying a large number of chicks, sample them. Open a few boxes and inspect the chicks inside. Do not just look at the top of the box. Look at the bottom. Look at the corners. Look for chicks that are huddled or spread out. Huddling means they are cold. Spreading out means they are hot. Both are signs of poor transport or poor brooding. Now consider chick quality assessment on arrival at your farm. The transport itself can reduce chick quality. Chicks that have been in transit for many hours may be dehydrated, chilled, or overheated. On arrival, weigh a sample of chicks. Compare to the day-old weight standard. If they have lost more than 10 percent of their body weight, they are dehydrated. Check their vents. If they are pasted, they may have been too hot or too cold. Check their navels. If they are not closed, they may have been infected. Check their activity. If they are lethargic, they may be sick or stressed. Check their distribution in the box. If they are all huddled in one corner, there may be a draft. If they are all spread out, it may be too hot. Check the temperature and humidity in the transport vehicle. If it was too hot or too cold, the chicks may be stressed. Once you have assessed the chicks, put them in the brooder immediately. The brooder should be preheated to 32 to 35 degrees Celsius for the first week. Provide clean water with glucose or electrolytes for the first few hours. Provide feed within the first 24 hours. Make sure the chicks can reach the feed and water. Use bright lighting for the first few days so they can find feed and water. Observe the chicks. If they are huddling under the heat source, they are cold. If they are panting and spreading away from the heat source, they are hot. If they are evenly spread, the temperature is right. If they are chirping loudly and constantly, they are uncomfortable. If they are quiet and content, they are comfortable. Good chick quality is not just about the chick itself. It is also about the management you provide in the first 48 hours. Chicks that are stressed during transport can recover if you provide good brooding. Chicks that are healthy can become sick if you provide poor brooding. So your job is to minimize stress and maximize comfort. A practical tool for chick quality assessment is the Tona score or a similar scoring system. You score each chick on activity, down appearance, navel condition, leg condition, and size. A score of 100 means perfect. A score below 80 means poor quality. You can use this to compare batches from different hatcheries or different transport conditions. Keep records of chick quality, mortality, and growth. Over time, you will see which hatcheries and which transport methods give you the best results. Another important point is vaccination. Chicks should be vaccinated at the hatchery for Marek's disease, and sometimes for Newcastle disease and infectious bronchitis. Ask for the vaccination record. If the chicks are not vaccinated, you must vaccinate them yourself according to your local schedule. Do not skip vaccination. Disease outbreaks can wipe out your flock and your investment. Also consider the breed and strain. Different breeds have different chick sizes and different growth rates. Make sure you know what you are buying. If you are buying commercial layers, you should get a breed that is suited to your market and climate. If you are buying dual-purpose or indigenous chicks, you should expect more variation in size and growth. Finally, remember that chick quality is a business issue. Poor quality chicks mean higher mortality, higher feed costs, and lower egg production. Good quality chicks mean lower mortality, better feed conversion, and higher egg production. The extra cost of good chicks is almost always worth it. In the next lesson we will look at how to plan your flock replacement so that you have a steady supply of pullets and a steady income from eggs.

4. Planning Flock Replacement and Pullet Rearing Schedules

A layer flock is not a permanent asset. Hens lay well for a limited time, usually from about 18 to 20 weeks of age until about 72 to 80 weeks of age, depending on breed and management. After that, egg production declines, egg quality drops, and feed costs stay the same or rise. To keep a profitable egg business, you must plan flock replacement. This means you must decide when to bring in new pullets, how many to bring in, and how to manage the transition so that you always have eggs to sell. This lesson teaches you how to plan flock replacement and how to rear pullets on a schedule that matches your market and your cash flow. Start with the laying cycle. A commercial layer typically starts laying at 18 to 20 weeks. Production rises quickly to a peak of 90 to 95 percent at about 28 to 30 weeks. It stays high for several weeks, then gradually declines. By 72 weeks, production may be 65 to 70 percent. By 80 weeks, it may be 60 percent or lower. The eggs also get larger as the hen ages, but the shell quality may decline. The feed conversion gets worse because the hen is eating more feed for each egg. At some point, the cost of feed for the old hen is greater than the value of the eggs she produces. That is the point at which you should cull her. The exact point depends on feed prices, egg prices, and the cost of new pullets. As a rule of thumb, many commercial farms replace the flock at 72 to 80 weeks. Some replace at 60 to 65 weeks if egg prices are low or feed prices are high. Some keep the flock longer if egg prices are high and the hens are still laying well. You must calculate your own break-even point. To do this, you need records of feed intake, egg production, mortality, and prices. If you do not have records, start keeping them now. Now consider the replacement schedule. If you want a steady supply of eggs, you need a steady supply of pullets. You cannot wait until your old hens stop laying and then order pullets. You must order pullets months in advance. A good rule is to order pullets so that they start laying just before your old flock is culled. This means you need to plan 18 to 20 weeks ahead for the pullets to grow, plus the time it takes to order and receive them. If you are buying day-old chicks, you need to plan about 18 to 20 weeks before they start laying. If you are buying point-of-lay pullets, you need to plan a few weeks ahead. A common system is to have two or three flocks of different ages on the farm at the same time. This is called a multi-age system. For example, you might have a flock of 20-week-old pullets just starting to lay, a flock of 50-week-old hens in full production, and a flock of 80-week-old hens about to be culled. This gives you a steady supply of eggs and a steady cash flow. It also spreads your risk. If one flock gets sick, the others may still be healthy. However, a multi-age system also increases the risk of disease spread. You must have strict biosecurity. You must keep different age groups separate. You must clean and disinfect between flocks. You must control traffic and visitors. If you cannot manage biosecurity, an all-in-all-out system may be better. In an all-in-all-out system, you bring in all your pullets at once, keep them for one laying cycle, and then cull them all at once. Then you clean and disinfect the house and bring in a new flock. This is simpler and reduces disease risk, but it means you have no eggs for a period between flocks. You must plan for that gap. You can use the gap to clean and repair the house, to market your spent hens, and to prepare for the next flock. You can also time the gap to coincide with a period of low egg prices or a holiday. Now consider how many pullets to bring in. This depends on your market. If you have a contract to supply a certain number of eggs per week, you need enough hens to meet that contract. If you sell in a local market, you need enough hens to meet demand. You also need to account for mortality and culling. A good rule is to add 5 to 10 percent to your target number to account for losses. For example, if you want 1,000 laying hens, you might start with 1,050 to 1,100 pullets. You also need to consider housing space. Do not overcrowd. Overcrowding reduces production and increases disease. Follow the breed standard for space. For commercial layers in deep litter, allow about 6 to 8 birds per square meter. In cages, allow about 18 to 20 birds per square meter, depending on cage size and local regulations. For free-range, allow more space. Now consider pullet rearing. If you raise your own pullets, you must manage them carefully. The rearing period is from day-old to about 18 weeks. During this time, you must provide the right feed, the right lighting, the right vaccination, and the right space. You must also monitor body weight and uniformity. A good pullet rearing program produces pullets that are the right weight, the right size, and the right age at first egg. If you buy pullets, you must still manage them well after arrival. You must transition them to your feed, your water, and your housing. You must vaccinate them if needed. You must monitor them for disease. You must keep records. A practical schedule for a 1,000-bird flock might look like this. Week 0: order day-old chicks or point-of-lay pullets. Week 1 to 6: brooding and early rearing. Week 7 to 12: growing. Week 13 to 18: pre-lay. Week 18 to 20: transfer to laying house. Week 20 to 72: laying. Week 72 to 80: culling and replacement. You can adjust this schedule to fit your breed, your climate, and your market. You should also plan your cash flow. Pullets cost money. Feed costs money. Vaccines cost money. You need to have enough cash to cover these costs before the eggs start coming in. If you are buying point-of-lay pullets, you pay a higher price but you get eggs sooner. If you are buying day-old chicks, you pay less but you wait longer. You must decide which is better for your cash flow. A good practice is to keep a simple budget. List your costs: chicks, feed, housing, labor, vaccines, medicine, transport, and marketing. List your income: egg sales, manure sales, spent hen sales. Calculate your profit. Update your budget every month. This will help you decide when to replace your flock and how many pullets to keep. Finally, remember that flock replacement is not just about numbers. It is about quality. A new flock of healthy, well-reared pullets will lay better than an old flock of tired hens. Do not keep old hens just because you are attached to them. Treat them as a business asset. When they stop paying, replace them. In the next lesson we will look at how to manage the transition from pullet to laying hen so that your birds start laying on time and reach peak production quickly.

5. Managing the Transition from Pullet to Laying Hen

The transition from pullet to laying hen is a critical period in the life of your flock. If you manage it well, your birds will start laying on time, reach peak production quickly, and produce large, strong-shelled eggs. If you manage it poorly, your birds will be delayed, they will lay small eggs, they will have more health problems, and your profits will suffer. This lesson teaches you how to manage the transition, also called the pre-lay and early lay period, in African conditions. We will cover housing, feeding, lighting, health, and record-keeping. Start with the timing. Most commercial layers start laying at 18 to 20 weeks of age. Some start at 17 weeks, some at 21 weeks. The exact age depends on breed, body weight, lighting, and nutrition. Your goal is to have the birds reach the right body weight and the right body condition before they start laying. If they start laying too early, they may lay small eggs and they may not reach peak production. If they start too late, you lose income. To manage this, you must monitor body weight. Weigh a sample of birds every week from 14 weeks onward. Compare to the breed standard. If they are underweight, increase feed. If they are overweight, reduce feed. Do not let them get too fat, because fat hens lay poorly. Now consider housing. The pullets should be moved to the laying house at least one to two weeks before they start laying. This gives them time to adjust to the new environment. The laying house should be clean, dry, and well-ventilated. It should have enough space. It should have enough feeders and drinkers. It should have nest boxes if you are using a deep litter or free-range system. The nest boxes should be clean, dark, and comfortable. They should be placed in a quiet area. They should have clean bedding. They should be at least 30 centimeters above the floor if possible. Provide one nest box for every five hens. If you are using cages, make sure the cages are clean and in good repair. Make sure the birds have enough space. Overcrowding causes stress, which reduces production and increases disease. Now consider feeding. The transition from grower feed to layer feed is important. Grower feed has about 15 to 16 percent protein. Layer feed has about 16 to 18 percent protein and higher calcium, usually 3.5 to 4.5 percent. The calcium is needed for eggshell formation. If you switch to layer feed too early, the excess calcium can damage the kidneys. If you switch too late, the hens may not have enough calcium to lay strong-shelled eggs. A good practice is to switch to a pre-lay feed at about 16 to 17 weeks, or to mix grower and layer feed gradually. By the time the hens are laying, they should be on full layer feed. Provide feed free-choice, but do not let the feeders run empty. Hens need constant access to feed. Also provide clean water at all times. Water is essential for egg production. A hen needs about 200 to 300 milliliters of water per day, depending on temperature. In hot weather, she needs more. Make sure the water is cool and clean. Add electrolytes or vitamin C during heat stress. Now consider lighting. Light controls egg production. A hen needs about 14 to 16 hours of light per day to lay well. If she gets less, she may not lay. If she gets more, she may lay too early or too much. In Africa, natural daylight varies by season and location. Near the equator, daylight is about 12 hours all year. In southern Africa, daylight can be 10 hours in winter and 14 hours in summer. You must manage lighting to give your hens the right amount. The general rule is to never decrease light during the laying period. You can increase light, but you cannot decrease it. If you are using artificial light, use a timer. Provide light in the early morning and late evening. Use one 40-watt bulb per 20 square meters of floor space. Keep the bulbs clean. Replace burned-out bulbs. <p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-size:14px;color:#4a0000;">Chlorine used for drinking-water sanitation must be dosed so that free residual chlorine at the drinker is <strong>13 ppm</strong> never above 5 ppm, which reduces intake and damages gut lining. Do not mix chlorine with acids, or with vaccines, in the same water line; chlorine destroys live vaccines. If you medicate through water, observe the full withdrawal period on the label before selling eggs residue-positive eggs can close your market access permanently. Store all chemicals, disinfectants and drugs in a locked cabinet away from feed and out of reach of children. Never use agricultural pesticides, formalin or industrial disinfectants in drinkers or feed stores. If you suspect a notifiable disease (e.g. highly pathogenic avian influenza), stop all sales and movement and contact your veterinary authority immediately do not self-treat.</p>

<h3>Daily Stockmanship Benchmarks</h3>
<p>Walk the house at the same times each day: early morning when lights come on, midday at peak heat, and evening at last feed. Record the same six numbers every day. Trends matter more than single readings a 2% drop in intake across three days is an early warning you can act on before mortality rises.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Feed intake:</strong> weigh a sample of feed for 100 birds, or read the feed bin gauge daily. A drop of more than 5 g/bird/day needs investigation within 24 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Water intake:</strong> read the water meter at the same time daily. Water falls before feed intake falls it is the earliest indicator of trouble.</li>
<li><strong>Mortality:</strong> remove and count dead birds twice daily. Normal is under 0.05% per day (under 1 bird per 1,000 per day) in a healthy laying flock. Anything above that for two consecutive days requires a post-mortem.</li>
<li><strong>Egg production:</strong> count eggs and calculate % lay against live hens, not against housed birds. Record floor eggs separately.</li>
<li><strong>Egg quality:</strong> check for cracks, dirty shells, thin shells, and soft-shelled eggs. A sudden rise in cracks often points to calcium, heat stress or handling problems.</li>
<li><strong>Environment:</strong> check temperature, ammonia smell (sharp smell = above 20 ppm), litter condition, and drinker height. Litter that balls in the hand is too wet.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Weigh or meter feed and water daily; record both in the same notebook.</li>
<li>Walk the flock three times daily morning, midday heat, evening and handle birds.</li>
<li>Collect and count dead birds twice daily; investigate any rise above 0.05%/day.</li>
<li>Check calcium source and particle size monthly; limestone grit should be visible in the feed.</li>
<li>Test drinking water for TDS, pH and coliforms at least once per year, and after any flooding.</li>
<li>Clean and flush drinker lines weekly; check chlorine residual at the far end of the line.</li>
<li>Record % lay, egg weight, cracks and floor eggs daily; review weekly against breed standard.</li>
<li>Keep a locked chemical store and observe all drug withdrawal periods before egg sales.</li>
</ol>
