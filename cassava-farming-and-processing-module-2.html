1. Morphology and Anatomy of Cassava Plant

The cassava plant (Manihot esculenta) is a woody shrub that grows 13 meters tall. It has a fibrous root system that produces 510 storage roots per plant, each weighing 13 kg at maturity. The stem is woody, with nodes and internodes; buds on nodes can sprout into new branches. Leaves are palmate, with 57 lobes, and grow on petioles 1030 cm long. Cassava is monoecious, producing male and female flowers on the same plant, usually in terminal inflorescences. The fruit is a capsule containing three seeds.

The storage roots are the main economic part. They are formed from adventitious roots that swell due to starch accumulation. The root has a central vascular strand and a thick parenchyma (starchy flesh) covered by a thin periderm (bark). Roots can be white, cream, or yellow-fleshed. The leaves are rich in protein (2025% dry matter) and vitamins, often used as vegetables.

Understanding morphology helps farmers identify healthy plants. For example, a good variety should have strong stems, many leaves, and roots that are easy to pull. When selecting planting materials, choose stems from healthy, high-yielding plants. Stems should be 23 cm thick, with at least 57 nodes. Avoid stems with disease symptoms like mosaic or brown streaks.

In Africa, common tools for cassava farming include hoes, machetes, and hand trowels. For root harvest, a digging stick or fork is used to lift roots without breaking them. Proper identification of plant parts helps in pest and disease management. For instance, cassava mosaic disease causes leaf distortion and yellowing, while cassava brown streak disease causes brown streaks in roots. Early detection through morphology is key.

In summary, the cassava plant has distinct parts: leaves for photosynthesis, stems for propagation, and roots for food. Knowing these parts helps you manage the crop better, from planting to harvest. Always select healthy stems and inspect plants regularly for signs of trouble.

2. Growth Stages and Development Cycle

Cassava takes 612 months to mature, depending on variety and climate. The growth cycle has four stages: sprouting, vegetative growth, root bulking, and maturity. Sprouting occurs 12 weeks after planting when buds on the stem produce shoots. During this stage, keep the soil moist but not waterlogged. Weeds compete for nutrients, so weed 34 weeks after planting.

Vegetative growth lasts 24 months. The plant develops leaves and stems rapidly. This is when you apply fertilizer if needed: a balanced NPK (e.g., 15-15-15) at 200400 kg per hectare. In Africa, many farmers use organic manure like poultry litter or compost. Ensure spacing of 1 m x 1 m to allow air and sunlight. During this stage, watch for pests like cassava green mite and diseases like mosaic.

Root bulking starts around 35 months and continues until maturity. Roots swell and accumulate starch. This is the most critical stage for yield. Water stress now reduces root size. If you have irrigation, water weekly. Otherwise, plant at the start of the rainy season. Avoid disturbing the soil around roots. Some farmers do earthing up to cover exposed roots.

Maturity is reached when leaves turn yellow and drop, and roots reach full size. For sweet varieties, harvest at 69 months; for bitter types, 912 months. To test maturity, cut a root: if the flesh is firm and starch oozes, it's ready. Harvest with a fork or machete, lifting roots carefully. Yields range from 1020 tons per hectare for local varieties, up to 3040 tons for improved ones.

After harvest, roots spoil within 23 days. Process immediately or store in the ground for up to 2 weeks. The cycle repeats if you keep stems for next planting. Understanding these stages helps you time weeding, fertilizing, and harvesting for maximum profit.

3. Classification of Cassava Varieties: Sweet vs Bitter

Cassava varieties are classified as sweet or bitter based on cyanogenic glucoside content, which produces cyanide when eaten raw. Sweet varieties have less than 50 mg of cyanide per kg of fresh root, while bitter varieties have 50200 mg or more. This difference matters for food safety and processing.

Sweet cassava roots can be boiled, fried, or eaten raw after peeling. They are popular for home consumption and local markets. Examples in Africa include 'TME 419' (sweet) and 'TMS 30572' (sweet). Bitter varieties, like 'TME 7' and many local landraces, require processing to remove cyanide. Processing methods include soaking, grating, fermenting, and drying. Bitter cassava is often used for gari, fufu, and starch because the processing also improves taste and shelf life.

How to tell them apart? Sweet varieties often have softer roots and are ready earlier (69 months). Bitter types have harder roots, mature later (912 months), and are more resistant to pests and diseases. However, cyanide levels vary with environment; a sweet variety grown in drought may become bitter. Always test by peeling and boiling a small piece; if it tastes bitter, process it.

For farmers, choose based on end-use. If you want to sell fresh roots for boiling, plant sweet varieties. If you process into gari or flour, bitter varieties give better texture and yield. In Africa, many farmers grow both: sweet for home, bitter for market. Always source planting materials from certified suppliers to ensure true-to-type.

Safety note: Never eat raw bitter cassava. Even sweet types should be peeled and cooked. Soaking in water for 24 hours reduces cyanide. Fermentation for 35 days is effective. Teach your family and workers about the danger of cyanide poisoning, which causes dizziness, vomiting, and even death.

4. Improved Varieties and Their Characteristics

Improved cassava varieties are bred for higher yield, disease resistance, and better processing quality. In Africa, organizations like IITA and national research institutes have released many varieties. Examples include 'TMS 30572' (yield 3040 t/ha, resistant to mosaic), 'TME 419' (yield 2535 t/ha, sweet), and 'TMS 91/02324' (yield 3545 t/ha, tolerant to brown streak). These varieties mature in 912 months.

Key characteristics to look for: high root yield, high starch content (2530%), resistance to cassava mosaic disease (CMD) and cassava brown streak disease (CBSD), and tolerance to drought. Some improved varieties also have good cooking quality. For example, 'TMS 30572' has white roots, is sweet, and good for boiling. 'TMS 91/02324' is bitter, ideal for gari.

Where to get them? Contact local agricultural extension officers, research stations, or certified seed producers. In Nigeria, the National Root Crops Research Institute (NRCRI) distributes stems. In Ghana, the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) does. Always buy certified stems, not from unknown sources, to avoid disease.

Planting improved varieties requires good management: use healthy stems, plant at 1 m x 1 m, weed regularly, and apply fertilizer if soil is poor. Yields can double compared to local varieties. However, improved varieties may need more inputs. For example, without fertilizer, yield may drop to 15 t/ha. So, combine with organic manure.

Adopting improved varieties increases income. A farmer with 1 hectare can harvest 30 tons, selling at $50/ton = $1,500. With local varieties, 10 tons = $500. The extra $1,000 is worth the investment. Start with a small plot and expand as you learn.

5. Selection Criteria for Variety Based on End-Use

Choosing the right cassava variety depends on what you want to do with the roots. End-uses include fresh consumption, gari, fufu, starch, and animal feed. Each use has specific requirements.

For fresh consumption (boiling, frying), select sweet varieties with low cyanide, good taste, and soft texture. Examples: 'TME 419', 'TMS 30572'. Harvest at 69 months. Roots should be easy to peel and not too fibrous.

For gari and fufu, bitter varieties with high starch and good fermentation qualities are best. 'TMS 91/02324', 'TME 7'. These have high cyanide, so processing is necessary. They yield more and have better texture. Harvest at 912 months.

For starch production, choose varieties with high starch content (2830%) and white flesh. 'TMS 30572' works well. Starch extraction requires grating, washing, and drying. Bitter varieties are preferred because cyanide is removed during washing.

For animal feed, use any variety, but high-yield ones reduce cost. Dry the roots and grind into meal. Sweet varieties can be fed raw in small amounts.

Other criteria: disease resistance, drought tolerance, and market demand. If you sell to a gari processor, ask what variety they prefer. If you have poor soil, choose drought-tolerant types. Always test a small plot before large-scale planting. Record yields and processing results. Over time, you'll know which variety suits your farm and market.

6. Seed System and Certified Planting Materials

Cassava is propagated from stem cuttings, not seeds. A good seed system ensures farmers get healthy, disease-free stems of the right variety. In Africa, the informal system (farmer-to-farmer) dominates, but it spreads diseases. Certified planting materials are produced by trained seed growers under inspection.

To get certified stems, contact your local agricultural office or research institute. They can link you to registered seed producers. In Nigeria, the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC) certifies. In Kenya, the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS). Always ask for a certificate of analysis showing freedom from viruses.

How to select stems? Choose stems from plants 812 months old that are healthy and high-yielding. Stems should be 23 cm thick, with 57 nodes. Avoid stems with mosaic, brown streaks, or insect damage. Cut stems into 2030 cm lengths, each with at least 3 nodes. Treat with fungicide if available.

Storage: Keep stems in a cool, shaded place. You can store them vertically in soil or horizontally in a pit. Use within 23 days for best sprouting. If you must store longer, dip in wax or keep in a nursery.

Planting: Insert cuttings at 45 angle, with 23 nodes underground. Space 1 m x 1 m. Water immediately. Certified stems cost more (e.g., $0.10$0.20 per cutting) but give higher yields and fewer diseases. Over time, you can multiply your own stems by planting a seed plot. Always rogue out diseased plants. A good seed system is the foundation of a profitable cassava farm.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cassava Botany and Varieties Field & Business Decision Guide

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) is Africa's most reliable food-security and cash crop, feeding over 800 million people globally and providing raw material for starch, ethanol, flour, chips, and livestock feed. For agribusiness operators, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to two decisions made before planting: which variety and which planting material. This practical reference consolidates the botany, growth cycle, variety classification, and seed-system knowledge you need to make those decisions with commercial confidence.

1. Morphology and Anatomy What Matters Commercially

Cassava is a woody perennial shrub grown as an annual. Every part has an economic or agronomic function:

  • Leaves: palmate, 57 lobes, produced continuously; they drive photosynthesis and are also a protein source (2025% crude protein) for livestock feed.
  • Stem: the primary planting material. Nodes and internodes determine cutting quality. Stem yield is a secondary revenue stream for seed entrepreneurs.
  • Storage roots: the commercial product 510 per plant, 2040% starch on fresh weight. Root shape, peel colour, and cyanogenic content vary by variety.
  • Fibrous roots: absorb nutrients; poor root architecture signals poor soil or variety mismatch.

2. Growth Stages and Development Cycle

Cassava follows a predictable cycle that determines harvest timing and cash-flow planning:

  1. Sprouting (04 weeks): buds emerge from cuttings; soil moisture is critical.
  2. Vegetative growth (14 months): canopy develops; weed control is essential.
  3. Root bulking (49 months): storage roots enlarge; starch accumulates. This is the peak nutrient-demand phase.
  4. Maturity (918 months): roots reach maximum starch. Early varieties mature at 912 months; late varieties at 1518 months.

For processors, the bulking-to-maturity window is the operational planning horizon: contract farmers must stagger planting so roots arrive at the factory continuously.

3. Classification: Sweet vs Bitter Varieties

The key commercial distinction is cyanogenic glucoside content (measured as HCN). Sweet varieties (<50 mg HCN/kg fresh root) can be boiled and eaten directly; bitter varieties (>100 mg/kg) require processing (fermentation, pressing, roasting) to detoxify but often give higher starch yields and better pest resistance.

SAFETY WARNING Cyanide Poisoning Risk

Bitter cassava varieties contain high levels of cyanogenic glucosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). Never eat raw or inadequately processed bitter cassava. Improper processing (short fermentation, insufficient pressing, under-roasting) has caused fatal cyanide poisoning outbreaks in Africa. Always: (1) confirm HCN level before selecting a variety for food use; (2) follow validated processing protocols (2472 hour fermentation, thorough pressing, roasting at 60C); (3) test processed products where possible. Factory workers handling large volumes of bitter roots must use protective equipment and ensure adequate ventilation.

6. Seed System and Certified Planting Materials

Cassava is propagated vegetatively through stem cuttings (stakes). This makes the seed system both an opportunity and a risk:

  • Informal system: farmer-to-farmer exchange cheap but spreads Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD) and Cassava Brown Streak Disease (CBSD).
  • Formal system: certified seed entrepreneurs produce disease-free, true-to-type cuttings from tissue-culture or clean foundation stocks.
  • Certification: in Nigeria, NAQS and NASC regulate; in Uganda, MAAIF; in Kenya, KEPHIS. Look for certified labels and lot numbers.
  • Business opportunity: certified cassava seed is a high-margin enterprise a hectare of stem multiplication can supply 1020 hectares of commercial planting.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the end-use buyer before choosing a variety.
  2. Match variety to HCN level (sweet for food, bitter for starch/ethanol).
  3. Verify maturity period against your cash-flow and factory supply schedule.
  4. Source only certified, disease-free cuttings from registered seed entrepreneurs.
  5. Check for CMD and CBSD resistance in the variety's trait profile.
  6. Confirm starch content and dry matter meet processor specifications.
  7. Plan staggered planting to supply roots continuously for 918 months.
  8. Train all handlers on cyanide-safe processing before handling bitter varieties.

Variety selection, certified seed, and cyanide-safe processing are the three levers that determine whether your cassava enterprise delivers consistent margins or chronic losses. Choose the variety your buyer needs, source it from a certified seed producer, and process it safely. Everything else follows.
