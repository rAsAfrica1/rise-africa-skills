1. Visual Inspection Criteria

Visual inspection is your first and cheapest quality check. Before you power up any board, look at it under good light. You need a bright, white light sourcea 10W LED desk lamp works well. Hold the board at a 45-degree angle to catch reflections. Start with the big things: are all components present? Check against your bill of materials (BOM). Missing parts are common when you hand-place. Next, look for solder bridgestiny balls of solder connecting two pads that shouldn't touch. A bridge between pins on an IC can destroy the chip when you power up. Also check for cold solder joints: they look dull and grainy, not shiny and smooth. In Africa, humidity and dust can cause problems, so inspect for corrosion or dirt on pads. Use a magnifying glass (10x) to see fine details. For through-hole parts, check that the lead fills the hole and solder wets both sides. For surface-mount, look for tombstoningwhere one end of a component lifts off the pad. Also check for component orientation: diodes and electrolytic capacitors have polarity. A reversed electrolytic capacitor can explode. Look for scratches on the board that expose copper. Any exposed copper will oxidize and cause intermittent faults. Write down every defect you find on a paper log with the board serial number. A simple rule: if you wouldn't buy it, don't ship it. In a small workshop in Nairobi or Lagos, you might inspect 20 boards a day. Train your eyes to spot these issues in under 30 seconds per board. Remember, fixing a defect now takes 2 minutes; fixing it after the customer complains takes 2 hours and loses trust.

2. Using Magnification and Lighting

Your naked eye can only see details down to about 0.1 mm. Solder joints on modern boards are often 0.2 mm wide, so you need magnification. A simple 10x jeweler's loupe costs about $5 and clips to your eye or a stand. For serious work, get a stereo microscope with 10x to 40x zoom. In Africa, you can find used stereo microscopes on Jumia or from university surplus for $100$300. Lighting is just as important. Use a ring light or two flexible gooseneck LEDsone from the left, one from the rightto eliminate shadows. Shadows hide cracks and cold joints. For inspecting solder joints, use a technique called 'coaxial lighting': light comes from the same direction you look. This makes shiny solder look bright and dull solder look dark. A cheap alternative is a white LED flashlight held at a low angle. Also use a dark backgroundblack mat or black paperto increase contrast. For very fine pitch ICs (0.5 mm pitch), you need at least 20x magnification. Clean your lens with a microfiber cloth; dust looks like defects. Set up a dedicated inspection station: a table with a boom stand for the microscope, adjustable LED lights, and a grounded wrist strap to prevent ESD damage. In hot, humid climates, add a small fan to prevent fogging on lenses. Practice by inspecting a known-good board first to calibrate your eyes. Then inspect a board with intentional defects (create them yourself) to learn what to look for. With practice, you can inspect a 100-component board in 5 minutes using 10x magnification and proper lighting. Without it, you'll miss 3050% of defects.

3. IPC-A-610 Standards Overview

IPC-A-610 is the global bible for accepting or rejecting electronic assemblies. It's written by IPC, a trade association, and it defines what a 'good' solder joint looks like. You don't need to memorize the whole book (it's 400+ pages). But you need to know three classes. Class 1 is for general productstoys, cheap radios. Class 2 is for dedicated serviceTVs, computers, phones. Class 3 is for high-reliabilitymedical, aerospace, military. Most African workshops build Class 2 products. For Class 2, a solder joint must be smooth, shiny, and have a concave fillet (like a tiny volcano). The solder must wet the pad and the lead. No cracks, no voids, no bridges. For through-hole, solder should fill the hole and form a fillet on both sides. For surface-mount, the fillet must be visible on all sides of the component. IPC-A-610 gives specific numbers: for example, a solder fillet on a chip resistor should be at least 0.2 mm high. A void in a BGA solder ball is acceptable if it's less than 25% of the ball's area. You can download a free summary of IPC-A-610 from the IPC website. Print the key pages and laminate them for your workbench. Train your inspectors using the 'accept' and 'reject' photos in the standard. In Africa, many buyers (like those exporting to Europe) require IPC-A-610 Class 2 compliance. If you can show you inspect to this standard, you can charge 2030% more. Start by buying a used copy of IPC-A-610 from eBay or Amazon; it costs $50$100. Or find a local training center that offers IPC certificationit's worth the investment.

4. Solder Joint Inspection

Solder joint inspection is the heart of quality control. A good solder joint looks like a shiny, smooth fillet that curves like a tiny volcano from the pad up to the component lead. It should be concave, not convex. A convex blob means too much solder. A dull, grainy joint means cold solderthe iron wasn't hot enough or you moved the joint while it cooled. Cold joints crack easily and cause intermittent faults. Inspect each joint under 10x magnification. Use a pointed probe (like a dental pick) to gently push on the joint. If it moves, it's a cold jointreject it. For through-hole joints, check that solder wicks up the lead and fills the hole. A good joint has a fillet on both the top and bottom of the board. For surface-mount, check for 'tombstoning'where one end of a chip component lifts off the pad. That happens when one pad heats faster than the other. Also check for 'solder balls'tiny spheres of solder that can roll around and cause shorts. Remove them with tweezers. For fine-pitch ICs, use a microscope. Look for bridges between pins. A bridge is any solder connecting two adjacent pins. Even a hair-thin bridge will short the circuit. Use solder wick and flux to remove bridges. For BGA (ball grid array) packages, you can't see the joints. You need X-ray inspection, which is expensive. Instead, do a 'dye and pry' test on a sample: inject dye under the chip, let it cure, then pry it off. The dye shows where solder didn't wet. In a small African workshop, focus on visual inspection of visible joints. Keep a log of every defect type you find. After 100 boards, you'll see patternsmaybe your iron temperature is too low, or your flux is expired. Fix the process, not just the board.

5. Documenting Inspection Results

If you don't write it down, it didn't happen. Documentation is your proof of quality and your tool for improvement. Start with a simple paper log. For each board, record: serial number, date, inspector name, and a list of defects found. Use a checklist with common defect codes: 'SB' for solder bridge, 'CJ' for cold joint, 'MP' for missing part, 'WP' for wrong part, 'TO' for tombstone, 'SC' for scratch. For each defect, note the reference designator (e.g., R12, C5) and the location. Take a photo with your phonemost phones have a 12 MP camera that's good enough for documentation. Store photos in a folder named by board serial number. At the end of each day, count the defects. Calculate your first-pass yield: (number of boards with zero defects / total boards inspected) 100. A good target for hand assembly is 90% first-pass yield. If you're below 80%, you have a process problem. Use a simple spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel) to track defects over time. Make a Pareto chart: which defect type is most common? If 60% of your defects are solder bridges, train your assemblers on drag soldering or use a smaller solder tip. For customer orders, include a 'Certificate of Conformance' that states you inspected to IPC-A-610 Class 2 and list the serial numbers. This builds trust. In Africa, where buyers may be overseas, clear documentation can be the difference between getting repeat orders or not. Keep records for at least 2 years. If a customer reports a failure, you can trace it back to the exact board and inspector. That's how you learn and improve.

6. Quality Control Tools and Techniques

Quality control is not just inspectionit's a system. Start with the 'Plan-Do-Check-Act' cycle. Plan: set a quality target (e.g., 95% first-pass yield). Do: assemble boards. Check: inspect and record defects. Act: fix the root cause. For root cause analysis, use the '5 Whys' method. Ask 'why' five times. Example: Why did the board fail? A solder bridge. Why? Too much solder. Why? The iron tip was too large. Why? We didn't have the right tip. Why? We never ordered it. So the fix is to order the correct tip. Other tools: a 'fishbone diagram' (also called Ishikawa) helps you brainstorm causes. Draw a spine and branches for Man, Machine, Material, Method, Measurement, and Environment. For electronics, common causes are: wrong iron temperature (Method), expired flux (Material), humid air (Environment), or uncalibrated multimeter (Measurement). Use a multimeter to check for shorts and opens. A cheap $20 multimeter is fine. For continuity, set it to beep mode. For voltage, set to DC 20V. For current, use a bench power supply with current limiting. Also use a 'test jig'a simple board with pogo pins that connects to your board's test points. This speeds up functional testing. In Africa, you can make a test jig from a wooden block, pogo pins (from AliExpress), and a multimeter. Finally, do 'sampling inspection'inspect every 5th board fully, but do a quick visual on all. That balances speed and quality. Train everyone on these tools. Post the Pareto chart on the wall. Celebrate when you hit your target. Quality is a habit, not an accident.

<h2>Practical Reference: Inspection and Quality Control for Electronics Assembly</h2>

<p>In African electronics assembly operations from contract manufacturers in Lagos and Nairobi to solar inverter assemblers in Accra and Johannesburg inspection is not a bureaucratic afterthought. It is the gate that decides whether a batch ships, gets reworked, or becomes warranty cost. For an SME running a small SMT line or hand-assembly bench, a disciplined inspection routine protects cash flow, customer relationships, and the ability to win the next order.</p>

<p>This lesson is a practical reference you can pin to the wall of your inspection station. It covers what to look for, how to look for it, the standards that define acceptable work, and the tools that make quality measurable rather than a matter of opinion.</p>

<h3>Visual Inspection Criteria</h3>
<p>Visual inspection is the first and cheapest line of defence. At a minimum, every assembled board should pass a structured check covering: correct component orientation and value, no missing or extra parts, no tombstoning or billboarding, no solder bridges, no disturbed joints, clean flux residue within limits, and no physical damage to pads, traces, or connectors. In African workshops where reflow ovens may be older or hand-soldering is common, the two most frequent defects are insufficient solder and cold joints both visible under good light.</p>

<p>Inspection must be done against a written standard, not against the inspector's mood. The reference for most commercial work is IPC-A-610, which defines three classes: Class 1 (general electronics), Class 2 (dedicated service, the default for most African contract work), and Class 3 (high reliability medical, aerospace, automotive safety). Know which class your customer is paying for before you start rejecting boards.</p>

<h3>Using Magnification and Lighting</h3>
<p>Human visual acuity limits reliable inspection to features around 0.2 mm without aid. For 0402 chip components, fine-pitch QFN, or BGA perimeter inspection, you need 3 to 10 magnification. A stereo microscope with 1040 is the workshop standard; a 5 loupe is acceptable for coarse through-hole work but not for fine pitch.</p>

<p>Lighting matters as much as magnification. Use diffuse, shadow-free illumination from at least two angles. Ring lights are common but can hide fillet shape; a combination of ring and oblique light reveals both presence and profile of solder. Avoid single-point LED torches they create glare that hides cold joints. In dusty or humid environments (common in coastal West Africa and East African industrial parks), keep lenses capped and clean them with lint-free wipes before each shift.</p>

<h3>IPC-A-610 Standards Overview</h3>
<p>IPC-A-610 is the most widely used workmanship standard in the world and is the de facto requirement for most export-bound African electronics. It is a visual acceptance document it tells you what a joint, a component placement, or a PCB surface should look like, not how to achieve it. Key sections cover soldering (through-hole and SMT), component mounting, mechanical assembly, and cleanliness.</p>

<p>For an SME, the practical move is to buy or borrow the current revision, extract the criteria relevant to your product family, and build a one-page acceptance sheet per product. This turns a 400-page standard into a usable shop-floor tool. Training on IPC-A-610 is available through IPC-certified centres, and some African technical universities and industry associations now offer short courses.</p>

<h3>Solder Joint Inspection</h3>
<p>A good solder joint has a smooth, shiny, concave fillet that wets both the pad and the lead. The wetting angle should be low solder should flow onto the surfaces, not ball up. For lead-free solder (now standard in most export work due to RoHS), the surface is slightly matte but should still be smooth and continuous; do not confuse this with a cold joint, which is rough, dull, and lumpy.</p>

<p>Common defects and their causes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insufficient solder:</strong> too little paste, poor stencil aperture, or short reflow. Reject if the fillet does not cover the pad.</li>
<li><strong>Excess solder:</strong> too much paste or poor pad design. Acceptable only if it does not hide the lead or bridge to a neighbour.</li>
<li><strong>Cold joint:</strong> insufficient heat or contaminated surface. Dull, grainy, and unreliable always reject.</li>
<li><strong>Bridging:</strong> excess paste or poor stencil release. Always reject; it causes shorts.</li>
<li><strong>Disturbed joint:</strong> movement during cooling. Frosty appearance reject.</li>
<li><strong>Lifted pad or cracked fillet:</strong> thermal stress or handling damage reject and investigate process.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Documenting Inspection Results</h3>
<p>Documentation is what converts inspection from a cost into a business asset. Without records, you cannot prove conformance to a customer, cannot trace a field failure back to a batch, and cannot see which defect keeps recurring. At minimum, record: date, batch/lot number, inspector name, product and revision, inspection class, defects found (type, location, quantity), disposition (accept, rework, scrap), and rework actions taken.</p>

<p>Photograph every defect. A phone camera with a cheap macro clip-on lens is enough. Store images by batch number. When a customer complains six months later, you can show exactly what left your facility and what your process was. This is also the evidence base for continuous improvement Pareto your defect types monthly.</p>

<h3>Quality Control Tools and Techniques</h3>
<p>Inspection alone does not create quality it detects it. The tools below are the ones that make sense for an African SME without a full metrology lab.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>First Article Inspection (FAI):</strong> fully inspect the first board of a new batch or after any process change. Do not release the batch until FAI passes.</li>
<li><strong>In-Process Checks:</strong> inspect after paste printing (paste height/volume), after placement (orientation), and after reflow (joints). Catching defects early is far cheaper than at final test.</li>
<li><strong>Statistical Process Control (SPC):</strong> track defect rate per batch on a simple chart. If it trends up, stop and investigate before shipping.</li>
<li><strong>Pareto Analysis:</strong> rank defect types monthly. Attack the top one or two this is where most of your rework cost sits.</li>
<li><strong>5 Whys / Root Cause:</strong> for every recurring defect, ask why five times. Fix the process, not the symptom.</li>
<li><strong>Calibration:</strong> verify your microscope, light source, and any measurement tools on a schedule. An uncalibrated tool gives false confidence.</li>
<li><strong>Supplier Quality:</strong> inspect incoming PCBs and components. A bad batch of boards from a cheap supplier will cost you more in rework than you saved.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING Lead and Flux Exposure</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0">Solder wire, paste, and flux fumes are hazardous. Lead-containing solder (still used in some repair and legacy work) can be absorbed through skin and ingested. Flux fumes irritate airways. In African workshops with limited ventilation, this is a real occupational health risk.</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 0">
<li>Always solder and inspect under fume extraction or with an open window and fan moving air away from your face.</li>
<li>Wear nitrile gloves when handling solder paste and leaded wire. Do not eat, drink, or smoke at the bench.</li>
<li>Wash hands with soap after every shift, before eating or touching your face.</li>
<li>Store solder paste and flux in sealed containers; label them clearly.</li>
<li>Dispose of solder dross and used wipes as hazardous waste, not in general rubbish.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm the inspection class (1, 2, or 3) and the customer's acceptance standard before starting.</li>
<li>Verify magnification (310 minimum for SMT) and diffuse, shadow-free lighting.</li>
<li>Check component orientation, value, and placement against the BOM and assembly drawing.</li>
<li>Inspect every solder joint for concave fillet, wetting, and absence of bridges or cold joints.</li>
<li>Check for physical damage: lifted pads, cracked traces, damaged connectors, contamination.</li>
<li>Record every defect with type, location, quantity, and a photograph.</li>
<li>Disposition each board: accept, rework with instructions, or scrap. Never ship an unresolved reject.</li>
<li>Review defect trends weekly; act on the top cause before it repeats in the next batch.</li>
</ol>
