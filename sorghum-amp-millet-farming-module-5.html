1. Understanding NPK needs for sorghum and millet

Sorghum and millet need three main nutrients from the soil: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Think of them as building blocks: nitrogen makes leaves green and tall, phosphorus builds strong roots and grain, and potassium helps the plant resist drought and disease. A 50 kg bag of compound fertilizer like NPK 15-15-15 contains 15% of each nutrient. Your crop also needs smaller amounts of calcium, magnesium, sulphur, and zinc, but these are usually present in African soils unless the land is very sandy or worn out.

A good sorghum crop yielding 2 tonnes of grain per hectare removes about 40 kg of nitrogen, 20 kg of phosphorus (P2O5), and 30 kg of potassium (K2O) from the soil. Millet yields less, around 1 tonne per hectare, and removes about 20 kg N, 10 kg P2O5, and 15 kg K2O. If you harvest and remove the stalks for fuel or animal feed, you take away even more nutrients. That is why continuous farming without fertilizer slowly starves the soil.

In most African smallholder fields, nitrogen is the biggest limiting nutrient. You will see stunted, yellowing plants. Phosphorus is often second, causing purple stems and poor root growth. Potassium deficiency is less common but appears as yellow or brown edges on older leaves. A simple soil test from your local agricultural extension office can tell you exactly what your soil lacks. If you cannot test, a general recommendation for sorghum and millet is to apply 100-150 kg per hectare of NPK 15-15-15 at planting, and then top-dress with 50-100 kg per hectare of urea or ammonium sulphate.

Remember that fertilizer is not a substitute for organic matter. Manure, compost, and crop residues improve soil structure, hold water, and feed beneficial microbes. Combining organic and mineral fertilizers gives the best results. Start with what you can afford: even a few handfuls of NPK per planting hole can double your yield on poor soil. The key is to apply nutrients where the roots can reach them, not broadcast them where weeds steal them.

2. When and how to apply basal fertilizer at planting

Basal fertilizer is the fertilizer you put in the soil at planting time. It gives young seedlings a strong start before their roots can search for nutrients. For sorghum and millet, the best basal fertilizer is a compound containing nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, such as NPK 15-15-15 or NPK 20-10-10. Phosphorus is especially critical at this stage because it drives root development. Without it, seedlings stay weak and never catch up.

Apply basal fertilizer at the rate of 100 to 150 kg per hectare. If you plant in rows 75 cm apart and 25 cm between hills, that is about 53,000 planting hills per hectare. So 100 kg per hectare means roughly 2 grams per hill, which is about a bottle cap full. You can measure with a small tin or a calibrated cup. Do not guess wildly; too much fertilizer can burn seeds and kill them. Too little wastes money.

The method matters. Open your planting hole or furrow, drop the seed, then place the fertilizer 5 to 8 cm to the side and 5 cm below the seed. Never let fertilizer touch the seed directly. If you mix fertilizer with the seed, it will absorb moisture, release salts, and destroy germination. In dry areas, you can apply fertilizer in a band along the row and cover it with soil before planting. This is called banding. It concentrates nutrients near the roots and reduces loss to weeds.

Timing is also important. Apply basal fertilizer when the soil is moist, either just before planting or at planting. If the soil is bone dry, wait for rain. Fertilizer applied to dry soil may not dissolve and can be blown away by wind. In sandy soils, which are common in the Sahel and parts of East Africa, nutrients leach quickly. Split your basal application: put half at planting and the other half two weeks later. In heavier clay soils, one application at planting is usually enough.

Finally, combine basal fertilizer with organic matter. Put a handful of well-rotted manure or compost in each planting hole before you add the mineral fertilizer. The organic matter holds water and nutrients, and it feeds soil life. This simple practice can increase your yield by 30% or more compared to mineral fertilizer alone.

3. Top dressing with urea or ammonium sulphate at knee height

Top dressing means adding nitrogen fertilizer after the crop is already growing. For sorghum and millet, the best time is when the plants reach knee height, about 30 to 45 cm tall, usually 3 to 5 weeks after planting. At this stage, the crop is rapidly growing leaves and stems, and it needs a big supply of nitrogen. Without top dressing, you may get a tall plant with a small head or no head at all.

The two common top-dressing fertilizers are urea (46-0-0) and ammonium sulphate (21-0-0). Urea is stronger, so you need less of it. Ammonium sulphate also contains sulphur, which is good for sandy soils. Apply 50 to 100 kg per hectare of urea, or 100 to 200 kg per hectare of ammonium sulphate. For a small plot, 50 kg of urea per hectare is about 1 gram per planting hill. Use a bottle cap or a small spoon to measure accurately.

How you apply matters. Broadcast the fertilizer evenly between the rows, not on the leaves. If granules land on the leaves, they can burn them, especially when the leaves are wet. The best method is to place the fertilizer in a band 10 cm from the plant base and then cover it lightly with soil. This is called side-dressing. Covering prevents nitrogen from escaping into the air as ammonia gas, especially with urea. If you broadcast and do not cover, you can lose up to 30% of the nitrogen.

Timing with rain is critical. Apply top dressing just before a light rain or when the soil is already moist. Do not apply on dry soil or before a heavy downpour, because the fertilizer will wash away. If there is no rain, you can dissolve urea in water and pour it near the roots, but this is more work. After applying, if you have a hoe, lightly cultivate the soil to mix the fertilizer in and kill weeds. Weeds compete for the same nitrogen, so a clean field gives you more from your fertilizer.

One warning: do not top-dress too late. If you apply urea after the plant has started flowering, it will mostly go to leaves and stalks, not grain. You will get a lot of green matter but poor yield. Knee height is the sweet spot. For a small farm, you can also split the top dressing: half at knee height and half one week later. This reduces the risk of losing nitrogen to a single heavy rain.

4. Making and using liquid organic fertilizers (manure tea)

Liquid organic fertilizer, often called manure tea, is a cheap and powerful way to feed your sorghum and millet. It is made by soaking animal manure or compost in water, which extracts nutrients into a liquid that plants can absorb quickly. You can make it on your farm with materials you already have. It is especially useful for top dressing when you cannot afford urea, or when you want to give your crop a quick boost.

To make manure tea, you need a container (a drum, bucket, or old water tank), fresh animal manure (cow, goat, chicken, or rabbit), and water. A good recipe: fill a sack or porous bag with 10 kg of manure. Tie the sack and place it in a 200-litre drum of water. Let it soak for 7 to 14 days, stirring daily. The water will turn dark brown. Remove the sack and use the liquid. If you want a stronger tea, use more manure or let it soak longer. For chicken manure, use less because it is very strong and can burn plants. A ratio of 1 part manure to 10 parts water is safe for most crops.

You can also add wood ash, which provides potassium and some phosphorus. Add a handful of ash per 20 litres of tea. Do not add synthetic fertilizers to manure tea, because they can kill the beneficial microbes. The tea is alive with bacteria and fungi that help your soil. Use it within a few days of straining, because it can spoil and smell bad. If you store it, keep it in a shaded area and cover it to prevent mosquito breeding.

How to use manure tea: dilute it with water if it is very dark, about 1 part tea to 2 parts water. Apply it to the soil around the base of your sorghum or millet plants, about 250 ml (one cup) per plant. You can also spray it on the leaves as a foliar feed, using a knapsack sprayer. Foliar feeding gives a quick response, but do it early in the morning or late in the evening to avoid leaf burn from the sun. Apply every two weeks, starting at knee height and continuing until flowering.

Manure tea is not a complete replacement for mineral fertilizer, because it has lower nutrient concentrations. But it improves soil health, adds organic matter, and supplies micronutrients. Many farmers in Africa use it successfully on vegetables and cereals. It is also a way to use animal manure that would otherwise be wasted. With one cow, you can produce enough manure to make tea for half a hectare of sorghum. The cost is almost zero, and the benefit is real.

5. Recognizing nutrient deficiency symptoms in leaves

Your sorghum and millet leaves tell you what the plant needs. If you learn to read the signs, you can fix problems before they cut your yield. The most common deficiencies are nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients like zinc and iron. Each shows up differently. Start by looking at the older leaves at the bottom of the plant, because mobile nutrients like nitrogen and potassium move to new growth when the plant is short.

Nitrogen deficiency: older leaves turn pale green, then yellow, starting from the tip and moving along the midrib. The whole plant looks stunted and thin. In sorghum, the lower leaves may dry up and die early. In millet, the leaves become narrow and the plant stays small. If you see this, top-dress with urea or manure tea immediately. A quick test: apply a small amount of nitrogen fertilizer to one patch. If those plants green up within a week, the rest of the field needs nitrogen.

Phosphorus deficiency: leaves and stems turn dark green, sometimes with a purple or reddish tint, especially on the undersides of leaves and on young plants. The plants are stunted, and roots are poorly developed. In sorghum, the purple colour appears on the leaf margins and stems. Phosphorus deficiency is worst in cold, wet soils and in very acidic soils. If you see purple leaves, add basal fertilizer with phosphorus (like NPK 15-15-15) or bone meal. Liming acidic soil also helps release phosphorus.

Potassium deficiency: older leaves show yellow or brown scorching along the edges, and the tips may die. The plant may have weak stalks and lodge easily. In millet, the leaves may have a ragged appearance. Potassium deficiency is more common in sandy soils and after heavy harvests without fertilizer. Wood ash is a good source of potassium. Apply it around the plants or add it to manure tea.

Micronutrient deficiencies: zinc deficiency causes white or yellow bands on both sides of the midrib of young leaves, and the leaves may be shorter than normal. Iron deficiency causes yellowing between the veins of young leaves, while the veins stay green. These are less common but can occur in highly weathered soils. A foliar spray of a micronutrient mix or a manure tea can help. Remember that deficiency symptoms can also be caused by drought, waterlogging, or root damage. Check the soil moisture and root health before you blame nutrients. When in doubt, ask your extension officer or send a leaf sample to a lab.

6. Cost-effective fertilizer micro-dosing for small farms

Fertilizer is expensive, and many smallholder farmers in Africa cannot afford the recommended rates. Micro-dosing is a technique that gives each plant a small, precise amount of fertilizer right at the root zone. It can double or triple yields with just one-quarter to one-half of the usual fertilizer rate. This is perfect for sorghum and millet, which are often grown on poor soils with little rain.

The idea is simple: instead of broadcasting fertilizer over the whole field, you place a small amount in each planting hole or next to each plant. For sorghum and millet, a common micro-dose is 2 to 4 grams of NPK 15-15-15 per planting hill. That is about half a bottle cap or one level teaspoon. For a field with 10,000 plants, you need only 20 to 40 kg of fertilizer. Compare that to the 100-150 kg per hectare for broadcasting. You save money and get better results because the fertilizer is close to the roots, not feeding weeds.

You can micro-dose with both basal and top-dressing fertilizers. At planting, place the micro-dose in the hole, mix it lightly with soil, then drop the seed. Do not let the seed touch the fertilizer directly. For top dressing, use urea or ammonium sulphate at 1 to 2 grams per plant at knee height. Again, place it 5-10 cm from the plant base and cover with soil. You can use a small spoon, a bottle cap, or a specially made measuring cup. Some farmers use a stick with a notch to measure the right amount.

Micro-dosing works best when combined with other good practices: improved seeds, proper spacing, timely weeding, and organic matter. Even a handful of manure in each hole along with the micro-dose gives a big boost. In dry areas, micro-dosing is safer than broadcasting because the fertilizer is concentrated where there is some moisture. It also reduces the risk of burning the crop.

To be cost-effective, calculate your fertilizer needs before you buy. If a 50 kg bag of NPK costs $30 and you micro-dose at 2 grams per hill, that bag will treat 25,000 hills, which is about half a hectare of sorghum at 50,000 hills per hectare. That is a good investment if it gives you an extra 500 kg of grain. Many farmers in the Sahel, such as in Niger and Burkina Faso, have adopted micro-dosing and seen their yields rise from 500 kg per hectare to over 1,500 kg per hectare. Start with a small plot, see the difference, and expand. You do not need to fertilize the whole farm at once. Micro-dosing puts the power in your hands, even with a small budget.

<h2>Practical Reference: Feeding Your Sorghum &amp; Millet for Profit</h2>

<p>Fertilizer is the single largest cash cost in most African cereal budgets often 3050% of variable costs for sorghum and millet. Every kilogram you buy must return more grain than it costs. This Practical Reference pulls together the numbers, timing, rates and low-cost alternatives you need to make fertility decisions that pay, whether you farm one hectare in Kano or fifty hectares in Nakuru.</p>

<p>The guiding principle across sorghum and millet is <strong>balanced, targeted, timely</strong> feeding. These are not maize: they are more drought-tolerant, often grown on poorer soils, and respond strongly to small, well-placed doses of nutrients rather than blanket applications. Get the ratio, the timing and the placement right, and you can lift yields 4080% with modest inputs.</p>

<h3>1. Understanding NPK Needs for Sorghum and Millet</h3>

<p>Sorghum and millet remove roughly <strong>2030 kg N, 812 kg PO and 1525 kg KO per tonne of grain</strong> harvested, plus similar amounts in stover if you remove it. Nitrogen drives tillering, leaf area and grain protein. Phosphorus drives root development and early vigour critical on Africa's weathered, P-deficient soils. Potassium supports stalk strength, drought tolerance and grain filling. Sulphur, zinc and boron matter too, especially on sandy Sahelian soils.</p>

<p>Recommended blanket rates in West and East Africa are typically <strong>4060 kg N/ha, 2030 kg PO/ha and 1520 kg KO/ha</strong> for sorghum, and slightly lower for millet. But blanket recommendations assume uniform soils rarely true on smallholder fields. Soil testing (even a simple colour chart or local lab) or a history of deficiency symptoms should guide your actual rates.</p>

<h3>2. When and How to Apply Basal Fertilizer at Planting</h3>

<p>Basal fertilizer goes down <strong>at planting, in the planting furrow or hole, 35 cm below and beside the seed</strong> never in direct contact with seed, which burns germinating roots. Use a phosphorus-rich blend: DAP (18-46-0), NPK 15-15-15, or the cheaper single super phosphate (SSP) where P is the main constraint. On very sandy soils, add 1015 kg KO/ha as muriate of potash.</p>

<p>Placement matters more than rate on smallholder fields. A band or hill placement puts fertilizer where roots can reach it, reducing fixation and leaching losses. Broadcasting P on weathered African soils is largely wasted.</p>

<h3>3. Top Dressing with Urea or Ammonium Sulphate at Knee Height</h3>

<p>Sorghum and millet need nitrogen most between <strong>tillering and stem elongation roughly 35 weeks after emergence, when plants reach knee height</strong>. This is when tiller number and panicle size are being set. Top dress with <strong>urea (46-0-0) at 5075 kg/ha</strong> or <strong>ammonium sulphate (21-0-0-24S) at 100150 kg/ha</strong>. Ammonium sulphate is preferable on alkaline or sulphur-deficient soils, and its acidifying effect can help on high-pH Sahelian soils.</p>

<p>Apply on moist soil, then cover lightly or weed immediately after. Urea volatilises within days if left on the surface in hot, dry conditions losses can reach 30%. Split applications (half at knee height, half at booting) reduce risk and match crop demand, but only if labour allows.</p>

<h3>4. Making and Using Liquid Organic Fertilizers (Manure Tea)</h3>

<p>Manure tea is the smallholder's cheapest nitrogen and micronutrient source. Fill a 200-litre drum one-third with fresh poultry, cattle or goat manure, top up with water, cover and stir daily. After <strong>714 days</strong> the liquid turns dark brown and smells slightly sweet it is ready. Dilute 1:5 with water before applying; undiluted tea burns leaves.</p>

<p>Apply 200500 ml per plant as a soil drench at knee height, or as a foliar spray (filtered, diluted 1:10) in early morning or evening. Manure tea supplies N, K and micronutrients, improves soil microbial activity, and costs almost nothing where livestock are kept. It is not a full substitute for P pair it with a small basal P dose.</p>

<h3>5. Recognizing Nutrient Deficiency Symptoms in Leaves</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Nitrogen (N):</strong> Uniform pale-green to yellow older leaves, starting from the tip and midrib; stunted plants, few tillers.</li>
<li><strong>Phosphorus (P):</strong> Purple or reddish tinge on older leaf margins and stems; delayed flowering, poor root system.</li>
<li><strong>Potassium (K):</strong> Yellow-brown scorching along leaf margins of older leaves; weak stalks, lodging.</li>
<li><strong>Sulphur (S):</strong> Pale yellowing on <em>young</em> leaves first distinguishes it from N deficiency.</li>
<li><strong>Zinc (Zn):</strong> White or brown bands between veins on young leaves; short internodes ("little leaf").</li>
<li><strong>Iron (Fe):</strong> Interveinal yellowing on young leaves, common on high-pH soils.</li>
</ul>

<p>Scout weekly from emergence. A deficiency caught at knee height can still be corrected with a top dress or foliar spray; caught at flowering, it is a yield loss you cannot recover.</p>

<h3>6. Cost-Effective Fertilizer Micro-Dosing for Small Farms</h3>

<p>Micro-dosing applying <strong>26 g of fertilizer per planting hill</strong> (roughly one bottle cap) was developed by ICRISAT for the Sahel and has consistently delivered 4080% yield gains at 2030% of the cost of blanket application. It works because the small dose is placed exactly where roots can reach it, minimising fixation and leaching.</p>

<p>For sorghum at 60 cm 30 cm spacing (55,000 hills/ha), a 3 g dose of DAP per hill uses only about <strong>165 kg DAP/ha</strong> but you can cut that to 2 g (110 kg/ha) for millet and still see strong returns. Combine micro-dosing with manure tea top dressing and you have a complete, low-cost fertility strategy.</p>

<p><strong>Business check:</strong> At a farm-gate sorghum price of US$0.25/kg and DAP at US$0.55/kg, a 2 g micro-dose costing US$0.0011 per hill needs to return just 4.4 g of extra grain per hill to break even about 2% of a typical 200 g yield. Real-world responses are 3060 g per hill. The economics are compelling.</p>
