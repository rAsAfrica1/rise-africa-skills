1. Module 6 Lesson 1: Stock Control and Inventory Records for Small Shops

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on one of the biggest killers of small shop profits in Africa: poor stock control. Many traders lose money not because they do not sell, but because they do not know what they have, what they sold, what was stolen, or what expired. Good stock control is not a big company idea. It is a simple discipline that any market trader or kiosk owner can learn and use every day. By the end of this lesson you should be able to count your stock, record it, set reorder levels, and reduce losses from theft, damage, and expiry. First, understand why stock control matters. Your stock is your money in another form. If you sell tomatoes, airtime, rice, soap, or phone accessories, every item on your shelf or in your store represents cash you have already spent. When stock is lost, stolen, expired, or spoiled, you lose that cash. When stock sits too long, your money is trapped and cannot be used to buy faster-selling items. When you run out of a fast seller, customers go to another shop and may not come back. Good stock control protects your cash, keeps customers happy, and shows you which products make real profit. Start with a simple stock count. Choose a quiet time, maybe early morning before customers come or late evening after closing. Count every item you have. Do not guess. Write the number on paper or in a notebook. If you sell in units, count units. If you sell in tins, count tins. If you sell in kilograms, weigh or estimate by known package sizes. For small items like sweets, matches, or sachets, count in dozens or in full packets. Record the date, the item name, the quantity counted, and the cost price per unit. If you have a smartphone, you can use a simple spreadsheet or a free stock app. If not, a hardcover exercise book is enough. The important thing is that the record is written, dated, and kept safe. Next, create a stock card for each product. A stock card is a simple page or row that shows: item name, date, quantity received, quantity sold, quantity remaining, and any notes about damage or returns. Every time you buy stock, add it to the card. Every time you sell, subtract it. At the end of the day, the remaining quantity on the card should match what you count on the shelf. If it does not match, investigate. Maybe a sale was not recorded, maybe a customer took goods on credit, maybe there was a breakage, or maybe there was theft. Do not ignore differences. Small unexplained differences become big losses over a month. Set reorder levels for every product. A reorder level is the quantity at which you must buy more stock before you run out. To set it, look at your daily sales. If you sell about 20 bottles of soda per day and it takes 3 days for your supplier to deliver, you need at least 60 bottles to cover those 3 days. Add a safety buffer of 20 to 30 percent for busy days or delays. So your reorder level might be 75 to 80 bottles. When stock falls to that level, order immediately. Do not wait until you have zero. Running out of stock is more expensive than holding a little extra. Also identify your fast movers and slow movers. Fast movers are products that sell every day or almost every day. Keep them well stocked and never let them run out. Slow movers are products that sell once a week or less. Reduce the quantity you buy of slow movers. Consider replacing them with something that sells faster. In many African markets, customers are price sensitive and loyal to shops that always have what they need. Being reliable is a competitive advantage. Now think about theft and shrinkage. Shrinkage is the difference between the stock you should have and the stock you actually have. It comes from theft by customers, theft by workers, supplier short deliveries, damage, expiry, and recording errors. To reduce shrinkage, keep stock in a secure place, limit who has access, count high-value items daily, and do surprise counts. If you have workers, make one person responsible for stock and check their records regularly. Do not accuse without evidence, but do not be careless either. A simple daily stock count of your top ten items can catch problems early. Expiry and damage also destroy profit. For food, drinks, medicine, and cosmetics, always check expiry dates when you buy and when you sell. Use the first-in-first-out rule: sell the oldest stock first. Arrange shelves so older items are at the front and newer items at the back. For perishable goods like vegetables, fruits, meat, and milk, buy smaller quantities more often. If you have a fridge or freezer, keep it clean and at the right temperature. If you do not, sell perishables quickly and consider processing or preserving what you cannot sell. Another key habit is separating business stock from personal use. Many small shop owners take goods from the shop for personal or family use and forget to record it. This makes stock records wrong and hides the true profit of the business. If you take stock for personal use, record it as a withdrawal and reduce the stock. Better still, pay the business for it and then take it. This keeps your records honest and your business healthy. You should also do a full stock take at least once a month. A full stock take means counting every item in the shop and comparing it to your records. It may take a few hours, but it is worth it. After the count, calculate the value of your stock at cost price and at selling price. The difference between the two is your potential gross profit. If your stock value is falling but your sales are not rising, you may have a problem with theft, pricing, or slow-moving goods. Use the stock take to make decisions: what to buy more of, what to stop buying, what to discount, and what to secure better. Finally, keep your records simple and consistent. Use the same format every day. Write in pen, not pencil. Do not erase mistakes; cross them out and write the correction. Keep receipts for all purchases. If you buy from a supplier who does not give receipts, write the details yourself: date, supplier name, items, quantities, prices, and amount paid. This protects you if there is a dispute and helps you calculate your true costs. In summary, stock control is about knowing what you have, protecting it, and using that knowledge to buy better and sell more. Count your stock, record it, set reorder levels, watch fast and slow movers, reduce theft and expiry, separate business from personal use, and do a monthly stock take. These habits will increase your profit and give you peace of mind. In the next lesson we will look at pricing and profit calculation, which builds directly on the stock records you create here.

2. Module 6 Lesson 2: Pricing, Costing, and Profit Calculation

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 2. In this lesson we learn how to price your goods correctly and calculate your profit. Many small shop and market traders in Africa work hard but earn little because they do not know their true costs and do not price for profit. They guess prices, copy the next stall, or sell at a price that feels fair but does not cover all expenses. This lesson will help you set prices that attract customers and still leave you with money at the end of the day. First, understand the difference between cost price, selling price, gross profit, and net profit. Cost price is what you pay to buy or produce the item. Selling price is what the customer pays you. Gross profit is selling price minus cost price. Net profit is what remains after you subtract all your business expenses, such as transport, rent, market fees, airtime, packaging, electricity, wages, and loan interest. Many traders confuse gross profit with net profit. They see money in the till and think it is all profit. But if you do not subtract expenses, you may be losing money without knowing it. Start by calculating the true cost of every item you sell. If you buy a bag of rice for 50,000 shillings and it contains 25 kilograms, your cost per kilogram is 2,000 shillings. If you repackage into 1 kilogram bags and use plastic bags that cost 100 shillings each, your true cost per unit is 2,100 shillings. If you paid transport of 2,000 shillings for the whole bag, add that to the cost: 50,000 plus 2,000 equals 52,000, divided by 25 equals 2,080 per kilogram, plus 100 for packaging equals 2,180. Now you know your break-even price. Anything above 2,180 is gross profit. Next, decide your target profit margin. A margin is the percentage of the selling price that is profit. A mark-up is the percentage added to the cost price. They are different. If an item costs 2,000 and you sell it for 2,500, your mark-up is 25 percent of cost, but your margin is 20 percent of selling price. For small shops, a common mark-up is 20 to 50 percent, depending on the product. Fast-moving essential goods like salt, sugar, and cooking oil often have lower mark-ups because customers know the prices and competition is high. Luxury or convenience items like phone chargers, cosmetics, and snacks can have higher mark-ups. Perishable goods like tomatoes and fish may need higher mark-ups because of spoilage losses. Your location also matters. A shop near a busy market or bus park can charge slightly more for convenience. A shop in a residential area with many competitors must be price competitive. Study your customers. Are they price-sensitive or convenience-sensitive? Do they buy small quantities daily or bulk weekly? Adjust your pricing and packaging to match. Now let us calculate profit properly. Use this simple formula: Net profit equals total sales minus total cost of goods sold minus total expenses. Total sales is all the money you received from customers. Total cost of goods sold is the cost price of the items you actually sold, not the cost of all the stock you bought. Total expenses are all the costs of running the business during the period. For example, in one day you sell goods worth 100,000 shillings. The cost price of those goods is 70,000. Your expenses for the day are transport 3,000, market fee 2,000, airtime 1,000, and packaging 2,000, totaling 8,000. Your net profit is 100,000 minus 70,000 minus 8,000 equals 22,000 shillings. If you had only looked at the 30,000 difference between sales and cost of goods, you would have overestimated your profit by 8,000. Do this calculation daily or weekly. It will show you which days and which products are most profitable. You should also calculate your break-even point. Break-even is the level of sales at which you cover all your costs and make zero profit. If your fixed expenses per month are 200,000 shillings and your average gross profit margin is 25 percent, you need sales of 800,000 shillings to break even. Every shilling above that is profit. Knowing your break-even point helps you set sales targets and stay motivated. If you are below break-even, you know you must sell more, reduce expenses, or increase margins. Pricing strategies for African small shops include cost-plus pricing, competitive pricing, value-based pricing, and psychological pricing. Cost-plus pricing is the simplest: cost plus a fixed mark-up. Competitive pricing means matching or slightly undercutting nearby shops. Value-based pricing means charging more because you offer convenience, credit, delivery, or better quality. Psychological pricing means using prices like 1,900 instead of 2,000, or 4,500 instead of 5,000, which customers perceive as significantly cheaper. In many markets, round numbers are also common for ease of change, so choose what works for your customers. Be careful with credit sales. Selling on credit can increase sales, but it also increases risk. If you sell on credit, record the customer name, date, items, amount, and agreed payment date. Add a small margin to cover the risk and the time value of money. Do not let credit customers exceed a limit you can afford to lose. Follow up politely but firmly. If a customer does not pay, stop giving credit until they clear the balance. Many small shops fail because too much money is tied up in unpaid debts. Also watch for hidden costs. These include your own time, transport to buy stock, phone calls to suppliers, spoilage, theft, and the cost of holding slow-moving stock. If you do not pay yourself a salary, your business may look profitable but you are actually working for free. Decide on a small monthly salary or drawing for yourself and include it in your expenses. This gives you a true picture of whether the business can support you. Another important tool is the profit and loss statement, also called an income statement. It lists sales, cost of goods sold, gross profit, expenses, and net profit for a period. You can write it in a notebook or use a simple spreadsheet. Do it every month. Compare month to month. Are sales growing? Are expenses growing faster? Is your net profit margin improving? If not, investigate why. Maybe you need to renegotiate with suppliers, reduce transport costs, buy in bulk, or drop unprofitable products. When you know your numbers, you can negotiate better. You can ask suppliers for discounts for bulk purchases or prompt payment. You can compare prices from different wholesalers. You can decide whether it is cheaper to travel to a distant market or buy from a nearby supplier. You can also spot when a product is no longer profitable and replace it. Remember that pricing is not permanent. Costs change, competition changes, and customer demand changes. Review your prices regularly, at least once a month or whenever you buy stock at a new price. If your supplier increases prices, do not absorb the increase silently. Either increase your selling price, reduce your packaging size, or find a cheaper supplier. Communicate changes to your customers honestly. Most customers understand that prices rise when costs rise. In summary, know your cost price, set a selling price that covers costs and gives a target profit, calculate gross and net profit, track expenses, know your break-even point, and review your prices regularly. This discipline will turn your hard work into real money. In the next lesson we will look at customer care and sales techniques that help you sell more at your chosen prices.

3. Module 6 Lesson 3: Customer Care and Sales Techniques for Market Traders

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 3. In this lesson we focus on customers and sales. In small shop and market trading, the customer is not just someone who buys today. The customer is the person who can buy again tomorrow, tell others about you, and keep your business alive. Good customer care and smart sales techniques can increase your sales without spending more on stock. Many traders in Africa have great products but lose customers because of poor service, impatience, or lack of follow-up. This lesson will help you build a loyal customer base and sell more effectively. First, understand what customers want. They want fair prices, good quality, quick service, respect, and reliability. They want to feel welcome and valued. They want to find what they need when they need it. They also want to trust you. Trust is built when you are honest about prices, weights, and quality. If you sell a product that is expired or damaged, you lose trust. If you shortchange a customer, even by a small amount, they will tell others. In many African communities, word of mouth is the strongest marketing. One happy customer can bring five more. One unhappy customer can drive ten away. So treat every customer as important, whether they are buying a single sweet or a full bag of rice. Greet customers warmly. A simple hello, welcome, or how are you today costs nothing and makes people feel seen. In busy markets, customers often choose the stall where they feel respected. Learn to read customers. Some want to browse without pressure. Others want quick service. Some want to negotiate. Others want a fixed price. Adapt your approach. Do not follow customers around or rush them. Be available but not pushy. When a customer asks for something you do not have, do not just say no. Offer an alternative. If you do not stock it, offer to order it for them. Write down their request and follow up. This turns a lost sale into a future sale. Keep your shop or stall clean and organized. Customers judge quality by appearance. A clean, well-arranged stall signals that you care about your products. Make sure prices are visible. If you can, label items with prices so customers do not have to ask. This saves time and reduces confusion. Keep small change ready so you can serve quickly. Long waiting times frustrate customers, especially in busy markets where people are in a hurry. If you have many customers, serve them in order and acknowledge those waiting. Now let us look at sales techniques. The first is upselling. Upselling means encouraging a customer to buy a larger size or a better version of what they already want. If a customer asks for a small packet of tea, you can politely mention that the large packet is better value. Do not force it. Just inform. The second is cross-selling. Cross-selling means suggesting a related product. If a customer buys bread, ask if they need milk or butter. If they buy a phone charger, ask if they need a cable or a phone case. Cross-selling increases the average sale per customer. The third technique is bundling. Bundling means selling two or more items together at a slightly lower total price. For example, sell soap and a sponge as a cleaning pack, or sell rice and cooking oil as a meal pack. Bundles help you move slow-moving stock and give customers a sense of value. The fourth technique is sampling. If you sell food or cosmetics, let customers try a small sample. A taste or a smell can convince a customer to buy. The fifth technique is creating urgency, but honestly. If you have a limited stock of a popular item, you can mention that it sells out quickly. Do not lie. False urgency destroys trust. The sixth technique is loyalty rewards. You can offer a small discount or a free item after a certain number of purchases. For example, every tenth purchase gets a free sachet of milk. This encourages repeat business. You can track loyalty with a simple card or notebook. The seventh technique is credit, used carefully. Offering small credit to trusted regular customers can increase sales and loyalty. But keep records and set limits. The eighth technique is home delivery or market delivery. If you can deliver goods to customers who cannot come to your shop, you can charge a small fee or offer it free for large orders. This is especially useful for elderly customers, busy professionals, and customers buying bulky items. The ninth technique is using WhatsApp or SMS. Many customers in Africa use mobile phones. You can create a broadcast list or a WhatsApp group to announce new stock, special offers, and price changes. Do not spam. Send useful messages only. The tenth technique is asking for feedback. Ask your best customers what they like and what they would like you to stock. This makes them feel valued and helps you improve. It also gives you ideas for new products. Now think about handling complaints. Complaints are opportunities to show that you care. If a customer complains about quality, listen without arguing. Apologize sincerely if you are wrong. Offer a replacement, a refund, or a discount on their next purchase. Fix the problem quickly. A customer whose complaint is handled well often becomes more loyal than one who never complained. Never insult a customer, even if they are rude. Stay calm and professional. If a customer is unreasonable, politely explain your policy and move on. Do not let one difficult customer ruin your day or your reputation. Also, be fair to all customers. Do not give special prices to some and not others unless it is a clear loyalty or bulk discount. Favoritism creates resentment. Keep your promises. If you say you will have an item tomorrow, have it. If you cannot, inform the customer early. Reliability is a powerful competitive advantage. Another important area is pricing psychology in sales. Customers are more likely to buy when they see value. Explain the benefits of your products, not just the features. For example, instead of saying this soap has aloe vera, say this soap is gentle on your skin and good for children. Instead of saying this phone has a long battery, say you can use it all day without charging. Speak the language your customers understand. Use local languages where appropriate. This builds connection and trust. You should also track your sales by customer and by product. Which customers buy the most? Which products sell fastest? Which products have the highest profit margin? Use this information to focus your efforts. Spend more time on your best customers and your most profitable products. Do not ignore small customers, because they may grow. But do prioritize. If you have workers, train them in customer care. Teach them to greet, to be polite, to handle cash carefully, and to call you if there is a problem. A rude worker can destroy your business. A good worker can grow it. Pay them fairly and treat them well so they treat your customers well. Finally, remember that customer care is not a one-time event. It is a daily habit. Every interaction is a chance to build or break trust. Be consistent. Be honest. Be helpful. Be patient. Be clean. Be reliable. These qualities will set you apart in any market. In summary, treat customers with respect, keep your shop clean and organized, use upselling, cross-selling, bundling, sampling, loyalty rewards, and careful credit, handle complaints well, keep your promises, and track your sales. These practices will increase your sales and build a loyal customer base. In the next lesson we will look at record keeping, cash management, and separating business money from personal money.

4. Module 6 Lesson 4: Record Keeping, Cash Management, and Separating Business Money

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 4. In this lesson we focus on record keeping and cash management. Many small shop and market traders in Africa handle cash every day but do not keep proper records. They mix business money with personal money, spend without tracking, and at the end of the month they cannot tell whether they made a profit or a loss. This lesson will help you keep simple records, manage cash flow, and separate business money from personal money. Good records are the foundation of a healthy business. They help you know your sales, expenses, profit, debts, and stock. They help you make decisions about pricing, buying, and expansion. They also help you get loans or support from banks, microfinance institutions, and government programs. Many lenders require records before they give credit. Even if you never borrow, records give you control. Start with a daily sales record. Every day, write down the date, total sales, and any notes about unusual events. If you sell different product categories, record sales by category. For example, food items, drinks, airtime, and household goods. This shows you which categories perform best. If you have a cash register or a phone app, use it. If not, a notebook is fine. Write in pen and keep the notebook safe. Next, keep an expense record. Every time you spend money for the business, write it down. Include the date, what you bought, the amount, and who you paid. Examples include buying stock, transport, rent, market fees, electricity, airtime, packaging, wages, and loan repayments. Keep receipts where possible. If there is no receipt, write the details yourself. At the end of the week or month, add up your expenses. You may be surprised at how much small expenses add up. Then keep a stock record, as we learned in Lesson 1. Your stock record shows what you bought, what you sold, and what remains. It connects your sales and expenses to your physical goods. Without it, you cannot calculate your true cost of goods sold. You should also keep a debtors record. A debtor is a customer who owes you money. Write the customer name, date, items taken, amount owed, and agreed payment date. Follow up regularly. Keep a separate page for each debtor or a single list with dates. When they pay, record the payment and update the balance. Do not rely on memory. Memory fails, especially when you are busy. Keep a creditors record too. A creditor is a supplier or lender you owe money to. Write the name, date, amount owed, and due date. Pay on time to maintain good relationships and avoid interest or loss of credit. Now let us talk about cash management. Cash is the lifeblood of a small business. You need cash to buy stock, pay expenses, and handle emergencies. But cash is also easy to lose, steal, or spend unwisely. Here are some practical rules. First, count your cash at the start and end of each day. Write down the opening cash, total sales, total expenses, and closing cash. The closing cash should equal opening cash plus sales minus expenses. If it does not match, investigate. Maybe a sale was not recorded, maybe change was given incorrectly, or maybe money was taken. Second, keep a cash box or a secure place for money. Do not carry all your cash in your pocket or leave it under the counter where anyone can reach it. If possible, use a lockable box and keep the key safe. In busy markets, consider using mobile money for some transactions to reduce cash handling. Third, bank or save a portion of your daily sales. Many traders spend all the cash they collect and then have nothing for restocking. Set a rule: for example, save 10 percent of daily sales in a savings account, mobile money wallet, or a locked savings box. Use this for restocking, emergencies, or expansion. Fourth, do not use business cash for personal expenses without recording it. If you take money for food, school fees, rent, or personal items, record it as a drawing or salary. This is not a business expense, but it reduces your cash. If you do not record it, your records will be wrong and you may think you have more money than you do. Fifth, separate business money from personal money. This is one of the most important habits. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. If you cannot, use separate envelopes or boxes for business cash and personal cash. Never mix them. When you need personal money, pay yourself a fixed salary or drawing from the business. This makes it clear how much the business is earning and how much you are taking out. It also prevents you from accidentally spending stock money on personal needs. Sixth, plan your cash flow. Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. You need to know when money will come in and when bills are due. If you buy stock on credit and must pay in 14 days, make sure you will have enough cash by then. If sales are slow, delay non-essential purchases. If you have a big expense coming, save ahead. A simple cash flow plan lists expected cash in and expected cash out for the next week or month. Seventh, keep personal and business records separate. If you use a notebook, use one page or section for business and another for personal. Better still, use two different notebooks. If you use a phone, use separate apps or folders. This separation makes tax, loan, and budgeting easier. Eighth, review your records weekly and monthly. At the end of each week, add up sales and expenses. At the end of each month, calculate your profit. Compare with previous months. Look for trends. Are sales growing? Are expenses rising? Are you collecting debts? Are you paying suppliers on time? Use this review to make adjustments. Ninth, protect your records. Keep them in a safe, dry place. If you use a phone, back up your data. If you use paper, keep it away from water, fire, and theft. Records are valuable. Losing them can set you back months. Tenth, be honest with yourself. It is tempting to hide money from family or business partners, or to avoid recording a sale so you can spend it. But dishonest records hurt you most. You cannot improve what you do not measure. If you want to grow, you need the truth. Now let us consider taxes and licenses. In many African countries, small businesses must pay some form of tax, license fee, or market fee. Keep records of these payments. If you are not sure what you owe, ask your local government office, market association, or a business advisor. Paying taxes and fees keeps you legal and avoids fines. It also makes it easier to get government support or loans. If you have employees, keep payroll records. Write down their names, wages, advances, and deductions. Pay them on time and treat them fairly. Good workers are hard to find and expensive to replace. Finally, use your records to set goals. For example, if you want to increase sales by 20 percent next month, your records show you what you need to do. If you want to save for a bigger shop, your records show how much you can set aside each week. If you want to reduce expenses, your records show where the money is going. Records turn wishes into plans. In summary, keep daily sales records, expense records, stock records, debtor and creditor records, and cash records. Count cash daily, keep it secure, save a portion, separate business and personal money, plan cash flow, review weekly and monthly, protect your records, and be honest. These habits will give you control and help your business grow. In the next lesson we will look at growth strategies, risk management, and adapting to change in African markets.

5. Module 6 Lesson 5: Growth Strategies, Risk Management, and Adapting to Change

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 5. In this final lesson we look at how to grow your small shop or market trading business, manage risks, and adapt to change. Africa is a continent of dynamic markets. Prices change, weather changes, regulations change, and customer tastes change. The traders who succeed are not necessarily the ones with the most money. They are the ones who learn, adapt, and manage risk wisely. This lesson will help you plan for growth and protect what you have built. First, let us define growth. Growth does not always mean opening a bigger shop or adding many products. Growth can mean increasing sales, increasing profit, reducing costs, adding new customers, or expanding to a new location. It can also mean improving your skills, your record keeping, or your customer service. Choose a growth goal that fits your resources and your life. Do not grow so fast that you cannot manage your cash, stock, or staff. Slow, steady growth is often safer than sudden expansion. One common growth strategy is increasing sales to existing customers. You can do this by stocking more of what they already buy, offering bundles, or introducing complementary products. For example, if you sell bread, add butter, jam, and tea. If you sell phone accessories, add repair services or airtime. Another strategy is attracting new customers. You can do this by improving your signage, offering a small discount for first-time buyers, or promoting your shop through WhatsApp, local radio, or community groups. You can also attend market days or trade fairs in nearby towns. Another strategy is increasing your profit margin. You can negotiate better prices with suppliers, buy in bulk, reduce transport costs, or sell higher-margin products. You can also reduce waste and theft, which directly increases profit. Another strategy is diversifying. This means adding new product lines or services that are different from what you currently sell. For example, a food vendor could add catering for events. A clothes seller could add shoe sales. A kiosk could add mobile money services. Diversification spreads risk, but it also requires new skills and capital. Start small and test demand before investing heavily. Another strategy is vertical integration. This means controlling more of your supply chain. For example, instead of buying tomatoes from a wholesaler, you could grow some yourself or buy directly from farmers. Instead of buying packaged goods, you could repackage and brand your own. This can reduce costs and increase margins, but it requires more work and knowledge. Another strategy is forming partnerships or cooperatives. In many African markets, traders who work together can buy in bulk, share transport, and negotiate better prices. A cooperative can also access loans, training, and government programs that individual traders cannot. If you join or form a group, choose trustworthy partners and write down your agreements. Another strategy is using technology. Mobile money, WhatsApp, Facebook, and simple accounting apps can help you sell more and manage better. You can accept mobile payments, send receipts by SMS, and advertise to a wider audience. You can also use online marketplaces or delivery apps if they operate in your area. Do not ignore technology, but do not spend too much on it either. Start with what you can afford and learn step by step. Now let us talk about risk management. Every business faces risks. â  SAFETY WARNING â FOOD, CHEMICAL AND HEALTH RISKS IN SMALL TRADING

If you sell food, drinks, or agricultural inputs, the following safety rules are non-negotiable. Failure to follow them can cause serious illness, death, or legal penalties.

  • Food: Never sell expired, mouldy, or spoiled food. Keep raw meat and fish separate from cooked or ready-to-eat foods. Use clean water and wash hands with soap before handling food. Cover all food to keep off flies and dust. WHO estimates that unsafe food causes 600 million cases of foodborne illness and 420,000 deaths globally each year, with Africa carrying the highest burden per capita.
  • Chemicals: If you sell pesticides, herbicides, or other agrochemicals, store them in a locked cabinet away from food, children, and animals. Never repackage chemicals into drink bottles. Wear gloves when handling. MSD Animal Health and FAO both warn that accidental pesticide poisoning is a leading cause of death among smallholder farmers and traders in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Veterinary products: Do not sell veterinary drugs without proper licensing and cold-chain storage where required. Expired or improperly stored vaccines and antibiotics are dangerous and illegal to sell. Follow Aviagen and MSD Animal Health biosecurity guidance if you trade poultry or livestock inputs.
  • Personal hygiene: If you or a family member is sick with diarrhoea, vomiting, or fever, do not handle food for sale. Seek medical care and arrange for someone else to manage the stall until you are well.

Quick Reference Checklist for Daily Trading

  1. Count and record opening stock and cash. Write the value of stock at cost and the cash float before you start selling. This is your baseline.
  2. Check expiry dates and quality. Remove any expired, damaged, or spoiled items before customers see them. Rotate stock so older items sell first (FIFO).
  3. Display prices clearly. Every item should have a visible price. This reduces arguments, builds trust, and prevents undercharging.
  4. Record every sale â even small ones. Use a notebook, a tally sheet, or a simple phone app. Unrecorded sales are the biggest cause of unexplained losses.
  5. Record every credit sale. Write the customer's name, date, amount, and when they promised to pay. Follow up within the agreed time.
  6. Reconcile cash and stock at closing. Count cash, count remaining stock, and compare with your records. Investigate any difference immediately.
  7. Separate business money from personal money. Keep a business cash box or mobile money wallet. Pay yourself a fixed "salary" rather than dipping into stock money.
  8. Plan tomorrow's restocking based on today's records. Buy more of what sold fast, less of what did not. Avoid tying up cash in slow-moving stock.
