1. Measuring the right amount of flour and water

To cook good fortified porridge, you must start with the right amounts. For one child's serving, use 50 grams of fortified corn-soy flour (about 4 heaped tablespoons) and 250 millilitres of clean water (1 cup). This gives a thick porridge that is easy for a sick child to eat. If you add too much flour, the porridge becomes too thick and the child may not be able to swallow it. If you add too much water, the porridge is too thin and the child gets less energy and protein in each spoonful. Always use the same cup and spoon so you learn the correct amounts. A simple kitchen scale that measures in grams is best, but if you do not have one, use a standard tablespoon: 1 tablespoon of fortified flour weighs about 12 grams, so 4 tablespoons give about 50 grams. For water, use a clean measuring cup. If you do not have a measuring cup, use a 300 ml bottle and fill it to 250 ml. When you cook for more children, multiply both the flour and water by the number of children. For example, for 10 children, use 500 grams of flour and 2.5 litres of water. Always measure the water first, then add the flour to the water. This helps prevent lumps. Mix the flour with a small amount of cold water first to make a smooth paste, then add the rest of the water. This is called making a slurry. Never add dry flour directly to hot water because it will form lumps that are hard to stir out. Use a wooden spoon or a whisk to mix well. If you are using a fortified flour that already contains sugar and oil, do not add more sugar or oil at this stage. Just measure the flour and water correctly. Clean hands, clean utensils, and clean water are essential. Wash your hands with soap before you start. Use water that has been boiled and cooled if you are not sure it is safe. Measuring correctly is the first step to giving a malnourished child the nutrients they need to recover.

2. Cooking porridge to the right thickness for children

The right thickness for a malnourished child is like thick cream or custard. It should coat the spoon and not run off quickly. If you tilt the spoon, the porridge should move slowly. This thickness gives more energy and protein per spoonful, which is important because a sick child can only eat small amounts. To get this thickness, use the ratio of 1 part fortified flour to 5 parts water by weight. For example, 50 grams of flour to 250 millilitres of water. If you use less water, the porridge becomes too stiff and the child may choke or refuse to eat. If you use more water, the porridge is too thin and the child fills their stomach with water instead of food. When cooking, bring the water to a boil first. Then add the flour slurry while stirring constantly with a wooden spoon. Keep stirring to prevent lumps and burning. Cook for 5 to 7 minutes after it starts to bubble. You will see the porridge thicken. If it becomes too thick, add a little hot water, one tablespoon at a time, until you reach the right consistency. If it is too thin, let it simmer longer with the lid off, stirring often, until it thickens. Remember that the porridge will thicken more as it cools. So if it looks a little thin while hot, it may be just right after cooling. For young children, you can make it slightly thinner than for older children, but still thick enough to stay on a spoon. Test by dipping a clean spoon into the porridge and letting it drip. It should drip slowly, not in a stream. Another test: draw a line through the porridge in the pot with your spoon. The line should stay visible for a moment before the porridge fills it in. If it fills in immediately, it is too thin. If the line stays and the porridge does not move, it is too thick. Adjust with small amounts of water or flour slurry. Always taste a small amount to check for lumps. A smooth porridge is easier for a child to eat. Serve immediately after cooking, as it will continue to thicken and may become too stiff if left standing.

3. Adding ingredients at the right time to keep nutrients

Fortified corn-soy flour already contains added vitamins and minerals. These nutrients can be destroyed by too much heat or by cooking for too long. So you must add the flour at the right time. The best way is to mix the flour with cold water first to make a slurry, then add it to boiling water. This way, the flour cooks quickly and the nutrients are exposed to heat for a shorter time. Do not boil the porridge for more than 7 minutes. If you cook it longer, you lose vitamins like vitamin C and some B vitamins. If you are adding other ingredients like sugar, oil, or milk, add them after the porridge is fully cooked and has been removed from the heat. For example, add sugar or oil when the porridge has cooled for a minute or two. Do not add them while the porridge is still boiling because high heat can damage some nutrients in the oil, and sugar can burn. If you are using fresh milk or powdered milk, add it after cooking. Fresh milk should be pasteurised or boiled and cooled, then stirred in. Powdered milk can be mixed with a little warm water and added. Do not boil milk with the porridge because it can curdle and lose nutrients. If you are adding fruits like mashed banana or pawpaw, add them after the porridge has cooled to lukewarm. Heat destroys vitamin C in fruits. If you are adding vegetables like mashed carrot or pumpkin, they should be cooked separately and then added at the end. Always use clean utensils when adding ingredients. Stir well to mix evenly. Remember that the goal is to give the child as many nutrients as possible. So treat the fortified flour gently: do not overcook it, and add heat-sensitive ingredients last. Also, do not add too many extra ingredients that might dilute the nutrients. Keep it simple: the fortified flour is the main source of nutrients. Adding a little oil increases energy, and a little sugar improves taste, but they are not necessary if the child accepts the plain porridge. If you are using a premix that already contains oil and sugar, do not add more. Always check the label on the fortified flour package for specific instructions. If there is no label, follow the general rule: cook the flour in water, then add other ingredients off the heat.

4. Adjusting sweetness without using too much sugar

Many children like sweet porridge, but too much sugar is not good for a malnourished child. Sugar adds empty calories and can cause tooth decay. It can also make the child full without giving them the protein and vitamins they need. The goal is to make the porridge tasty enough for the child to eat, without adding too much sugar. Start by tasting the porridge without any sugar. Fortified corn-soy flour often has a mild, slightly nutty taste. If the child refuses it, you can add a small amount of sugar. For one child's serving (250 ml), start with 1 teaspoon of sugar (about 4 grams). Mix it in well. Taste again. If it is still not sweet enough, add another half teaspoon. Do not add more than 2 teaspoons per serving. That is about 8 grams of sugar, which is less than a tablespoon. For a large batch for 10 children, use no more than 20 teaspoons (about 80 grams) of sugar. Instead of sugar, you can use other natural sweeteners that also add nutrients. For example, mashed ripe banana adds sweetness and potassium. One small ripe banana per child can sweeten the porridge and add vitamins. Grated carrot or pumpkin adds natural sweetness and vitamin A. A small amount of honey can be used for children over one year old, but never give honey to babies under one year because it can cause botulism. Honey is also expensive. Another option is to add a little milk, which has natural sugars and adds protein. If you are using a fortified flour that already contains sugar, do not add any more. Check the label. If you are cooking for a feeding program, it is best to keep the porridge unsweetened or lightly sweetened. You can offer sugar on the side, but that is not practical for large groups. Instead, train the children to eat the porridge without added sugar. You can gradually reduce the sugar over time. For example, if you start with 2 teaspoons, reduce to 1.5 teaspoons for a week, then 1 teaspoon, and so on. This helps children get used to less sweet food. Remember, the main goal is nutrition, not sweetness. A child who is malnourished needs energy and protein, not empty sugar. So use sugar sparingly and only if necessary.

5. Serving porridge at the right temperature for young children

Serving temperature is very important for young children. If the porridge is too hot, it can burn the child's mouth and throat. If it is too cold, it may be less appealing and can carry more germs if left out. The right temperature is lukewarm, about 37 degrees Celsius, which is body temperature. You can test it by putting a small drop on your wrist. If it feels neither hot nor cold, it is safe. Another way is to stir the porridge and wait for it to stop steaming. Steam means it is still hot. For a young child, you should let the porridge cool for 5 to 10 minutes after cooking before serving. Stir it occasionally to release heat. Do not blow on the porridge to cool it, because your breath can spread germs. Instead, pour it into a clean bowl and let it sit. If you are outside and it is windy, cover the bowl with a clean cloth to keep dust out. If you are serving many children, you can pour the porridge into individual bowls and let them cool together. Never serve porridge that has been sitting out for more than 2 hours. Bacteria grow quickly at room temperature. If you need to keep it longer, keep it hot (above 60 degrees Celsius) or refrigerate it. But for a feeding program, it is best to cook and serve immediately. For very young children, you can use a small spoon and feed them slowly. Make sure the child is sitting upright and not lying down. Do not force-feed. Let the child eat at their own pace. If the child refuses, try again later. Always wash your hands before feeding. Use a clean spoon for each child. Do not use the same spoon for different children without washing it. If the child is too weak to eat, you may need to feed small amounts every 2 hours. In that case, keep the porridge warm in a thermos or a clean pot with a lid. But remember that repeated heating can destroy nutrients. So it is better to cook small batches frequently. For a feeding program, you can cook a large batch, then keep it in insulated containers. Serve each child a portion, and let it cool to lukewarm before feeding. Always check the temperature before giving it to a child. A simple test: drop a little porridge on the inside of your wrist. If it feels warm but not hot, it is safe. If it feels hot, wait. If it feels cold, reheat gently but do not boil.

6. Making porridge in large batches for a feeding program

When you cook for a feeding program, you need to make porridge for many children at once. This requires planning and the right equipment. First, calculate how many children you will feed. For each child, use 50 grams of fortified flour and 250 millilitres of water. So for 50 children, you need 2.5 kilograms of flour and 12.5 litres of water. For 100 children, use 5 kilograms of flour and 25 litres of water. You will need a large cooking pot, at least 30 litres, and a strong wooden paddle or a long-handled spoon for stirring. You also need a clean source of water, a measuring scale or cups, and clean bowls and spoons for serving. Before cooking, wash your hands and all utensils with soap and clean water. Set up a clean table or mat for serving. If possible, have a helper to stir while you add the flour. The method is the same as for small batches, but scaled up. First, mix the flour with cold water to make a slurry. Use a large bucket. For 5 kilograms of flour, add about 10 litres of cold water to make a smooth slurry. Stir well to remove lumps. Then, in the large pot, bring the remaining 15 litres of water to a boil. Once boiling, slowly pour the slurry into the pot while stirring constantly with the paddle. Keep stirring to prevent burning at the bottom. Cook for 7 to 10 minutes after it returns to a boil. The porridge will thicken. If it is too thick, add hot water a little at a time. If too thin, simmer longer. Taste a small amount to check for lumps and doneness. Once cooked, remove from heat. If you are adding sugar or oil, add now and stir well. For 50 children, you might add up to 250 grams of sugar (about 1 cup) and 250 millilitres of oil (1 cup). But it is better to add less sugar and more oil for energy. Remember that the porridge will thicken as it cools. So make it slightly thinner than you want at serving time. Serve immediately. Use a ladle to pour porridge into each child's bowl. Each child should get about 250 millilitres (1 cup) of porridge. If you have a scale, you can weigh each serving to be exact. Otherwise, use a standard cup. Make sure each child gets the same amount. For children who are very malnourished, you may need to give a second serving. Plan for that by cooking extra. Keep the porridge covered while serving to keep it warm. Do not let it sit out for more than 2 hours. If you have leftover porridge, cool it quickly and refrigerate. Reheat only once. For a feeding program, it is best to cook only what you will serve in one meal. Clean all pots and utensils immediately after cooking. Store the fortified flour in a cool, dry place in a sealed container. Train your helpers on hygiene and measuring. With good planning, you can feed many children safely and effectively.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cooking and Serving Fortified Corn-Soy Porridge

This practical reference is written for small-scale processors, community feeding programmes, school kitchens, and agribusinesses producing fortified corn-soy blend (CSB) porridge for malnourished children. It covers the six core lessons of this module: measuring flour and water, cooking to the right thickness, adding ingredients at the right time, adjusting sweetness without excess sugar, serving at a safe temperature, and making large batches for feeding programmes. Each section includes practical ratios, timing, and quality checks you can apply immediately in your kitchen or production unit.

1. Measuring the Right Amount of Flour and Water

The single most common cause of failed porridge in African feeding programmes is inconsistent measurement. Volume measures (cups, spoons) vary by region and by user. For a business producing fortified porridge at scale, you must standardise by weight.

Standard ratio: For a thin porridge suitable for children 6â8 months, use 1 part fortified corn-soy flour to 12 parts water by weight. For a thick porridge for children 1â5 years, use 1 part flour to 6 parts water. These ratios assume a flour with approximately 10â12% moisture content. If your flour is drier, reduce water slightly; if it is freshly milled and retains more moisture, increase water by 5â10%.

Practical business tip: Invest in a digital kitchen scale (0.1 g precision for small batches, 5 g precision for large batches). A 5 kg bag of fortified corn-soy flour should yield approximately 60 litres of thin porridge or 30 litres of thick porridge. Price your product per 250 ml serving, not per kilogram of flour, because that is how feeding programmes and parents buy.

2. Cooking Porridge to the Right Thickness for Children

Thickness is not just a texture preference â it determines energy density and whether a child can physically swallow the porridge. A porridge that is too thin provides too few calories per spoonful; a porridge that is too thick is a choking risk and is often rejected by young children.

Method: Bring the water to a rolling boil. Make a cold slurry by mixing the flour with 2â3 times its weight in cold water until smooth. Pour the slurry slowly into the boiling water while stirring continuously with a wooden spoon or industrial whisk. Reduce heat to medium-low and simmer for 10â15 minutes, stirring every 30 seconds. The porridge is ready when it coats the back of a spoon and a clean line drawn through it holds its shape.

Quality check: Drop a spoonful onto a clean plate. It should spread slowly but not run like water. If it runs, simmer uncovered for 3â5 more minutes. If it is too thick, add hot water 50 ml at a time, stirring well.

3. Adding Ingredients at the Right Time to Keep Nutrients

Fortified corn-soy flour contains added vitamins and minerals â typically vitamin A, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, iron, zinc, and folic acid. These micronutrients are sensitive to heat, especially vitamin C (if added), thiamine, and vitamin A. Adding them at the wrong time can destroy 20â60% of the nutrient value you paid for.

Rule: If your fortified flour already contains the premix, do not add it again. If you are fortifying at the point of cooking (adding a separate premix), add it after cooking, when the porridge has cooled to below 60Â°C. Stir thoroughly for at least 2 minutes to ensure even distribution.

Other ingredients: Sugar, oil, and milk powder can be added during the last 2 minutes of cooking. Vitamin Cârich fruits (mashed mango, papaya, orange) should be added after cooking and just before serving. Never boil fruit in the porridge.

Corn and soy are highly susceptible to aflatoxin contamination, especially in humid African storage conditions. Aflatoxin is a carcinogen and causes stunting in children. Never use mouldy, discoloured, or musty-smelling corn or soy flour. Test every batch of flour for aflatoxin using a rapid test kit (available from most agricultural input suppliers) before use. Additionally, cooked porridge is a high-risk food for bacterial growth. Serve within 2 hours of cooking. Discard any porridge held at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Never reheat porridge more than once. For feeding programmes, use insulated containers and monitor holding temperature â keep above 60Â°C until serving.

4. Adjusting Sweetness Without Using Too Much Sugar

Sugar is the most expensive ingredient in porridge after the fortified flour, and excessive sugar consumption contributes to dental caries and unhealthy weight gain in children. The WHO recommends that free sugars should be less than 10% of total energy intake, and ideally below 5% for additional health benefits.

Practical strategy: Use naturally sweet ingredients to reduce added sugar. Mashed ripe banana, sweet potato puree, or roasted groundnut paste can add sweetness and nutrients. If you must add sugar, use no more than 5 g (1 teaspoon) per 250 ml serving for children over 2 years. For children under 2 years, do not add sugar at all â their taste preferences are still forming, and unsweetened porridge is healthier.

Business tip: Market your porridge as "no added sugar" or "naturally sweetened" â this is a premium positioning that health-conscious parents and NGOs will pay more for. Train your staff to taste-test each batch and record sugar levels in a batch log.

5. Serving Porridge at the Right Temperature for Young Children

Serving temperature is both a safety and a palatability issue. Porridge that is too hot (above 70Â°C) can burn a child's mouth and oesophagus. Porridge that is too cold (below 40Â°C) is unpalatable and increases the risk of bacterial growth if held for long periods.

Target serving temperature: 50â60Â°C. At this temperature, the porridge is warm enough to be appetising but safe for a young child to eat. Test by placing a drop on the inside of your wrist â it should feel warm, not hot.

For feeding programmes: Use insulated food carriers (cooler boxes or thermos containers) to maintain temperature during transport. Do not serve porridge that has been sitting out for more than 2 hours. If you must hold porridge, keep it above 60Â°C in a hot-holding unit and stir regularly to prevent surface cooling.

6. Making Porridge in Large Batches for a Feeding Programme

Scaling from a household pot to a 200-litre cauldron requires more than multiplying the recipe. Heat distribution, stirring efficiency, and holding time all change.

Large-batch formula: For 100 children (250 ml serving each = 25 litres of thick porridge), use 4.2 kg fortified corn-soy flour and 25 litres water. Use a 50-litre stainless steel pot with a heavy base. Bring 20 litres of water to a boil, add the slurry made from 4.2 kg flour and 5 litres cold water, and simmer for 15 minutes, stirring with a long-handled paddle every 2 minutes. Add oil (250 ml) and sugar (if used, 250 g) in the last 2 minutes. Add premix after cooking, when temperature drops below 60Â°C.

Equipment checklist for large batches: digital scale (25 kg capacity), stainless steel paddle, 50-litre pot, insulated transport containers, serving ladles (250 ml), thermometer, batch log book, and a dedicated handwashing station.

Business model: A feeding programme producing 25 litres of porridge per day (100 children) uses approximately 4.2 kg flour per day, or 105 kg per month. At a wholesale flour price of $1.20/kg, that is $126/month in flour cost. If you charge $0.15 per serving, revenue is $15/day or $450/month â a gross margin of approximately 72% before labour and overhead. This is a viable social enterprise model, especially when combined with government or NGO contracts.
