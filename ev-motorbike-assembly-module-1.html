1. Overview of Electric Motorbike Components

An electric motorbike has five main parts: the battery, motor, controller, throttle, and frame. The battery stores energy, usually lithium-ion, with a capacity of 1-3 kWh for a typical African commuter bike. For example, a 48V 20Ah battery gives about 960Wh, enough for 40-60 km. The motor is usually a brushless DC hub motor, rated 500W to 3000W, mounted in the rear wheel. It converts electrical energy into rotation. The controller is the brain: it takes signals from the throttle and regulates power to the motor. It also protects against overcurrent and low voltage. The throttle is a twist-grip or thumb lever that sends a 0-5V signal to the controller. The frame is often a modified petrol bike frame, but EV-specific frames have stronger dropouts and battery mounts. Other components include the DC-DC converter (steps 48V down to 12V for lights and horn), the charger, and the wiring harness. In Africa, many builders start with a second-hand petrol frame and convert it. You must check that the frame can hold the battery weightâoften 10-15 kg. Use a tape measure and angle grinder to adapt mounts. The battery is the most expensive part, so protect it from vibration and water. A simple test: connect the battery to the controller, twist the throttle, and see if the wheel spins. If not, check the throttle signal with a multimeter. This overview gives you the map; later lessons detail each part.

2. Understanding Electric vs. Internal Combustion Engines

An internal combustion engine (ICE) burns petrol or diesel to create expanding gases that push a piston. It has hundreds of moving parts: pistons, valves, crankshaft, camshaft, and more. It needs oil changes every 1,000-3,000 km, air filters, spark plugs, and a exhaust system. An electric motor has one moving part: the rotor. It uses electromagnetic fields to spin. No oil, no spark plugs, no exhaust. For a learner, the key difference is maintenance. An ICE bike in Africa might need a full service every month if used daily for deliveries. An EV motor needs almost no maintenanceâjust check bearings and bolts every 6 months. Efficiency also differs: ICE converts only about 25-30% of petrol energy into motion; EV converts 80-90% of battery energy. That means an EV can go 60 km on the same energy that an ICE bike uses for 20 km. But EVs have limitations: range is limited by battery capacity, and charging takes 4-8 hours from a wall socket. ICE bikes can refuel in 5 minutes at any petrol station. In Africa, where grid power can be unreliable, many EV owners use solar panels or swap batteries. Torque is another difference: electric motors give full torque from zero RPM, so they accelerate faster off the line. ICE engines need to rev up. For assembly, you must understand that an EV drivetrain is simpler: battery, controller, motor, and throttle. No clutch, no gearbox, no fuel tank. That means fewer parts to break and fewer skills needed for repair. But you must respect high voltageâlater lessons cover safety.

3. Safety Protocols for High-Voltage Systems

EV batteries run at 48V, 60V, or 72V DC. That is high enough to cause serious burns or stop your heart. Always treat any battery above 50V as lethal. Before you touch any wiring, turn off the battery isolator switch, then disconnect the main negative terminal first. Use a multimeter to confirm zero voltage between the positive and negative cables. Never assume a switch is off. Wear insulated gloves rated for 1000V, and use tools with insulated handles. Remove metal jewelleryârings, watches, necklacesâbecause they can short a battery and cause an explosion. Work on a dry rubber mat, not a concrete floor. If you need to test a live circuit, use one hand only (keep the other in your pocket) to avoid a path across your heart. Battery fires are different from petrol fires: they are chemical and need water or a Class D extinguisher, not a standard ABC extinguisher. Keep a bucket of sand and a fire blanket nearby. If a battery starts to smoke or swell, move away and let it burn in a safe open areaâdo not try to smother it. For assembly, never short the battery terminals with a wrench. Use tape on terminals when not connected. When charging, use the correct charger and never leave it unattended overnight. In Africa, where power surges are common, use a surge protector. Teach everyone in your workshop these rules. A single mistake can kill. Practice lockout/tagout: put a padlock on the isolator and keep the key in your pocket. That way no one can turn power on while you work.

4. Tools and Equipment for EV Assembly

You need a basic set of hand tools: metric spanners from 8mm to 19mm, a set of screwdrivers (flat and Phillips), pliers, wire cutters, and a crimping tool for making battery cables. A digital multimeter is essentialâget one that measures DC volts up to 200V, resistance, and continuity. A clamp meter that reads DC amps is useful for checking motor current. For battery work, you need a torque wrench to tighten terminals to the manufacturer's spec (often 5-8 Nm). A soldering iron (60W or more) for splicing wires, with lead-free solder and heat shrink tubing. For mechanical work, a socket set with extensions, a rubber mallet, and a hacksaw or angle grinder for cutting brackets. A bench vice and files help with fitting. For electrical safety, insulated gloves, safety glasses, and a rubber mat. For testing, a 48V/72V battery load tester is expensive, but you can make a simple test with a 100W light bulb in series. For assembly flow, you need a workbench at waist height, good lighting (LED floodlight), and a parts organizer. In Africa, many tools are available in local marketsâbuy good quality, not the cheapest. A cheap multimeter can give wrong readings and cause a short. Also get a wire stripper, cable ties, and electrical tape. For motor installation, you need a torque wrench for axle nuts (often 40-60 Nm). A bearing puller helps remove old bearings. Keep a notebook to record torque values and wiring colours. With these tools, you can assemble a complete EV motorbike.

5. Reading Electrical Schematics and Diagrams

A schematic is a map of how electricity flows. It uses symbols: a long line for a wire, a circle with a letter for a component, and a switch symbol for a switch. You must learn the common symbols: battery (two parallel lines, one long one short), resistor (zigzag), capacitor (two parallel lines), motor (circle with M), controller (rectangle with pins), and ground (three decreasing horizontal lines). In EV diagrams, the main power circuit goes from battery positive to a fuse, then to the controller, then to the motor, then back to battery negative. The throttle connects to the controller with three wires: +5V, ground, and signal. The brake switch cuts power to the controller when pressed. Colour codes vary by manufacturer, but common: red for positive, black for negative, green for signal. Always trace a circuit from the battery to the component and back. Use a multimeter in continuity mode to check if a wire is broken. For assembly, you will get a wiring diagram with your kit. Study it before connecting anything. Label each wire with tape and a marker. If you don't have a diagram, you can draw your own by following the wires. Start with the battery, then controller, then motor. Test each section with a multimeter before connecting the next. A common mistake is reversing positive and negativeâthat can destroy the controller. Double-check with a multimeter: red probe on positive, black on negative should read battery voltage. If it reads negative, swap probes. Practice reading simple diagrams from online sources. In Africa, many kits come with Chinese diagrams, so learn to interpret symbols, not words.

6. Workplace Organization and Assembly Flow

A clean, organized workshop saves time and prevents accidents. Start with a dedicated assembly area: a concrete floor, a sturdy table 1m high, and shelves for parts. Use the 5S method: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. Sort means remove everything not needed for EV assembly. Set in order means each tool has a marked placeâpaint outlines on a pegboard. Shine means clean up spills immediately, especially battery acid or oil. Standardize means write a checklist for each assembly step. Sustain means do it every day. For assembly flow, break the build into stations: Station 1: frame preparation (grinding, painting). Station 2: motor installation (mount wheel, torque axle nuts). Station 3: battery box mounting and wiring. Station 4: controller and throttle connection. Station 5: testing and final checks. Each station has its own tools and parts. Move the bike from station to station, not the tools. This reduces walking and mistakes. Use a Kanban card or a simple whiteboard to track progress. In Africa, space may be limited, so use vertical storage: hang cables on hooks, put small parts in clear jars. Keep a fire extinguisher and first aid kit visible. Label all bins: 'M8 bolts', 'Anderson connectors', 'heat shrink'. At the end of each day, spend 10 minutes tidying. A well-organized shop means you can build a bike in 4-6 hours instead of 2 days. And you will make fewer errors, like crossing wires or losing screws. Good organization is a skill you can learn.

A 72 V battery pack at full charge exceeds 80 V DC. This is above the 50 V threshold that OSHA and IEC classify as hazardous. Never work on a live HV circuit with bare hands. Always:

  • Disconnect the main battery isolator and verify 0 V with a multimeter before touching any HV conductor.
  • Use Class 0 (1,000 V) insulated gloves and insulated tools rated for at least 1,000 V DC.
  • Remove metal jewelry â rings, watches, chains. A shorted 72 V pack can deliver hundreds of amps and cause severe burns or arc flash.
  • Never assemble or service a battery pack alone. Work with a second person present who knows where the isolator is.

3. Reading Schematics â What Matters on the Bench

Most EV motorbike schematics follow a standard convention: high-voltage lines in red or orange, low-voltage control lines in black or blue, and ground in green or black. You do not need to understand every symbol â you need to trace four circuits:

  1. Main power path: Battery â isolator â fuse â controller â motor. Verify fuse rating matches controller current draw.
  2. Control path: Throttle â controller signal input. Typically 0â5 V. Test with multimeter before connecting.
  3. Brake cut-off: Brake lever switch â controller. This must interrupt motor power. Test every unit.
  4. Low-voltage auxiliary: DC-DC converter â 12 V lights, horn, instrumentation. Isolate this from HV.

In the African context, wiring harnesses are often the weakest link. Heat, dust, and vibration degrade insulation faster than in temperate markets. Use heat-shrink on every crimp, and zip-tie looms away from sharp frame edges. A five-minute harness routing job done well saves a two-hour warranty repair later.

4. Workplace Organization for Repeatable Assembly

Use a simple 5S approach adapted to EV assembly:

  • Sort: Separate HV components from LV components on the bench. Never mix.
  • Set in order: Lay out parts in the sequence of the assembly table above.
  • Shine: Clean the bench before every unit. Metal dust and swarf cause shorts.
  • Standardize: Print the checklist below and tape it to the wall.
