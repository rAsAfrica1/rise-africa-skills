1. Introduction to SMT

Surface Mount Technology (SMT) is how almost all modern electronics are built. Instead of pushing component legs through holes in a board, SMT parts sit flat on the surface of the board and are soldered directly to pads. This lets you make smaller, lighter, and cheaper devices. In Africa, SMT is growing fast in places like Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, where local startups are assembling phones, solar charge controllers, and medical devices. As a technician, knowing SMT opens doors to factory jobs and repair work.

SMT components are tiny. A common resistor might be 1.0 mm long by 0.5 mm wide (0402 size). You need good light, tweezers, and a steady hand. The board itself has copper pads instead of holes. Solder pasteâa mix of tiny solder balls and fluxâis printed onto the pads. Then components are placed on the paste, and the whole board is heated in a reflow oven until the solder melts and forms joints.

Compared to through-hole, SMT has advantages: higher density, better high-frequency performance, and lower cost for mass production. But it also has challenges: components are harder to handle, and you need magnification to inspect joints. In many African workshops, you'll start with manual SMT soldering using a fine-tip iron and thin solder wire (0.5 mm or 0.3 mm). Later, you might use a hot air rework station or a small reflow oven.

Safety first: lead-free solder is common, but it melts at higher temperatures (around 217Â°C) than leaded solder (183Â°C). Always work in a ventilated area and wash your hands after handling solder. For beginners, practice on scrap boards. Get used to the feel of placing a 0805 resistor with tweezers. Count your components before and afterâlosing a tiny capacitor on the floor is easy.

In this module, you'll learn to identify SMT parts, apply adhesives, solder manually, use reflow, and inspect joints. By the end, you'll be able to assemble a simple SMT board, like an LED flasher or a small power supply, using tools available in most African cities: a temperature-controlled soldering station, fine tweezers, flux, and a magnifying lamp. Remember, SMT is a skill built by repetition. Start with larger sizes (1206 or 0805) and work down as you improve.

2. SMT Component Identification and Handling

SMT components come in many shapes and sizes. The most common are resistors, capacitors, inductors, diodes, transistors, and integrated circuits (ICs). Resistors and capacitors are usually rectangular and black or brown. They have no legs; instead, they have metal end caps that solder to the board. Their size is given by a code like 0805, which means 0.08 inch by 0.05 inch (about 2.0 mm x 1.25 mm). Common sizes you'll see: 1206 (3.2 mm x 1.6 mm), 0805 (2.0 mm x 1.25 mm), 0603 (1.6 mm x 0.8 mm), and 0402 (1.0 mm x 0.5 mm). Smaller sizes like 0201 are for factory machines, not hand work.

Resistors have a three- or four-digit code printed on top. For example, "103" means 10 x 10^3 = 10,000 ohms (10k). "472" is 4,700 ohms. A "0" or "000" is a zero-ohm jumper. Capacitors often have no marking, so you must measure them with a multimeter that has a capacitance setting, or trust the reel label. Tantalum capacitors have a stripe marking the positive terminal. Diodes have a stripe on the cathode end. LEDs are often clear or colored, with a flat edge or a green line indicating cathode.

ICs are black rectangles with pins on two or four sides. SOIC (Small Outline Integrated Circuit) has pins on two sides, with a pin 1 markerâa dot or a chamfered edge. QFN (Quad Flat No-leads) has pins underneath, which makes hand soldering hard; you need hot air. QFP (Quad Flat Package) has pins on all four sides. Always check pin 1 orientation before soldering. A wrong orientation can destroy the chip.

Handling SMT parts requires care. Use fine-point tweezers (ESD-safe, like stainless steel with carbon tips) to pick up components. Never use your fingersâskin oils cause solder problems. Work on a clean, lint-free surface. Keep components in their original reels or in small compartment boxes. Static electricity can kill ICs, so wear an anti-static wrist strap connected to a grounded mat. In dry African climates, static is a real risk. If you don't have a strap, touch a grounded metal object before handling ICs.

When placing components, use a vacuum pickup tool for tiny parts if available. Otherwise, tweezers work. Place the component gently on the paste, then press down slightly to ensure contact. Do not slide itâthat smears the paste. For manual soldering, hold the component with tweezers in one hand and the iron in the other. Practice with 0805 resistors first. Count your parts before and after each session; a lost 0402 capacitor can cost you a whole board.

3. SMT Adhesives and Their Application

SMT adhesives, also called SMT glue or chip glue, are used to hold components in place during wave soldering or before reflow. They are not the same as solder paste. Adhesives are usually epoxy-based and come in syringes or tubes. In Africa, you can find them at electronics suppliers in cities like Lagos, Nairobi, and Johannesburg, or order online. Common brands include Loctite, Henkel, and generic Chinese brands. A 10cc syringe of red glue (for example, Loctite 3611) costs around $15â$30 and can last for many boards.

Adhesives are needed when you have components on the bottom side of a board that will go through wave soldering, or when you want to prevent parts from moving during handling. They are also used for staking heavy components like connectors or electrolytic capacitors. The adhesive is dispensed as a small dotâabout 0.5 mm to 1 mm in diameterâon the board where the component body will sit, not on the pads. Then the component is placed on the dot, and the adhesive is cured by heat (typically 120â150Â°C for 5â10 minutes) or by UV light.

Applying adhesive by hand is tricky. You need a dispenserâa pneumatic syringe or a manual plunger. A steady hand and a fine needle (22â25 gauge) help. Practice on a scrap board: dispense dots of uniform size, then place 0805 resistors on them. Too much adhesive can squeeze onto the pads and prevent soldering. Too little won't hold. The dot should be about one-third the size of the component. After placing, check that the component sits flat and is centered.

Curing is critical. If you don't cure properly, components will fall off during soldering. A toaster oven can work for curing if you can control temperature. Set it to 130Â°C for 10 minutes. Do not use a microwave. For UV-cure adhesives, a UV flashlight (365 nm) works, but you must expose all sides. In humid African climates, store adhesive syringes in a sealed bag with desiccant. Cold storage (2â8Â°C) extends shelf life, but let the syringe warm to room temperature before use to avoid condensation.

Safety: uncured adhesive can irritate skin. Wear nitrile gloves and safety glasses. If adhesive gets on your skin, wash with soap and warm water. Do not use solvents like acetone near your skin. Work in a ventilated area. Adhesives are flammable before curing, so keep away from sparks. With practice, you can dispense 50 dots per minute by hand. Start with a few components, cure them, and test their strength by gently prying with tweezers. If they hold, you're ready for production.

4. Manual SMT Soldering Techniques

Manual SMT soldering is a core skill for African electronics technicians. You need a temperature-controlled soldering station (e.g., Hakko FX-888D or a Chinese clone like Aoyue 937+), a fine-tip iron (0.5 mm or 1 mm chisel tip), thin solder wire (0.3â0.5 mm, lead-free or leaded), flux (rosin or no-clean gel), fine tweezers, and a magnifying lamp or stereo microscope. A good setup costs $100â$300, but you can start with a $30 iron if you practice.

First, prepare the board. Clean the pads with isopropyl alcohol (99%) and a lint-free swab. Apply a small amount of flux to the pads using a flux pen or a toothpick. Flux helps solder flow and prevents oxidation. For a two-terminal component like a resistor, the process is: (1) Tin one pad with a small amount of solder. (2) Hold the component with tweezers and place it on the pads. (3) Solder one end firstâreheat the tinned pad and push the component into place. (4) Solder the other end. (5) Go back and re-solder the first end to ensure a good fillet. Use a iron temperature of 300â350Â°C for leaded solder, 350â380Â°C for lead-free. Do not exceed 400Â°C; you can lift pads.

For ICs with pins on two sides (SOIC), use the drag-soldering method. Align pin 1 correctly. Tack one corner pin, then the opposite corner. Apply flux generously. With a small amount of solder on the tip, drag the iron across all pins on one side. Solder bridges may form; remove them with solder wick and flux. For QFN or QFP, you need hot air. A hot air rework station (e.g., Quick 861DW) set to 300â350Â°C with medium airflow can solder a QFN in 30 seconds. Use tweezers to hold the chip, heat the pads, and let surface tension pull it into place.

Practice on a scrap board with 0805 resistors and SOIC chips. Start with 10 components. Check each joint with a magnifier. A good joint is shiny and concave (like a tiny volcano). A cold joint looks dull and blobby. If you bridge pins, add flux and drag the iron away from the bridge, or use solder wick. Clean flux residue with isopropyl alcohol and a brush. In dusty African workshops, cover your board when not working. Keep your tip clean with a brass spongeânever use a wet sponge, which thermal-shocks the tip. Replace tips when they become pitted. With 20 hours of practice, you can solder 50 components per hour by hand.

5. Reflow Soldering Basics

Reflow soldering is the standard way to attach SMT components in production. Instead of touching each joint with an iron, you apply solder paste to the pads, place components, and heat the whole board until the paste melts and forms joints. In Africa, small workshops use modified toaster ovens or dedicated reflow ovens (e.g., Puhui T-962, around $200â$400). For low volume, you can even use a hot plate or a skillet. The key is a controlled temperature profile.

Solder paste is a mixture of tiny solder balls (20â45 Âµm) and flux. It comes in a syringe or jar. Store it in the fridge (2â8Â°C). Before use, let it warm to room temperature for 30 minutes. Apply paste using a stencilâa thin stainless steel sheet with cutouts for the pads. Align the stencil, squeegee paste across it, and lift. You want a smooth, even layer about 0.1â0.15 mm thick. For hand application, you can use a syringe and dispense small dots, but stencils give better results. A basic stencil for a simple board costs $20â$50 from online services.

After paste application, place components with tweezers. Then reflow. The ideal profile has four stages: preheat (room to 150Â°C, 1â2 minutes), soak (150â180Â°C, 1â2 minutes), reflow (peak 235â245Â°C for lead-free, 210â220Â°C for leaded, 30â60 seconds), and cooling (controlled, not forced). A toaster oven with a PID controller (like a REX-C100) can follow this. Without a controller, you can manually adjust the dial and watch the boardâbut it's risky. Practice with a scrap board and a thermocouple to measure temperature.

Common defects: insufficient paste causes opens; too much paste causes bridges. Tombstoning (component stands up) happens when one pad heats faster than the other. To avoid, ensure even pad sizes and preheat slowly. After reflow, inspect with a magnifier. Good joints are shiny and filleted. If you see bridges, remove with solder wick and flux. For prototypes, you can reflow with a hot air station: set to 300Â°C, move the nozzle in circles over the board until solder melts. This is slower but works for a few components. In humid conditions, bake boards at 100Â°C for 1 hour before reflow to remove moisture, which can cause popcorning (cracks in components).

6. Inspecting SMT Solder Joints

Inspection is how you catch defects before they cause failures. For SMT, you need good lighting and magnification. A 10x loupe or a USB microscope (e.g., Andonstar ADSM302, around $80) works well. A stereo microscope with 20xâ40x is better for fine pitch. In African workshops, a simple magnifying lamp with a 5-inch lens and a bright LED ring light is affordable and effective. Always inspect under bright, white lightânot yellow.

Start with a visual check. A good solder joint is shiny, smooth, and concave, like a tiny fillet. It should cover the pad and the component terminal. For lead-free solder, the joint may look slightly grainy but should still be continuous. Look for these defects: (1) Cold joint: dull, blobby, or wrinkledâcaused by insufficient heat or movement during cooling. (2) Solder bridge: solder connects two adjacent pins or pads. (3) Tombstone: component stands up on one end. (4) Insufficient solder: joint looks thin or missing. (5) Excess solder: ball of solder covering the pad. (6) Cracked joint: visible crack, often from thermal stress.

Use a systematic approach. Inspect each component one by one, from one corner of the board to the other. For ICs, check every pin. For QFN, you cannot see the joints from the topâuse a side-view mirror or X-ray if available. In many African settings, X-ray is not accessible, so you rely on electrical testing. After visual inspection, use a multimeter to check for shorts and opens. Set it to continuity mode. Test between adjacent pins on ICsâif it beeps, you have a bridge. Test from component pad to a known test point to confirm connection.

If you find a defect, fix it. For bridges, add flux and use solder wick or a clean iron tip to drag the solder away. For cold joints, reheat with flux and a small amount of fresh solder. For tombstones, remove the component, clean the pads, and re-solder. Always clean flux residue with isopropyl alcohol and a brushâleftover flux can cause corrosion or shorts over time. In humid climates, flux residue absorbs moisture and can cause leakage currents.

Record your defects. Keep a log of what you find and how you fix it. If you see the same defect repeatedly, adjust your processâmaybe your iron is too cold, or your paste is too old. Good inspection saves time and money. A board that passes inspection will work reliably for years. Practice by inspecting scrap boards and identifying defects. With 10 hours of practice, you'll spot a cold joint in under 5 seconds.

Practical Reference: SMT Fundamentals for African Electronics Assembly Businesses

Surface Mount Technology (SMT) is the backbone of modern electronics manufacturing. For African electronics assembly businessesâwhether you are producing power inverters in Lagos, LED lighting in Nairobi, or IoT sensors in Kigaliâmastering SMT fundamentals is not optional. This practical reference distills the six core lessons of this module into actionable guidance for your production floor.

This reference is designed for business owners, production supervisors, and technicians who need to make decisions about equipment, materials, and quality control. It assumes you are working in a resource-constrained environment where every component, every gram of solder paste, and every hour of production time carries real cost.

1. Introduction to SMT: Why It Matters for Your Business

SMT involves mounting components directly onto the surface of a printed circuit board (PCB) rather than inserting leads through holes. This allows for smaller boards, higher component density, and faster automated assembly. For African manufacturers, SMT opens doors to contracts with telecom companies, solar energy firms, and consumer electronics brands that demand compact, reliable products.

The business case is straightforward: SMT reduces board size by 30â50%, lowers material costs through smaller components, and enables automated production that scales with demand. However, it also demands tighter process control. A through-hole joint can be visually inspected and reworked easily; an SMT joint may be hidden under a component and require X-ray or careful optical inspection.

2. SMT Component Identification and Handling

SMT components come in standardized package sizes. The most common are resistors and capacitors in 0402, 0603, 0805, and 1206 packages, where the number indicates dimensions in hundredths of an inch. For African assembly businesses, 0805 and 1206 are the most practical starting pointsâthey are large enough for manual placement and inspection without magnification, yet small enough to keep boards compact.

Handling these components requires care. Moisture sensitivity is a real threat: components absorb moisture from the air, and during reflow, that moisture turns to steam and causes internal cracks (popcorning). In humid African climatesâLagos, Accra, Mombasaâthis risk is elevated. Store moisture-sensitive components in sealed bags with desiccant and use a moisture sensitivity level (MSL) rating to determine floor life.

3. SMT Adhesives and Their Application

SMT adhesivesâoften called "red glue" due to their colorâserve two purposes: they hold components in place during wave soldering of through-hole parts on the same board, and they prevent component movement during reflow if the board is double-sided. For businesses doing mixed-technology assembly (SMT + through-hole), adhesive dispensing is a critical step.

Adhesives are typically epoxy-based and applied via syringe, stencil printing, or automated dispenser. The key parameters are dot size, placement accuracy, and cure schedule. In African workshops without automated dispensers, manual syringe application works but requires practice to achieve consistent dots. A dot that is too small will not hold the component; too large will contaminate pads and cause solder defects.

Business tip: If you are not doing wave soldering or double-sided reflow, you may not need adhesive at all. Many small African assembly shops skip adhesive entirely by using solder paste's natural tackiness to hold components during reflow. This simplifies the process and reduces material costs.

4. Manual SMT Soldering Techniques

Manual SMT soldering is a viable production method for low-to-medium volumes. It requires a temperature-controlled soldering iron with a fine tip (0.5â1.0 mm), fine solder wire (0.3â0.5 mm diameter), flux, and tweezers. The technique is:

  1. Apply flux to the pads.
  2. Tin one pad with a small amount of solder.
  3. Place the component with tweezers, holding it in position.
  4. Reflow the tinned pad while keeping the component aligned.
  5. Solder the remaining pads.
  6. Inspect the joints under magnification.

For African technicians, the biggest challenge is heat management. Too much heat damages components and lifts pads; too little creates cold joints. Practice on scrap boards before working on production units. A good rule: use a 30â40 W iron set to 320â350Â°C for lead-free solder, or 300â320Â°C for leaded solder.

6. Inspecting SMT Solder Joints

Inspection is where quality is confirmed. A good SMT solder joint has a shiny, concave fillet; a bad joint is dull, convex, or has insufficient solder. Common defects include:

  • Tombstoning: Component stands up on one end due to uneven heating.
  • Bridging: Solder connects two adjacent pads.
  • Cold joint: Dull, grainy surface from insufficient heat.
  • Insufficient solder: Weak connection, often from poor paste deposition.
  • Solder balls: Small spheres that can cause short circuits.

For African businesses, visual inspection with a 10Ã magnifying lamp is the minimum. For fine-pitch components (QFP, QFN), a USB microscope ($50â$150) is a good investment. X-ray inspection is expensive and usually not practical for small shops, but you can outsource it for critical boards.
