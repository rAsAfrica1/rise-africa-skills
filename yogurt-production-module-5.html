1. Pasteurization vs. Heat Treatment for Yogurt

In yogurt making, heat treatment does two jobs: it kills harmful bacteria and it changes milk proteins so they can hold water and form a thick gel. Pasteurization is a specific heat treatment that uses precise time and temperature to kill pathogens. For yogurt, we often heat milk more severely than simple pasteurization. For example, heating to 85C for 30 minutes or 90C for 5 minutes not only pasteurizes but also denatures whey proteins, which helps the yogurt set firm without added thickeners.

In many African settings, milk may be raw or informally pasteurized. Raw milk can carry E. coli, Salmonella, and Brucella, so you must heat it properly. A simple pasteurization for drinking milk is 63C for 30 minutes (low-temp long-time) or 72C for 15 seconds (high-temp short-time). But for yogurt, you need higher heat. If you only pasteurize at 72C for 15 seconds, your yogurt may be thin and watery because the whey proteins are not fully denatured.

Use a reliable thermometer. A digital probe thermometer costs little and is essential. If you don't have one, you can use the 'string test': heat milk while stirring, and when it starts to steam and small bubbles form around the edge, it's near 85C. But a thermometer is safer. For small-scale production, a stainless steel pot on a gas burner or charcoal stove works. Stir constantly to avoid scorching. Once you reach 85C, hold it there for 30 minutes, or bring to 90C and hold for 5 minutes. Then cool quickly.

Remember: heat treatment for yogurt is not just about safety; it's about texture. The higher heat causes whey proteins to unfold and bind with casein, creating a stronger gel. This means you can make thick yogurt even from milk that is not very high in fat. In Africa, where refrigeration is limited, a thicker yogurt also spoils more slowly because less water separates out. So always aim for the yogurt-specific heat treatment, not just basic pasteurization.

2. High-Temperature Short-Time (HTST) Processing

HTST stands for High-Temperature Short-Time. It means heating milk to a high temperature for a very short time, usually 72C to 75C for 15 to 20 seconds. This is common in commercial dairies because it kills pathogens quickly and preserves more of the milk's natural flavor. But for yogurt, HTST alone is often not enough to get a thick set. You need either a higher temperature or a longer holding time to denature whey proteins.

In small-scale African yogurt production, you can adapt HTST principles without expensive equipment. For example, you can heat milk to 85C for 5 minutes instead of 72C for 15 seconds. That is still a 'short-time' relative to 30 minutes, but it's hotter. Or you can use a two-stage process: first pasteurize at 72C for 15 seconds to kill pathogens, then heat to 90C for 5 minutes to denature proteins. But that's more work. Most small producers simply heat to 85C for 30 minutes or 90C for 5 minutes.

If you want to simulate HTST, you need a way to heat and cool quickly. A simple method: use a large pot of water as a double boiler. Heat the milk in a smaller pot inside the water bath, stirring constantly. When the milk reaches 85C, start a timer for 5 minutes. Then immediately transfer the inner pot to a basin of cold water to stop the heat. This rapid cooling is part of HTST. It prevents over-cooking and keeps the milk from developing a cooked flavor.

HTST is not just about time and temperature; it's also about flow. In a factory, milk flows continuously through a heat exchanger. In your kitchen, you can mimic that by stirring constantly so all the milk gets the same heat. Never let milk sit unstirred at high heatit will scorch and develop off-flavors. Also, use a clean thermometer that you sanitize before and after. If you don't have a thermometer, you can't do HTST reliably. So invest in one. For a 20-liter batch, a 85C for 5 minutes treatment is a good compromise: it's faster than 30 minutes and still gives good protein denaturation.

3. Denaturation of Whey Proteins and Water Binding

Milk proteins are mainly casein and whey proteins. Casein forms the main structure of yogurt, but whey proteins are like little balls that float in the water. When you heat milk to 85C or above, the whey proteins unfoldthis is called denaturation. Once unfolded, they can bind with casein and with water. That means the yogurt gel becomes stronger and holds more water, so you get less whey separation (the watery layer on top).

In Africa, where milk may be transported long distances and not always refrigerated, water binding is critical. A yogurt that holds water well will have a longer shelf life and a creamier texture. If you don't heat enough, the whey proteins stay folded, and they don't bind. The result is a weak gel that leaks water. You might see a pool of yellow liquid after a few hours. That is whey, and it means your heat treatment was insufficient.

To denature whey proteins, you need to hold the milk at 85C for at least 20-30 minutes, or at 90C for 5-10 minutes. At 85C for 30 minutes, about 80-90% of whey proteins are denatured. At 90C for 5 minutes, you get similar results. But if you only heat to 72C for 15 seconds, less than 10% are denatured. So for yogurt, always go higher.

You can see the effect yourself. Take two batches of milk. Heat one to 85C for 30 minutes, the other to 72C for 15 seconds. Cool both, add the same amount of yogurt starter, and incubate. After 6 hours, the first batch will be thick and firm; the second will be thin and watery. That difference is water binding. Also, denaturation exposes more surface area for the starter bacteria to work, so fermentation may be slightly faster. But be careful: overheating above 95C can cause off-flavors and destroy some vitamins. So stick to 85-90C.

4. Cooling Protocols After Heat Treatment

After you heat milk for yogurt, you must cool it to the incubation temperatureusually 42-45C for most yogurt cultures. If you add starter when the milk is too hot (above 50C), you will kill the bacteria. If it's too cool (below 40C), fermentation will be slow or fail. So cooling is as important as heating. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can be high, cooling can be a challenge. You need a fast, reliable method.

The best method is a cold water bath. After heating, put your pot of milk into a larger basin or sink filled with cold water and ice if available. Stir the milk gently to distribute the heat. Change the water if it gets warm. Within 15-20 minutes, the milk should drop from 85C to 45C. If you don't have ice, use the coldest water you can get, and add salt to the watersalt lowers the freezing point and makes the water colder. You can also use a wet cloth wrapped around the pot and a fan to speed evaporation.

Another method is to pour the hot milk into a clean, shallow container. The larger surface area helps it cool faster. But be careful: open containers can pick up contaminants. Cover with a clean cloth or lid. Never leave milk to cool overnight at room temperatureit may pick up wild bacteria that spoil the yogurt. Cool it within 30 minutes.

Use a thermometer to check. When the milk reaches 45C, it feels warm but not hot on your wrist. If you don't have a thermometer, drip a little milk on the inside of your wrist. If it feels comfortably warm (like bathwater), it's about right. But a thermometer is more accurate. Once cooled, add your starter culture immediately and mix well. Then incubate. If you cool too slowly, the milk may sit in the danger zone (40-60C) where unwanted bacteria can grow. So always cool quickly and cleanly.

5. Monitoring Heat Treatment Efficacy

You can't just guess that your heat treatment worked. You need to monitor it. The simplest tool is a thermometer. A digital probe thermometer with a range up to 100C is cheap and accurate. Check it in boiling water (100C) and ice water (0C) to make sure it reads correctly. During heating, stir and check the temperature every few minutes. For a 85C for 30 minutes treatment, once you reach 85C, start a timer and keep the temperature between 84C and 86C. If it drops, increase the heat slightly. If it goes above 90C, reduce heat and stir.

Another way to monitor is the 'alcohol test' for pasteurization. Mix equal parts milk and 68% ethanol (or strong local gin) in a small bottle. If the milk curdles, it means it wasn't heated enough (raw milk curdles). If it stays smooth, pasteurization was successful. This test is used in dairy labs, but you can do it in your kitchen. However, it only tells you if you reached pasteurization, not if you denatured whey proteins. For that, you need a heat treatment that goes beyond pasteurization.

You can also monitor the effect on yogurt quality. If your yogurt sets firm and doesn't leak much whey, your heat treatment was likely good. If it's thin and watery, you probably didn't heat enough. Keep records: write down the temperature, time, and how the yogurt turned out. After a few batches, you'll know what works for your milk and equipment. In Africa, milk composition varies (cow, goat, sheep), so you may need to adjust. For example, goat milk has smaller fat globules and may need slightly higher heat or longer time.

Finally, monitor cooling. Check that the milk reaches 45C before adding starter. If you add starter too hot, you'll kill it and the yogurt won't ferment. If you're unsure, use a thermometer. Don't rely on touch alone. A simple logbook helps you repeat success and avoid mistakes.

6. Troubleshooting Heat-Induced Defects

Even with good intentions, heat treatment can go wrong. Here are common defects and how to fix them. First, scorching: if you heat milk too fast or don't stir, it burns on the bottom. The yogurt will taste burnt and may be brown. To avoid, use a heavy-bottomed pot and stir constantly. If scorching happens, don't scrape the bottom into the milk. Discard the burnt layer and use the rest only if the flavor is not affected. Otherwise, start over.

Second, over-denaturation: heating above 95C or for too long can cause off-flavors (cooked, cabbage-like) and destroy vitamins. It can also make the yogurt grainy. If you accidentally overheat, cool quickly and add starter. The yogurt may still be okay but less pleasant. Next time, use a timer and thermometer.

Third, under-heating: if you don't reach 85C for enough time, whey proteins don't denature, and your yogurt will be thin and leak whey. To fix, you can't reverse it after fermentation. But you can strain the yogurt to remove whey, making it thicker (like Greek yogurt). However, that reduces yield. Better to heat properly next time.

Fourth, slow cooling: if milk cools too slowly, wild bacteria can grow, causing off-flavors or gas. Cool quickly in a water bath. If you see bubbles or a yeasty smell, discard the batch.

Fifth, temperature shock to starter: if you add starter when milk is above 50C, you kill the bacteria. The yogurt won't set. Check temperature with a thermometer. If it's too hot, wait. If you already added starter and it's too hot, you may need to add more starter once cooled.

Sixth, inconsistent heat: if you heat milk in a large pot without stirring, some parts may be hotter than others. This leads to uneven protein denaturation and weak gel. Always stir. Use a double boiler if possible. In Africa, where power outages are common, a gas or charcoal stove with a water bath is reliable. Keep a backup thermometer. With practice, you'll avoid these defects and make consistent, thick yogurt.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Heat Treatment and Cooling of Milk for Yogurt

In African yoghurt enterprises from smallholder co-operatives in Kenya's Rift Valley to urban dairies in Lagos and Kampala heat treatment is the single step that most determines product safety, texture, shelf life, and profit margin. Get it right and you produce a firm, clean-flavoured set yoghurt that survives 1421 days in the cold chain. Get it wrong and you get whey separation, weak gel, off-flavours, or a batch that fails the co-operative's quality gate and is rejected by the buyer.

This practical reference distils the science into decisions a production supervisor can act on. It assumes you are working with fresh cow, goat, or camel milk, a batch pasteuriser or HTST unit, and a yoghurt culture of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus.

1. Pasteurisation vs. Heat Treatment for Yoghurt

Yoghurt is not made from pasteurised milk in the legal sense alone. It is made from milk that has been heat-treated at 8595 C for 530 minutes, or the HTST equivalent. This is deliberately more severe than standard pasteurisation (72 C / 15 s or 63 C / 30 min). The reason is twofold:

  • Safety: kill vegetative pathogens Salmonella, Listeria monocytogenes, E. coli O157:H7, Campylobacter, and Brucella (important in pastoralist milk in Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria).
  • Functionality: denature 7090 % of whey proteins, especially -lactoglobulin, so they bind water, thicken the gel, and give the characteristic yoghurt body.

Standard pasteurisation alone does not denature enough whey protein. A batch that is only pasteurised will give a thin, syrupy yoghurt with heavy syneresis (whey separation) within days. This is the most common defect in African small-scale production and is entirely preventable.

2. High-Temperature Short-Time (HTST) Processing

HTST is a continuous process: milk flows through a plate heat exchanger, is held at temperature for a defined residence time, then cooled rapidly. For yoghurt, HTST is typically run at 9095 C for 25 minutes (sometimes called "high-heat HTST" or "yoghurt HTST"), which is more intense than the 72 C / 15 s used for drinking milk.

For an African processor, HTST offers three commercial advantages:

  • Throughput: a 1,000 L/h HTST unit can process a full day's milk intake in one shift, reducing labour cost per litre.
  • Consistency: automated temperature control reduces batch-to-batch variation, which matters when you are supplying supermarkets or export markets.
  • Energy efficiency: regenerative heating recovers 7090 % of heat, cutting fuel or electricity costs significant where diesel or grid power is expensive.

The trade-off is capital cost and the need for a reliable power supply. Many small African dairies start with batch vat pasteurisation (85 C / 30 min) and move to HTST only when daily volume exceeds 5001,000 L.

3. Denaturation of Whey Proteins and Water Binding

When milk is heated above ~70 C, -lactoglobulin unfolds and exposes reactive thiol groups. These interact with -casein on the casein micelle surface, forming a -lactoglobulin/-casein complex. The practical consequences for yoghurt are:

  • Increased water binding: denatured whey proteins hold water that would otherwise be expelled as whey. This is the main reason heat-treated yoghurt has low syneresis.
  • Firmer gel: the wheycasein complexes act as additional cross-links in the acid gel formed during fermentation.
  • Improved viscosity: important for drinkable yoghurt and for spoonable products that must survive transport.

The relationship between temperature, time, and denaturation is the core control variable. A useful rule of thumb: denaturation increases roughly 2.53 for every 10 C rise in the 7095 C range. That is why 90 C / 5 min is far more effective than 72 C / 15 s, even though the safety kill is comparable.

4. Cooling Protocols After Heat Treatment

Cooling is not a passive step. It determines inoculation temperature, culture viability, and the risk of post-heat contamination. The target is to bring milk from 9095 C to 4245 C within 3060 minutes, then inoculate immediately.

Cooling options, ranked by practicality for African dairies:

  1. Regenerative cooling in HTST: the incoming cold raw milk absorbs heat from the hot treated milk. Free energy recovery, fastest method.
  2. Chilled water in a plate cooler: for batch systems, circulate chilled water (25 C) through a plate heat exchanger. Requires a chiller or ice bank.
  3. Ice bank / cold water bath: low-cost option for < 200 L batches. A 100 L batch in a stainless vat with ice water can cool in 4590 min with stirring.
  4. Passive air cooling: not recommended slow, risks contamination, and holds milk in the 3050 C danger zone where thermophilic spore-formers can grow.

SAFETY WARNING Do not cool in the danger zone

Never allow heat-treated milk to sit between 5 C and 55 C for more than 2 hours. This range supports rapid growth of Bacillus cereus, Clostridium perfringens, and surviving thermoduric organisms. In warm African climates, ambient temperatures of 2835 C mean an uncovered vat can re-contaminate within minutes. Cool in a closed system, use potable chilled water, and sanitise all transfer lines and fittings with 0.5 % chlorine or 1.5 % peracetic acid before use. If the milk is not inoculated within 1 hour of reaching 45 C, re-heat to 85 C for 5 min or discard.

5. Monitoring Heat Treatment Efficacy

You cannot manage what you do not measure. At minimum, every batch should record:

  • Temperature profile: continuous chart recorder or data logger on the holding tube. Manual checks every 15 min are acceptable for batch vats.
  • Holding time: verified by flow rate (HTST) or stopwatch (batch).
  • Alkaline phosphatase test: the official pasteurisation verification test. A negative result confirms adequate heat treatment. Kits are available through national dairy authorities and regional labs.
  • Lactoperoxidase test: distinguishes high-heat (yoghurt) treatment from standard pasteurisation. Inactivated by 85 C / 30 min a useful confirmation that you achieved yoghurt-grade heat treatment.
  • Post-cooling temperature: must be 45 C at inoculation.

For co-operatives, a simple batch log with time, temperature, operator initials, and phosphatase result is sufficient for buyer audits and for the national dairy regulator (e.g., Kenya Dairy Board, Nigeria's NAFDAC, Uganda's DDA).

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Raw milk received at 8 C and processed within 24 h of milking.
  2. Heat treatment target: 85 C / 30 min (batch) or 9095 C / 25 min (HTST).
  3. Holding temperature continuously recorded; no drop below 85 C.
  4. Alkaline phosphatase test negative on every batch.
  5. Cool to 4245 C in 60 min in a closed system.
  6. Inoculate within 30 min of reaching 45 C; do not let milk sit in the danger zone.
  7. All transfer lines, valves, and tanks sanitised before and after use.
  8. Batch log completed: time, temperature, operator, test results, corrective action.
