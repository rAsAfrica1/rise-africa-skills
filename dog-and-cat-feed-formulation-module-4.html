Lessons

1. Growth and Reproduction Requirements

Puppies and kittens need about twice the energy per kilogram of body weight compared to adults. For a growing puppy, feed 2-3 times the adult maintenance energy requirement (MER). For example, a 5 kg puppy at 8 weeks needs around 500-600 kcal per day, while an adult of same weight needs about 250-300 kcal. Pregnant and lactating females also have high needs: during lactation, a bitch may need 2-4 times her normal food intake. In Africa, use locally available protein sources like fish meal, soybean meal, and poultry by-products. A simple growth diet: 30% protein, 20% fat, 1.0% calcium, 0.8% phosphorus. For kittens, feed a commercial kitten formula or homemade mix of cooked chicken, rice, and egg yolk. Always provide fresh water. Weigh puppies weekly; they should gain 5-10% of birth weight daily. If they don't, increase food. For pregnant females, feed 10% more food in the last third of pregnancy. After birth, keep food available at all times. Avoid overfeeding large breed puppies to prevent bone problems. Use a kitchen scale to measure ingredients. In hot climates, feed early morning and evening to reduce stress. Store feed in airtight containers to prevent mold. Vaccinate and deworm regularly; parasites steal nutrients. This stage sets the foundation for a healthy adult.

2. Adult Maintenance Requirements

Adult dogs and cats need food to maintain weight, not gain or lose. The maintenance energy requirement (MER) for a 10 kg dog is about 400-500 kcal per day; for a 4 kg cat, about 200-250 kcal. A simple formula: MER (kcal/day) = 70 x (body weight in kg)^0.75. For a 10 kg dog, 70 x 10^0.75 = 70 x 5.6 = 392 kcal. Adjust based on activity: inactive dogs need 10-20% less, active dogs 10-20% more. Adult maintenance diets should have 18-25% protein, 10-15% fat, and 0.5-0.8% calcium. In Africa, use affordable proteins like chicken necks, beef offal, and sardines. A homemade recipe: 500g cooked maize meal, 200g cooked meat, 100g vegetables, 1 teaspoon bone meal. Mix and feed according to weight. For cats, they are obligate carnivores: they need taurine, arachidonic acid, and vitamin A from animal sources. Never feed dog food to cats. Feed adult cats twice daily, dogs once or twice. Always provide clean water. Check body condition monthly: you should feel ribs but not see them. If ribs are visible, increase food by 10%. If you can't feel ribs, reduce by 10%. In hot weather, feed less in midday. Use a measuring cup or scale to avoid overfeeding. Store dry food in a cool, dry place. This keeps your pet healthy and saves money.

3. Senior Pet Nutritional Adjustments

Senior dogs and cats (over 7 years for large breeds, over 10 for small breeds and cats) need fewer calories but more high-quality protein. Their metabolism slows, so reduce energy by 10-20%. For a 10 kg senior dog, feed 350-400 kcal per day instead of 400-500. Protein should be 25-30% to maintain muscle. Fat should be 8-12% to prevent obesity. Add joint supplements like glucosamine (500 mg per 10 kg daily) and omega-3 fatty acids (fish oil, 100 mg per kg). In Africa, use soft cooked foods like boiled pumpkin, sweet potato, and minced meat. For senior cats, keep protein at 30-35% and phosphorus low (0.3-0.5%) to protect kidneys. A homemade senior diet: 300g cooked rice, 150g cooked chicken, 50g cooked carrots, 1 teaspoon fish oil. Feed smaller meals more often3-4 times a day. Watch for dental problems: soften kibble with warm water or broth. Provide easy access to water; seniors dehydrate quickly. Weigh monthly; sudden weight loss or gain needs vet attention. Senior pets often have arthritis, so keep food and water bowls low. In hot climates, ensure shade and cool water. Avoid high-salt table scraps. Regular deworming and vaccination are still important. Adjust diet based on vet advice. This stage requires patience and care to keep them comfortable.

4. Feeding for Performance and Work

Working dogsherding, guarding, hunting, or sleddingneed more energy and protein. A moderate working dog needs 1.5-2 times maintenance energy; a heavy worker needs 2-4 times. For a 20 kg working dog, MER is about 700 kcal, so feed 1,400-2,800 kcal per day. Protein should be 25-30%, fat 15-25% for endurance. In Africa, use high-energy ingredients: cooked maize, millet, sorghum, and animal fats like beef tallow or chicken fat. A performance diet: 40% cooked maize, 30% meat meal, 20% cooked eggs, 10% vegetables, plus 1% salt. Feed 2-3 hours before work, and again after cooling down. Always provide water before, during, and after work. For sprinting dogs, add creatine (1-2 g per day) and B-vitamins. For endurance, add electrolytes (a pinch of salt in water). Avoid feeding a heavy meal immediately before work to prevent bloat. In hot climates, work early morning or evening. Monitor body condition weekly; working dogs should have visible waist and ribs. If they lose weight, increase fat and protein. If they gain, reduce. Rest days still need maintenance food. Keep records of work and feeding. This ensures peak performance and long career.

5. Body Condition Scoring and Energy Needs

Body condition score (BCS) is a hands-on tool to check if your pet is too thin, ideal, or overweight. Use a 1-9 scale: 1-3 thin, 4-5 ideal, 6-9 overweight. For dogs, feel the ribs: you should feel them easily but not see them. Look at the waist from abovethere should be a visible waist. For cats, feel the ribs and spine; they should have a slight fat cover. To calculate energy needs, first find resting energy requirement (RER) = 70 x (weight in kg)^0.75. Then multiply by a factor: for weight loss, 0.8-1.0; for maintenance, 1.2-1.4; for growth, 2.0-3.0; for work, 1.5-4.0. For example, a 15 kg dog with BCS 7 (overweight) needs RER = 70 x 15^0.75 = 70 x 7.6 = 532 kcal. For weight loss, feed 0.8 x 532 = 426 kcal per day. Weigh food with a kitchen scale. Adjust every 2 weeks based on BCS. In Africa, use a tape measure for girth and length. Keep a chart. If BCS is 4-5, feed to maintain. If 6-7, reduce by 10-20%. If 8-9, reduce by 20-40% and increase exercise. For cats, never reduce more than 1-2% body weight per week to avoid liver problems. Always provide water. This method prevents obesity and related diseases.

6. Special Considerations for Small and Large Breeds

Small breeds (under 10 kg adult) have faster metabolisms and need more calories per kg. A 5 kg Chihuahua needs about 250-300 kcal per day, while a 5 kg puppy of a large breed needs similar but with different growth rates. Small breeds mature faster (8-12 months) and live longer. Feed small kibble or soft food; they can choke on large pieces. They are prone to dental issues, so offer raw bones or dental chews. Large breeds (over 25 kg adult) grow slowly (18-24 months) and need controlled growth to prevent hip dysplasia. Feed a large-breed puppy formula with 1.0-1.2% calcium and 0.8-1.0% phosphorus. Avoid overfeeding: a 30 kg large breed puppy should gain no more than 1-1.5 kg per week. In Africa, use locally available calcium sources like ground eggshells (1 teaspoon per 500g food). For small breeds, watch for hypoglycemia; feed 3-4 small meals daily. For large breeds, feed 2 meals to reduce bloat risk. Both need fresh water. In hot climates, small breeds overheat quickly; provide shade and cool water. Large breeds need space to exercise. Always check body condition monthly. Adjust food based on BCS. This ensures healthy growth and long life.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Nutritional Requirements and Life Stage Feeding for Dogs and Cats

In African agribusiness, pet food is a fast-growing segment driven by urbanisation, rising middle-class households, and increased demand for companion animal care in cities such as Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Johannesburg, and Cairo. For feed formulators, veterinarians, and entrepreneurs, understanding life stage nutrition is not merely academic it directly affects product performance, client trust, and repeat sales. This practical reference consolidates the key nutritional requirements across life stages, provides actionable tables, and highlights breed-specific and performance considerations relevant to African markets.

Whether you are formulating extruded kibble, wet pouches, or homemade rations for local clients, the principles below will help you meet AAFCO and FEDIAF standards while adapting to locally available ingredients such as poultry by-products, fish meal, maize, sorghum, and insect proteins.

Body Condition Scoring and Energy Needs

Body Condition Score (BCS) is a 9-point scale used to assess fat cover and muscle mass. In African markets where obesity is rising in urban pets, BCS is a powerful tool for selling portion-controlled diets and weight management products. A BCS of 45/9 is ideal for most adult dogs and cats. Each point above ideal represents approximately 1015% excess body weight.

Energy needs can be estimated using the formula: MER = RER Factor, where RER (Resting Energy Requirement) = 70 (BW in kg)^0.75. The table below provides activity factors for different life stages and conditions.

Growth and Reproduction Requirements

Puppies and kittens require nutrient-dense diets with high-quality protein, balanced calcium and phosphorus, and adequate DHA for neurological development. In Africa, where local ingredients like fish meal, poultry meal, and insect meal are increasingly available, formulators should ensure amino acid profiles meet AAFCO minimums. For reproduction, energy demands increase significantly during lactation up to 6 times maintenance in large litters. Folic acid supplementation is critical in early gestation to prevent neural tube defects.

Adult Maintenance Requirements

Adult maintenance diets must provide complete and balanced nutrition. For cats, taurine, arginine, arachidonic acid, and vitamin A are essential deficiencies cause blindness, heart disease, and reproductive failure. Dogs are more flexible but still require balanced Ca:P, essential fatty acids, and adequate fibre. In African markets, affordability drives formulation; however, cutting corners on essential nutrients leads to health problems and lost customers.

Senior Pet Nutritional Adjustments

Senior pets (7+ years) benefit from increased protein (to maintain muscle mass), reduced phosphorus (renal support), added antioxidants (vitamin E, C, beta-carotene), and joint supplements (glucosamine, chondroitin, omega-3). In cats, senior diets should be highly digestible and palatable. Avoid over-restricting protein unless renal disease is confirmed.

Feeding for Performance and Work

Working dogs herding, guarding, sledding, or police dogs have elevated energy needs. Fat is the primary energy source, and diets should contain 1525% fat. Endurance dogs benefit from fat adaptation, while sprint dogs may need higher carbohydrate. Electrolyte replacement is vital in hot African climates. Always provide free access to clean water.

Special Considerations for Small and Large Breeds

Small breeds (e.g., Chihuahua, Maltese) have higher metabolic rates per kg and need energy-dense diets. Large breeds (e.g., Boerboel, Rottweiler) are prone to rapid growth and joint issues; puppies should be fed controlled calcium and energy to prevent developmental orthopaedic disease. In Africa, indigenous breeds like the Basenji and Saluki have unique needs Basenjis are prone to Fanconi syndrome, requiring careful protein and electrolyte management.

SAFETY WARNING:

Never feed dogs or cats raw or undercooked meat from animals that may carry zoonotic pathogens (e.g., Salmonella, E. coli, Toxoplasma). In African contexts where informal meat supply chains exist, always source ingredients from certified abattoirs and apply thermal processing (extrusion, cooking) to achieve pathogen kill. Homemade diets must be formulated with a qualified animal nutritionist unbalanced homemade diets cause severe deficiencies, especially in growing puppies and kittens. Chocolate, xylitol, onions, garlic, grapes, and alcohol are toxic to pets and must never be included in formulations.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Identify life stage: Growth, reproduction, adult, senior, or performance each requires different nutrient targets.
  • Verify Ca:P ratio: 1.21.4:1 for growth; 1:1 to 2:1 for adults.
  • Check taurine for cats: Minimum 0.10% in dry matter; essential for heart and eye health.
  • Calculate energy needs: Use RER factor; adjust for BCS and activity.
  • Source locally: Use fish meal, poultry meal, insect meal, and maize gluten to reduce cost.
  • Test palatability: African pets may prefer stronger flavours; conduct local feeding trials.
  • Label accurately: Follow AAFCO/FEDIAF labelling; include guaranteed analysis and feeding guide.
  • Monitor BCS: Reassess every 46 weeks; adjust portions to maintain ideal body condition.
