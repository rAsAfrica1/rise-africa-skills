1. Types of Steel Used in Farm Buildings

In farm buildings, you will mainly use mild steel because it is strong, weldable, and affordable. Common shapes include I-beams (also called universal beams), channels, angles, square and rectangular hollow sections, and round bars. For a small poultry house, 100x50 mm rectangular hollow section (RHS) with 3 mm wall thickness works well for columns and rafters. For larger cattle sheds, use 150x75 mm I-beams or 200x100 mm channels. Always check for a mill certificate or ask for the steel gradeS275 or S355 are common in Africa. Rebar (deformed bars) is for concrete, not for structural frames, but you can use 12 mm rebar for truss diagonals in light roofs.

When buying steel, inspect for rust, bends, or cracks. A 6-meter length of 100x50 RHS weighs about 9 kg per meter, so a 6 m piece is 54 kgtwo people can carry it. Avoid second-hand steel from old buildings unless you know its history; it may be fatigued. For farm buildings, hot-rolled steel is better than cold-rolled because it is more ductile and forgiving under load. If you are welding, mild steel (S275) is easier than high-strength steel.

In Africa, steel is often sold in 6 m or 12 m lengths. For a typical 10 m x 20 m open shed, you might need 20 columns of 100x100 mm square tubing, 10 rafters of 150x75 mm I-beams, and 200 meters of 50x50 mm angle for purlins. Always order 10% extra for cutting waste. Store steel off the ground on wooden pallets and cover with tarpaulin to prevent rust before construction.

If you cannot afford new steel, consider used oil pipes (API 5L) for columnsthey are strong but hard to weld. For trusses, use 40x40 mm angle or 50x50 mm square tubing. Remember: the roof load (iron sheets, wind, rain) must be carried by the steel. A simple rule: for a 6 m span, use at least 100 mm deep beams; for 10 m span, use 200 mm deep beams. Always consult a structural engineer for spans over 8 m.

2. Fabrication and Welding of Steel Frames

Fabrication means cutting, drilling, and welding steel into frames. You need a welding machine (arc welder, 200-300 amps), angle grinder, measuring tape, square, chalk line, and clamps. For farm buildings, use E6013 electrodes (3.2 mm or 4.0 mm) for general welding. Set the welder to 90-120 amps for 3.2 mm rods. Clean the steel with a grinder before weldingrust and paint cause weak welds.

Start by marking and cutting steel with a cut-off saw or oxy-acetylene torch. For square cuts, use a square and clamp a guide. Drill holes for bolts with a 12 mm drill bit for M12 bolts. When welding a column base plate, tack weld first, check squareness, then weld 50 mm stitches on opposite sides to prevent distortion. For a truss, weld diagonals at 45 degrees to the chord. Use a jig (a flat concrete floor with blocks) to keep everything flat. After welding, chip off slag with a chipping hammer and wire brush.

A common farm frame is a portal frame: two columns and a rafter. Weld a 200x200x10 mm base plate to the bottom of each column. Weld a 10 mm gusset plate at the column-to-rafter connection. For a 6 m span, use 100x50 RHS for columns and 150x75 RHS for rafters. Weld all around the jointdo not just tack. If you are not a certified welder, practice on scrap first. A good weld looks like a stack of coins, with even ripples.

Safety: wear a welding helmet, gloves, apron, and boots. Work in a ventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. After fabrication, grind welds smooth and paint with red oxide primer within 4 hours to prevent rust. For a small poultry house, you can fabricate 4 trusses in one day with two people. Always check diagonal measurementsif the frame is not square, the roof sheets will not fit.

3. Erection of Steel Columns and Beams

Erection is lifting and fixing steel into place. You need a spirit level, plumb bob, spanner set, mobile scaffolding or ladders, and at least three workers. Start with the foundation: concrete pads (600x600x600 mm) with M16 anchor bolts set in a template. The bolts must match the base plate holes. Let concrete cure for 7 days before erecting steel.

Lift the first column with two people and one person guiding the base plate over the bolts. Add nuts finger-tight. Use a spirit level to check vertical in two directions. Adjust with steel shims under the base plate. Tighten nuts to 150 Nm with a torque wrench. Brace the column with temporary diagonal braces (50x50 mm angle) to keep it plumb. Repeat for all columns. For a 20 m x 10 m shed, set columns every 5 m along the length. Check that all columns are in a straight line with a string line.

Next, lift beams or rafters. For a 6 m span, two people can lift a 150x75 RHS rafter. Bolt it to the column top with M16 bolts through pre-drilled holes. Use a crane or a tripod with a chain block for heavier beams. Align the rafter so it is level and square. Weld or bolt purlin cleats (short angles) to the rafter at 1.2 m spacing. Check that the roof slope is correctusually 10 degrees for iron sheets. If the building is in a windy area, add cross-bracing (diagonal rods) between columns.

After all steel is up, re-check plumb and level. Tighten all bolts. Grind off temporary weld tacks and paint scratches with zinc-rich primer. Do not remove temporary braces until purlins and roof sheets are fixed. For a typical farm shed, erection takes 2-3 days for a team of four. Always use a safety harness when working above 2 m.

4. Steel Roof Trusses and Purlins

Roof trusses carry the roof load to the columns. For farm buildings, use simple triangular trusses made from 50x50 mm angle or 40x40 mm square tubing. For a 6 m span, the truss height should be 1.2 m (20% of span). The top chord (rafter) and bottom chord (tie beam) are connected by diagonals. Weld gusset plates at joints. For a 10 m span, use 75x75 mm angle and height 2 m. Trusses are spaced at 3 m to 4 m along the building length.

Purlins are horizontal members that support roof sheets. Use 50x50 mm angle or 100x50 mm C-section (lipped channel). Spacing depends on sheet thickness: for 0.5 mm thick iron sheets, purlins every 1.2 m; for 0.8 mm sheets, every 1.5 m. Bolt purlins to the truss top chord with M12 bolts or weld them. Overlap purlins at joints by 300 mm and bolt through. For a 20 m long roof, you need purlins cut to lengthorder 6 m lengths and cut with a grinder.

Install trusses by lifting them onto columns and bolting. Use a string line to keep them straight. Add sag rods (10 mm round bars) between purlins to prevent twisting. For a 10 m x 20 m poultry house, you need 6 trusses (spaced 4 m) and about 60 purlins (each 6 m long). Weld all connectionsdo not rely on bolts alone for trusses. After installation, check that the roof plane is flat by sighting along purlins. Paint all welds with red oxide. If you are in a high-rainfall area, increase roof slope to 15 degrees and use 0.8 mm sheets.

5. Corrosion Protection for Metal Structures

Steel rusts quickly in Africa due to humidity, rain, and animal waste. Corrosion protection starts with surface preparation. Remove all mill scale, rust, and dirt with a wire brush or angle grinder. For best results, sandblast or use a needle gun. Then apply a primer within 4 hours. Use a zinc-rich primer (epoxy or inorganic zinc) for harsh environments. For farm buildings, a two-coat system works: one coat zinc primer (75 microns dry film thickness) and two coats of acrylic or alkyd topcoat (50 microns each).

For areas in contact with animal waste (e.g., pig pens), use epoxy or polyurethane coatings. Hot-dip galvanizing is best but expensive; it gives 20-30 years protection. If you cannot galvanize, use a cold galvanizing spray (zinc-rich) on welds and cuts. For columns embedded in concrete, wrap the bottom 300 mm with bitumen tape or paint with two coats of epoxy. For roof sheets, use pre-painted galvanized iron (0.5 mm) or aluminum-zinc coated sheets. Avoid direct contact between steel and treated timberuse a plastic shim.

Maintenance: inspect every 6 months. Touch up scratches with zinc spray and topcoat. Keep the bottom of columns clear of soil and manure. In coastal areas, wash steel with fresh water monthly to remove salt. For a typical farm shed, painting takes 2 days for a team of two. Use a brush for edges and a roller for flat surfaces. Do not paint in rainy or very humid weatherpaint will not adhere. Store paint and thinners away from flames. Always wear a respirator when spraying.

6. Metal Cladding and Roofing Installation

Metal cladding includes roof sheets and wall sheets. In Africa, common sheets are galvanized iron (IBS), aluminum-zinc (AZ), and pre-painted. For farm buildings, use 0.5 mm thick sheets for roofs and 0.4 mm for walls. Sheet lengths are custom-cut to cover from ridge to eave in one pieceavoid overlaps. For a 6 m rafter, order 6.5 m sheets to allow 200 mm overhang. Fix sheets with self-drilling screws (12-14 x 50 mm) with neoprene washers. Use one screw per sheet per purlin, placed in the crest (top of corrugation) not the valley.

Start at the leeward end (opposite to prevailing wind) and work towards the wind. Overlap sheets by one corrugation for roofs and two for walls. For roof sheets, overlap the lower sheet over the higher sheet. Use a string line to keep the first sheet straight. Drill screws with a screw gun or variable speed drill. Do not over-tightenthe washer should just compress. For a 20 m x 10 m roof, you need about 200 screws. Cut sheets with a nibbler or aviation snipsnever use a grinder (it damages the coating).

For walls, fix sheets to girts (horizontal angles) at 1.2 m spacing. Leave a 10 mm gap at the bottom for ventilation. For ridge, use a ridge cap (bent sheet) fixed with screws every 300 mm. For eaves, use a barge board or flashing. After installation, check for leaks by spraying water. Replace any screws that strip. For a poultry house, use insulated panels or double-skin sheets to reduce heat. In hot climates, use light-colored sheets (white or silver) to reflect heat. Always wear gloves when handling sheetsedges are sharp.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Steel & Metal Structures for Farm Buildings

Steel is the backbone of modern farm construction across Africa from poultry houses in Ghana and grain stores in Kenya to cattle handling facilities in Botswana. For the agribusiness operator, steel offers speed of erection, long spans for machinery access, and resale value at end of life. But it also demands disciplined specification, fabrication, and maintenance. This practical reference condenses the essentials from the six lessons in this module into a desk-side guide you can use on site or in the procurement office.

Why Steel Dominates African Farm Buildings

Timber is increasingly scarce and expensive near African cities; concrete is slow and capital-heavy. Steel sections can be fabricated in a local workshop, trucked to a rural site, and erected in days. For a 500-bird layer house or a 200-tonne maize store, steel frames typically cost 1525% more than timber at purchase but last 35 times longer with proper corrosion protection a decisive advantage where humidity, termites, or bushfire risk is high.

Specifying the Right Steel

Most farm buildings in Africa use hot-rolled mild steel sections to EN 10025 S275JR or its regional equivalents (e.g., BS 4360 Grade 43A, or South African SANS 1431). For corrosive environments coastal Kenya, Niger Delta, or poultry houses with ammonia-laden air consider S355 with higher yield strength to reduce section sizes, or galvanised sections where budget allows.

Key section types you will encounter:

  • Universal beams (UB) and columns (UC) main portal frames, spans 615 m.
  • Angles and channels purlins, bracing, truss members.
  • Rectangular hollow sections (RHS) trusses and columns where appearance matters; easier to seal against corrosion.
  • Cold-formed light gauge sections purlins, side rails, and light trusses for spans under 8 m.
  • Reinforcement bar (Y10Y16) truss tension members in low-cost designs, though not ideal where humidity is high.

Fabrication & Welding: Getting It Right in the Workshop

Fabrication quality determines whether your building stands for 30 years or 5. Key controls:

  • Drawing discipline: Every frame must have a fabrication drawing showing member sizes, weld types, hole positions, and camber. In rural workshops, hand sketches cause expensive site rework.
  • Welding: Use E70XX electrodes (e.g., E7018) for structural welds. Minimum fillet weld leg length = 0.7 thinner plate thickness. All welds on tension members should be continuous, not tacked.
  • Bolted connections: Preferred on site M16 or M20 Grade 8.8 bolts in 18 mm or 22 mm holes. Torque to 200300 Nm depending on diameter.
  • Drilling: Never flame-cut holes for structural bolts; tolerance is 2 mm.
  • Trial erection: Assemble one bay in the workshop before transport. It costs a day and saves a week.

SAFETY WARNING WELDING & ERECTION

Welding fumes contain manganese and hexavalent chromium. In enclosed farm workshops, forced ventilation or respirators are mandatory. During erection, never work under a suspended load; use tag lines. In lightning-prone regions (most of sub-Saharan Africa), do not erect steel frames during thunderstorms steel columns act as lightning conductors. Ensure all site workers wear hard hats, safety boots, and fall arrest harnesses above 2 m. A single fatality can destroy a family agribusiness.

Erection: From Foundation to Frame

Steel columns must be set on concrete pad foundations typically 600600400 mm for a 6 m bay, reinforced with Y12 bars. Anchor bolts (M20 300 mm cast-in or chemical anchors) must be set to 3 mm tolerance using a template. Base plates are typically 25025010 mm.

Erection sequence:

  1. Set out foundations and check diagonals (equal diagonals = square).
  2. Position base plates and grout after columns are plumbed.
  3. Erect two columns and one rafter as a "goalpost" brace temporarily.
  4. Add purlins and permanent bracing before releasing temporary guys.
  5. Plumb and align the full frame before final bolt tightening.

Roof Trusses, Purlins & Cladding

For spans over 8 m, a truss is more economical than a solid rafter. Common farm trusses: Fink (span 612 m), Howe (span 1018 m), and Pratt (span 1220 m). Purlins at 1.21.8 m centres support cladding; for 0.5 mm IBR sheeting, use 1.5 m maximum spacing to prevent sag.

Cladding choices in Africa:

  • IBR (Inverted Box Rib) most common, cheap, good for roofs.
  • Corrugated traditional, more overlap, higher fixing count.
  • Chromadek / Colorbond pre-painted, 1525 year life, best for poultry and dairy.
  • Insulated sandwich panels higher cost, essential for climate-controlled poultry.

Fix with self-drilling screws at every second crest, with EPDM washers. Side laps: one corrugation for corrugated, one rib for IBR. End laps: 150 mm minimum, laid with prevailing wind.

Corrosion Protection: The Profit Protector

Corrosion is the single largest cause of premature steel failure on African farms. In coastal zones, unprotected steel can lose 0.1 mm/year. Protection options, in order of cost-effectiveness:

  1. Hot-dip galvanising 85 m coating gives 2025 years inland, 1015 years coastal. Best for columns and trusses.
  2. Epoxy / polyurethane paint systems 200 m DFT, 1015 year life, repaintable.
  3. Zinc-rich primer + topcoat cheaper, 58 years, requires regular maintenance.
  4. Powder coating good for RHS, less suitable for site-welded joints.

In poultry houses, ammonia attacks steel aggressively. Use galvanised or stainless fixings, and never mix galvanised and bare steel galvanic corrosion will destroy the joint.

Business-First Takeaway

For a 200 m broiler house in West Africa, a steel portal frame costs roughly USD 8,00012,000 in materials, versus USD 15,00020,000 for a comparable concrete frame. The steel option can be erected in 1014 days versus 68 weeks for concrete, meaning birds can be housed a full cycle earlier often worth USD 3,0005,000 in additional revenue. Protect your investment with galvanising and correct fixings, and the frame will outlast three production cycles of any competitor using timber.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm steel grade (S275JR or better) and mill certificates before fabrication.
  2. Check anchor bolt positions with a template tolerance 3 mm.
  3. Verify weld sizes: fillet leg 0.7 thinner plate; continuous on tension members.
  4. Use Grade 8.8 bolts; never substitute mild steel bolts on structural joints.
  5. Ensure purlin spacing 1.5 m for 0.5 mm sheeting.
  6. Confirm galvanising thickness 85 m, or paint DFT 200 m.
  7. Fix cladding with EPDM washered screws; every second crest minimum.
  8. Inspect and touch up all site welds and scratches before handover.
