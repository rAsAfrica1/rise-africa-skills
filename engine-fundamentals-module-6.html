Lessons

1. Why a Pump Engine Overheats Faster Than a Car Engine

When a car engine drives a pump, it runs at a steady, high load for hours, often in the hot sun. In a car, the engine gets air flow from the vehicle's motion, but a stationary pump engine sits still. The radiator relies only on the fan, and if that fan is weak or missing, heat builds up fast. Also, pump engines often run at 1500-1800 RPM continuously, which is higher than idle but lower than highway speeds, so the water pump may turn slower than designed. In Africa, ambient temperatures can exceed 40C, and dusty air clogs the radiator fins. Without proper cooling, the engine will overheat within 20-30 minutes, leading to cracked heads, blown gaskets, and seized pistons. You must adapt the cooling system to handle this constant heavy load. This module shows you how to modify the radiator, fan, and coolant flow to keep the engine at 80-90C, the safe operating range. We'll cover fan selection, radiator cleaning, thermostat removal, and adding a water tank. By the end, you'll be able to run your pump for 8 hours straight without overheating.

2. Removing the Thermostat and Restricting Bypass

In a car, the thermostat stays closed until the engine warms up, then opens to let coolant flow to the radiator. For a pump engine, you want full coolant flow all the time, because the engine is always under load. Remove the thermostat completely. On most engines, it's located in a housing where the upper radiator hose connects to the engine block. Unbolt the housing (usually two 12mm bolts), take out the thermostat, and either leave it out or install a blanking sleeve. But be careful: some engines have a bypass hose that lets coolant circulate through the block without going to the radiator. If you remove the thermostat, too much coolant may bypass the radiator, causing overheating. You need to block that bypass. Look for a small hose (about 20mm diameter) running from the water pump to the intake manifold or cylinder head. Plug it with a brass cap or a bolt clamped inside the hose. Alternatively, some thermostats have a bypass block built in; if you remove it, you must restrict the bypass. A simple method is to insert a metal plate with a 5mm hole in the bypass line. This allows some flow for warm-up but forces most coolant through the radiator. Test by running the engine and feeling the upper hose; it should get hot quickly. If it stays cold, coolant isn't flowing. Always use new gaskets and sealant when reassembling.

3. Upgrading the Radiator and Fan for Stationary Use

The original car radiator may be too small for continuous pump duty. If your engine is from a small car (1.0-1.6L), the radiator might handle it, but for larger engines (2.0L and above), you need a bigger radiator. Visit a scrapyard and find a radiator from a truck or a larger car, like a Toyota Hilux or Nissan Hardbody. Ensure it has similar hose connection sizes (usually 32mm and 38mm). If you can't find one, you can add a second radiator in parallel, but that's complex. More important is the fan. A mechanical fan driven by the water pump belt is often inadequate when stationary. Install an electric fan from a car air conditioner or a universal 12V fan. Mount it on the radiator with a shroud to pull air through the entire core. A 16-inch fan pulling 2000 CFM is good for most conversions. Wire it to a relay and a manual switch, or better, a thermostatic switch that turns on at 85C. Also, clean the radiator fins with a soft brush and compressed air; dust and mud block airflow. In dusty conditions, consider adding a screen in front of the radiator. Finally, ensure the radiator cap holds pressure: a 0.9 bar cap raises the boiling point to about 120C, which helps prevent boil-over.

4. Adding a Water Tank and Overflow System

A car's cooling system is sealed and uses a small overflow bottle. For a pump engine, you need a larger reserve because you'll be running for hours and may lose coolant through minor leaks or evaporation. Get a 20-liter plastic jerrycan or a metal tank. Connect it to the radiator overflow port with a hose. The tank should have a vent to atmosphere and a pickup tube that reaches the bottom. When the engine cools, coolant is sucked back into the radiator. This is called a recovery system. Make sure the radiator cap is a recovery type (it has a rubber seal at the top). If you use a standard cap, it won't suck back. Also, check for leaks regularly. A small leak can drop the coolant level and cause overheating. Use a 50/50 mix of antifreeze and water. Antifreeze raises the boiling point and prevents corrosion, which is important in older engines. In areas where water is hard, use distilled water if possible. If you can't get antifreeze, use clean rainwater and add a corrosion inhibitor. Never use plain borehole water without treatment; minerals will clog the radiator. Check the coolant level every morning before starting. Top up to the cold mark on the tank. During operation, watch the temperature gauge; if it climbs above 95C, stop and check for problems.

5. Flushing and Cleaning the Cooling System

A used car engine likely has rust, scale, and sludge in the cooling passages. Before you run your pump, flush the system thoroughly. Start by draining the old coolant. Open the drain plug at the bottom of the radiator and the block drain (often a 14mm bolt on the side of the engine block). Collect the old coolant in a pan; it's toxic, so dispose of it properly. Then fill the system with clean water and a flushing chemical, like a citric acid flush or a commercial radiator cleaner. Run the engine for 15 minutes with the radiator cap off, heater on full (if you have one), and then drain. Repeat until the water comes out clear. For heavy rust, you may need to remove the radiator and take it to a radiator shop for a professional rodding. If that's not possible, you can backflush with a garden hose: disconnect the upper hose and force water through the radiator from the bottom outlet. Also, remove the water pump and inspect the impeller; if it's corroded or broken, replace it. A new water pump costs about $30-50 and is worth it. Check all hoses for cracks and soft spots; replace any that are questionable. Use new hose clamps. After flushing, fill with a 50/50 mix of ethylene glycol antifreeze and clean water. Bleed air from the system by running the engine with the cap off until the thermostat opens (but remember, you removed it, so just run until no bubbles appear). Top up and close.

6. Monitoring Temperature and Preventing Overheat

You need to know the engine temperature at all times. Install a mechanical or electric temperature gauge if your engine doesn't have one. The sensor goes into the cylinder head or thermostat housing. A good gauge has numbers, not just a red light. Normal operating temperature is 80-90C. If it reaches 100C, stop the engine and let it cool. Common causes of overheating in pump engines: a slipping fan belt (check tension, should deflect 10-15mm), a stuck closed thermostat (but you removed it), a clogged radiator, a failed water pump, or low coolant. Always carry spare coolant and a spare fan belt. In hot climates, consider adding a second electric fan or a larger radiator. You can also reduce load by lowering pump speed if possible. Use a laser thermometer to check temperatures at the radiator inlet and outlet; the difference should be about 10C. If the outlet is much cooler, the radiator is working. If both are hot, the radiator is not dissipating heat. Also, listen for the fan running; if it's not, check the fuse and relay. Keep the radiator fins clean by blowing them out with compressed air every week. Never remove the radiator cap while the engine is hot; steam and boiling water can burn you. Let it cool for at least 30 minutes. By following these steps, your pump engine will run reliably for many seasons.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Cooling System Specifications, Temperature Limits and Maintenance Schedule



This Practical Reference consolidates the hard numbers you need when converting a car engine into a stationary irrigation pump. Unlike a vehicle, a stationary engine has no ram airflow, runs at constant load for hours, and often sits in 3542 C ambient heat. The cooling system therefore becomes the single most important determinant of engine life, fuel efficiency per cubic metre of water lifted, and the reliability your farm business depends on. Use the tables, diagram and checklist below as your workshop reference sheet. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in the pump house.

SAFETY WARNING PRESSURISED HOT COOLANT

Never open a radiator cap while the engine is hot. Pressurised coolant at 90100 C will flash to steam and cause severe scalding burns to the face, arms and torso. Allow the engine to cool for at least 30 minutes before removing the cap, and cover the cap with a thick cloth while turning it to the first stop to release residual pressure slowly. Ethylene glycol is toxic if swallowed store coolant in clearly labelled containers away from children, livestock and drinking water sources. If coolant is swallowed, seek medical attention immediately and contact your nearest poison information centre. Dispose of used coolant at an approved hazardous waste facility; never pour it onto soil or into irrigation channels.




Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Check coolant level daily before starting top up only when the engine is cold, using the correct glycol mix.

  2. Confirm the thermostat is fitted and functioning never run permanently without one; it regulates warm-up and flow.

  3. Verify the bypass orifice is 58 mm too large wastes cooling capacity, too small causes hot spots.

  4. Inspect the radiator fins weekly blow out dust with compressed air; a blocked core is the number one cause of pump-set overheating.

  5. Check fan belt tension and fan operation the electric fan must cut in automatically; test the switch monthly.

  6. Monitor the temperature gauge continuously investigate any reading above 95 C within two minutes.

  7. Inspect hoses, clamps and the expansion tank monthly for cracks, swelling or leaks.

  8. Record every top-up and flush in a logbook unexplained coolant loss signals a head gasket or water pump failure.
