Practical Reference

This section converts the theory in the module above into tools you can use on the farm, in the store, or at the market gate. Everything here is written for operators who need to move product, hit a margin, and stay compliant. Use the tables to benchmark your own numbers, the diagram to understand where value leaks out of the chain, and the checklist as a weekly discipline. Where figures are given in Kenyan Shillings (KES) or US Dollars (USD), convert to your local currency using the prevailing rate and adjust for your own cost base.

Reading the Numbers Like an Operator

The two tables above are meant to be used together. Table 1 tells you where you are losing physical product. Table 2 tells you what each recovered percentage point is worth in cash. A 10-tonne maize lot at 8% storage loss is roughly 9 bags gone â at a KES 3,400 selling price, that is KES 30,600 of revenue you never see. A KES 4,000 hermetic liner that eliminates most of that loss pays for itself in the first cycle. This is the simplest form of margin improvement available to an African aggregator, and it requires no new market, no new customer, and no additional land.

The same logic applies to tomato crates versus sacks, to milk cooling within two hours of milking, and to cassava processing within 24 hours of harvest. In every case the business decision is the same: compare the cost of the intervention against the value of the product you are currently throwing away. If the ratio is better than 1:3 in the first season, act.

â  SAFETY WARNING â AFLATOXIN AND AGROCHEMICALS

Maize, groundnuts, and other staples can carry aflatoxin, a mould toxin that causes liver cancer and stunting in children. It is invisible and has no taste. Never sell, feed to livestock, or blend grain that has been stored wet, shows mould, or smells musty. Drying to below 13.5% moisture and using hermetic storage are the primary controls. Test with a rapid aflatoxin kit before any bulk sale to a processor or exporter.

When handling pesticides, fumigants, or fertilisers: wear gloves, a mask, and long sleeves; never eat, drink, or smoke during application; store chemicals locked away from food and children; and observe the full pre-harvest interval on the label. If someone swallows a pesticide, call your local poison control centre or the nearest health facility immediately and take the container with you. Do not induce vomiting unless a health worker tells you to.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Measure moisture before storage. Buy or borrow a moisture meter. Maize and beans should be at or below 13.5% before bagging. If you cannot measure, the salt-in-a-bottle test is a rough fallback but not a substitute.
  • Buy hermetic storage, not just bags. PICS or GrainPro liners inside standard bags cut insect damage and aflatoxin risk. Amortise the cost across at least three seasons in your unit economics.
  • Track every bag in and every bag out. A simple notebook or a free app like FarmDrive or DigiFarm is enough. If you do not measure loss, you cannot price it.
  • Aggregate with neighbours before you buy inputs. A 10-tonne lot gets a better transport rate, a better storage cost per bag, and access to buyers who will not talk to a 1-tonne seller.
  • Know your buyer before you store. Off-season premiums only exist if you have a contract, a processor, or a confirmed market. Storage without a buyer is speculation.
  • Cool milk within two hours. If you have no tank, use a water bath with ice or a shaded ventilated can. Every hour of delay costs you litres and price.
  • Process perishables within 24 hours. Cassava, tomato, and fresh milk do not wait. Build the processing or cooling step into your harvest calendar, not your emergency plan.
  • Keep a chemical and safety log. Record every application: product, rate, date, operator, and pre-harvest interval. This protects your market access and your family.
