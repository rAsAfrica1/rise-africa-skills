1. Milling Wheat into Flour for Local Bakeries

Milling wheat into flour for local bakeries starts with clean grain. You need wheat that is dry (12-14% moisture) and free of stones, dirt, and broken kernels. A simple hand sieve or a mechanical cleaner can remove debris. If you don't have a cleaner, winnowing works: pour grain from one bucket to another in a breeze. Next, you need a mill. For small-scale, a diesel-powered hammer mill or a plate mill costs between $500 and $2,000. These mills can produce 100-500 kg of flour per hour. Set the mill to a fine setting for bakery flour. You want about 70-75% extraction rate: from 100 kg wheat, you get 70-75 kg white flour and 25-30 kg bran and germ. Bran and germ can be sold as animal feed. After milling, sift the flour through a fine mesh (like a 60-mesh sieve) to remove larger particles. This gives you a smoother flour. For local bakeries, package flour in 50 kg or 25 kg woven polypropylene bags. Label the bags with your brand, production date, and weight. Store in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. Flour absorbs moisture and odors, so keep it off the floor on pallets. If you want to sell to bigger bakeries, you may need to fortify flour with vitamins and minerals, but that requires special equipment. For a start, focus on consistent quality: same grind, same sifting, same packaging. Test your flour by baking a small loaf: if it rises well and has good texture, your flour is good. Price your flour competitively: check local market prices for wheat flour. In many African countries, a 50 kg bag of wheat flour sells for $30-$50. Your profit margin comes from buying wheat cheaply (e.g., $300-$400 per tonne) and selling flour at a higher price per kg. Remember to keep records of costs and sales. With steady production, you can supply nearby bakeries, restaurants, and even households. Start small, maybe 200 kg per day, and grow as you build a customer base.

2. Making Barley Malt for Home Brewing or Breweries

Making barley malt turns raw barley into a sweet, fermentable grain used for brewing beer or making malt drinks. You need good quality barley, preferably two-row varieties with high germination rate (over 95%). Start by cleaning the barley: remove stones, chaff, and broken kernels. Then steep the barley in water. Use a large bucket or tank. For 50 kg barley, use about 100 liters of water. Soak for 40-48 hours, changing water every 8-12 hours. The barley will absorb water and swell. After steeping, drain the water and spread the barley in a thin layer (5-10 cm deep) on a clean concrete floor or in shallow trays. This is called germination. Keep the grain moist and turn it every 4-6 hours to prevent mould. Germination takes 4-6 days at 15-20C. You'll see small rootlets (white sprouts) emerge. The grain is ready when the rootlet is about 1-1.5 times the length of the kernel. At this point, stop germination by drying the malt. You can sun-dry on a clean mat for 2-3 days, or use a solar dryer. For better flavour, you can kiln-dry: heat the malt at 50-60C for 24 hours, then increase to 80-100C for 2-4 hours if you want darker malt. After drying, remove the rootlets by rubbing the malt over a screen or in a sack. The rootlets are sold as animal feed. Store malt in dry sacks in a cool, dry place. For home brewing, you can grind the malt coarsely and mash it with hot water (65-68C) to convert starches to sugars. For breweries, they often buy malt in bulk and may have specific quality specs: moisture below 5%, protein 10-12%, and high enzyme activity. You can sell malt to local breweries or home brewers. Price: malt sells for $0.50-$1.50 per kg depending on quality and market. Start with 50 kg batches and scale up. Always keep records: weight of barley in, weight of malt out. A 50 kg batch yields about 40 kg malt after rootlet removal. With practice, you can produce consistent malt that meets buyer needs.

3. Producing Animal Feed from Screenings and Straw

After harvesting wheat and barley, you have screenings (small, broken grains, chaff) and straw. These can be turned into valuable animal feed. Screenings are often higher in protein and energy than straw, so they are better for poultry and pigs. Straw is high in fibre and better for cattle, goats, and sheep. Start by collecting screenings from your cleaning process. If you buy grain, ask the seller for screenings; often they are cheap or free. For straw, bale it after harvest and store under cover to keep it dry. To make feed, you can mix screenings with other ingredients. For poultry, a simple mix: 50% ground maize, 30% screenings, 10% soybean meal, 5% fish meal, 5% minerals and vitamins. This gives about 18% protein. For pigs, use 40% maize, 30% screenings, 15% soybean meal, 10% sunflower cake, 5% minerals. For cattle, you can chop straw and mix with molasses and urea to improve digestibility. A common recipe: 100 kg chopped straw, 5 kg molasses, 1 kg urea, 2 kg mineral premix, and 20 liters of water. Mix well and let it ferment for 24 hours before feeding. This increases protein content. You can also pellet feed using a small pellet machine ($500-$1,500). Pellets reduce waste and are easier to store. Package feed in 50 kg bags. Label with ingredients and protein content. Price feed competitively: poultry feed sells for $0.40-$0.70 per kg, cattle feed $0.20-$0.40 per kg. You can sell to local farmers, feed stores, or use it for your own livestock. Always ensure feed is free of mould; store in dry place. Test for aflatoxin if possible. With simple equipment, you can add value to what would otherwise be waste. Start with 100 kg batches and adjust based on customer feedback. Keep records of costs and sales to know your profit.

4. Packaging and Branding Your Flour or Malt

Packaging and branding your flour or malt helps you stand out and sell for a better price. Start with simple, clean packaging. For flour, use woven polypropylene bags for 25 kg or 50 kg. For smaller retail, use paper bags or plastic bags of 1 kg, 2 kg, or 5 kg. For malt, use similar bags or airtight containers for home brewers. Buy bags in bulk to reduce cost: a 50 kg woven bag costs about $0.30-$0.50, while a 1 kg printed plastic bag costs $0.05-$0.10. You can print your brand name, logo, weight, production date, and contact number on the bags. If you can't afford printing, use stickers or stencils. A simple logo can be designed for free using apps like Canva. Choose a name that is easy to remember and reflects your product. For example, 'Golden Wheat Flour' or 'Savanna Malt'. Include a tagline like 'Quality You Can Trust'. Register your business name with the local authorities to protect it. For food safety, your packaging must be food-grade. Never use bags that previously held chemicals or cement. Store packaged goods in a clean, dry room away from sunlight. Stack bags on pallets, not directly on the floor. For branding, tell your story: 'Made from locally grown wheat' or 'No additives'. This appeals to customers. You can also offer different sizes: 1 kg for households, 25 kg for bakeries. Price your packaged products higher than bulk: a 1 kg bag of flour might sell for $0.80-$1.20, while a 50 kg bag sells for $30-$50. Marketing: use word of mouth, social media (WhatsApp, Facebook), and local markets. Give free samples to potential buyers. Consistent quality and clean packaging build trust. Over time, your brand becomes known, and you can charge a premium. Keep records of packaging costs and adjust prices as needed.

5. Quality Control: Moisture, Ash, and Protein Tests

Quality control ensures your flour or malt meets buyer expectations. Three key tests are moisture, ash, and protein. Moisture: too high moisture causes mould; too low makes flour dry. For flour, aim for 12-14% moisture. For malt, below 5%. To test moisture without a lab, use a simple moisture meter ($50-$200). If you don't have one, use the oven method: weigh 10 g sample, dry in oven at 130C for 1 hour, cool, and reweigh. Moisture % = (initial weight - final weight) / initial weight x 100. Ash: ash indicates mineral content and bran contamination. For white flour, ash should be 0.4-0.6%. For whole wheat, 1.5-2.0%. To test ash, burn a sample in a furnace at 550C for 4 hours, then weigh the ash. This requires a furnace, so small processors can send samples to a lab. Protein: protein affects baking quality and malt enzyme activity. For bread flour, protein should be 11-13%. For malt, 10-12%. Use a protein tester ($200-$500) or send to lab. A simple test: mix flour with water to make dough, wash under running water to remove starch, and weigh the remaining gluten. Gluten content is roughly 80% of protein. For malt, you can test diastatic power (enzyme activity) by mashing a sample and checking sugar conversion. Keep records of each batch. If moisture is too high, dry the grain more before milling. If ash is too high, sift more to remove bran. If protein is low, blend with higher-protein wheat. For malt, ensure germination is even. Regular testing helps you maintain consistency. Buyers, especially breweries and bakeries, will ask for specs. Having test results builds trust. You can also use simple sensory tests: smell (fresh, no musty odour), touch (smooth, no lumps), and taste (neutral). Start with basic moisture and gluten tests, and add more as you grow. Document everything: date, batch number, results. This helps you trace problems and improve.

6. Licensing and Food Safety Basics for Small Processors

Licensing and food safety are essential for selling your flour, malt, or feed legally and safely. Start by registering your business. In most African countries, you need a business name registration from the registrar of companies. Cost: $20-$100. Then get a food handling license from the local health department or food safety authority. Requirements vary, but usually include a health certificate for workers, a clean production area, and proper waste disposal. For example, in Kenya, you need a food safety permit from the county government. In Nigeria, you need a NAFDAC registration for packaged food. Fees range from $50-$500. Your production area must meet basic hygiene: clean floors, walls, and equipment. Have handwashing stations with soap and water. Workers must wear clean clothes, hairnets, and gloves. No eating or smoking in production area. Pest control: keep rodents and insects out. Use traps and screens. Store raw materials and finished products separately. Water must be clean; if not, treat it. For food safety, follow these key practices: 1) Clean and sanitize equipment after each use. 2) Keep records of production, cleaning, and pest control. 3) Label products with date and batch number. 4) Store at correct temperature and humidity. 5) Train workers on hygiene. For flour and malt, ensure no contamination from chemicals, glass, or metal. Use metal detectors if possible, or visually inspect. For animal feed, ensure no mould; test for aflatoxin if you can. If you sell to breweries or large bakeries, they may require HACCP or ISO certification, which is more advanced. Start with basic compliance, then upgrade. Keep your license renewed annually. Display it in your shop or production area. Food safety also means traceability: if a problem occurs, you can recall a batch. Keep records of where you sold each batch. This protects your customers and your business. Finally, get liability insurance if available. It may seem costly, but it protects you from lawsuits. With proper licensing and safety, you can sell to formal markets and grow your business confidently.

<h2>Practical Reference: Value Addition and Processing for Wheat &amp; Barley in Africa</h2>

<p>Value addition is where the real money is made in wheat and barley farming. A tonne of wheat sold as grain might fetch USD 250350, but milled into flour and packaged for local bakeries, that same tonne can generate USD 600900 in revenue. Barley transformed into malt for breweries or home brewing can triple in value. This practical reference module covers the essential technical, business, and regulatory knowledge you need to start or improve a small-scale processing operation in Africa. Whether you are milling wheat for neighbourhood bakeries, malting barley for craft brewers, or producing animal feed from screenings and straw, the principles of quality control, packaging, and food safety licensing are the same. This lesson gives you the numbers, the process steps, and the official links to get started.</p>

<h3>1. Milling Wheat into Flour for Local Bakeries</h3>

<p>Small-scale milling in Africa typically uses hammer mills, plate mills, or roller mills. For bakery-quality flour, a roller mill is preferred because it separates bran, germ, and endosperm more cleanly. The extraction ratethe percentage of flour obtained from clean wheatranges from 7075% for white flour to 8595% for whole wheat flour. In many African markets, whole wheat and "brown" flour are growing in demand due to health awareness and lower production costs. A 500 kg/hour roller mill can serve 2030 small bakeries and generate USD 1,5003,000 monthly gross profit, depending on local wheat and flour prices.</p>

<h3>2. Making Barley Malt for Home Brewing or Breweries</h3>

<p>Malting is a controlled germination and drying process that develops enzymes (especially amylase) needed for brewing. The steps are: steeping (soaking barley in water for 4048 hours), germination (spreading grain on floors or in drums for 46 days at 1520C), kilning (drying with hot air at 5080C to stop germination), and deculming (removing rootlets). In Africa, small malting operations in Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa supply craft breweries and home brewers. A 1-tonne batch of barley can yield 800850 kg of malt, which sells for USD 400700 per tonne to craft brewerssignificantly more than raw barley at USD 200300 per tonne.</p>

<h3>3. Producing Animal Feed from Screenings and Straw</h3>

<p>Wheat and barley screenings (broken grains, weed seeds, chaff) and straw are valuable feed resources. A simple feed production setup involves grinding screenings, mixing with straw meal, molasses, and mineral premix, then pelletising or bagging as mash. Typical formulation for ruminants: 40% screenings, 40% straw meal, 15% molasses, 5% mineral premix. This feed can be sold to dairy and beef farmers at USD 150250 per tonne, turning waste into profit. In Ethiopia, Kenya, and Tanzania, small feed mills using this model have reduced feed costs for livestock keepers by 2030%.</p>

<h3>4. Packaging and Branding Your Flour or Malt</h3>

<p>Packaging protects your product from moisture, pests, and contamination, and branding builds trust and repeat sales. Use food-grade woven polypropylene (WPP) bags with inner liners for 25 kg and 50 kg flour; laminated foil or PET bags for 1 kg and 5 kg retail packs. Label must include: product name, net weight, ingredients, production date, expiry date (612 months for flour, 12 months for malt), batch number, and your contact. In many African countries, a simple printed label with a local-language brand name and a phone number is enough to enter markets. Invest in a distinctive logo and consistent coloursthis is how you compete with imported brands.</p>

<h3>5. Quality Control: Moisture, Ash, and Protein Tests</h3>

<p>Three tests are essential for flour and malt quality:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Moisture:</strong> Should be 1214% for flour, 46% for malt. High moisture causes mould and spoilage. Test with a moisture meter or oven-drying method.</li>
<li><strong>Ash:</strong> Indicates mineral content and extraction rate. White flour: 0.40.6%; whole wheat: 1.52.0%. Higher ash means more bran.</li>
<li><strong>Protein:</strong> Determines baking quality. Bread wheat: 1114%; biscuit wheat: 810%. Barley malt: 912%. Test with Kjeldahl or near-infrared (NIR) methods.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Aflatoxin and mycotoxin contamination is a serious risk in stored grains and flour, especially in humid African climates. Never use mouldy or discoloured grain for flour or malt production. Moisture must be kept below 14% for flour and below 6% for malt. Store in dry, ventilated conditions. Aflatoxin is heat-stable and survives baking and brewing. Regular testing (using ELISA kits or lab analysis) is mandatory before selling any product. Contaminated product must be destroyednever sold or fed to animals.</p>

<h3>6. Licensing and Food Safety Basics for Small Processors</h3>

<p>In most African countries, you need: (1) business registration (sole proprietorship or limited company), (2) food safety licence from the national food authority (e.g., Kenya Bureau of Standards, Ghana FDA, Nigeria NAFDAC, South Africa NRCS), (3) premises inspection approval, (4) product testing and certification, and (5) tax registration. The process typically takes 26 months and costs USD 2001,000 depending on the country. Key food safety practices: clean premises, pest control, personal hygiene, safe water, and traceability (batch records). Many countries offer simplified licensing for micro and small enterprisesask your local trade or agriculture office.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Test grain moisture before milling or malting (14% flour, 6% malt).</li>
<li> Clean and temper wheat for 1224 hours before milling.</li>
<li> Steep barley for 4048 hours, germinate 46 days, kiln at 5080C.</li>
<li> Test flour for ash and protein to meet bakery specifications.</li>
<li> Use food-grade packaging with full labelling (weight, date, batch, contact).</li>
<li> Register your business and obtain food safety licence before selling.</li>
<li> Keep batch records for traceability and recall if needed.</li>
<li> Never sell mouldy or aflatoxin-contaminated products.</li>
</ol>
