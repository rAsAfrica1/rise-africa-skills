1. Identifying Your Target Customers

Start by looking at who farms near you. In most African districts, you will find three main groups: smallholder farmers with 1â5 acres who grow maize, beans, cassava or vegetables; medium farmers with 5â20 acres who often keep poultry or dairy; and commercial growers who supply hotels, schools or export crops. Each group has different needs. A smallholder may buy one 50 kg bag of compost for a vegetable bed. A medium farmer may buy 20 bags for a maize field. A commercial grower may order 2 tonnes for a greenhouse. Your job is to choose one or two groups to serve first, not everyone.

Walk or ride through your area and count farms. Talk to at least 20 farmers. Ask: What crops do you grow? What fertiliser do you use now? How much do you pay per bag? What problems do you have with the soil? Write the answers in a notebook. You will quickly see who is already buying fertiliser, who has cash after harvest, and who complains about poor yields. Farmers who already spend money on NPK or urea are your best first customers because they understand the value of fertiliser.

Also identify the influencers. These are the lead farmers, extension officers, church leaders and agro-dealer shop owners whom others trust. If a lead farmer uses your compost and gets results, ten neighbours will ask about it. Visit the local agricultural extension officer and the nearest agro-dealer. Ask what farmers complain about and when they have money. In many areas, cash comes after maize harvest in JuneâJuly or after cocoa, coffee or cotton sales. Plan your selling around those months.

Write a simple customer profile for your top group. Example: âMaize farmers in Chikwawa with 2â5 acres, buying 2â10 bags per season, paying cash after July harvest, currently using urea but worried about soil hardness.â This profile tells you what to say, what price to charge, and where to find them. Update it every season as you learn more. Do not try to sell to everyone at once; master one group first, then expand.

2. Pricing Your Product for Profit

Price must cover your costs and leave a profit. First, calculate your cost per bag. Add all inputs: raw materials (manure, crop waste, ash), labour for turning and bagging, transport, bags, and rent for your composting site. Example: if you make 100 bags of compost and spend 40,000 shillings on materials, 30,000 on labour, 10,000 on bags, and 5,000 on transport, your total cost is 85,000 shillings. Divide by 100 bags: your cost is 850 shillings per bag. Never sell below this number.

Next, check local prices. Visit three agro-dealer shops and ask the price of a 50 kg bag of NPK, urea, and chicken manure. In many countries, a 50 kg bag of NPK sells for 35,000â55,000 shillings, while a bag of compost sells for 8,000â20,000 shillings. Your compost is cheaper because it is bulky and local, but it also improves soil over time. Set your price between your cost and the chemical fertiliser price. A common starting point is 12,000â15,000 shillings per 50 kg bag, or 300â400 shillings per kilo for small vegetable packs.

Offer different sizes for different customers. A 1 kg pack for 500 shillings suits a woman growing vegetables for her family. A 10 kg bag for 3,500 shillings suits a school garden. A 50 kg bag for 15,000 shillings suits a maize farmer. Small packs have a higher price per kilo but lower total cost, so they are good for first-time buyers. Always write your prices on a board at your site and at market so customers see them clearly.

Do not compete only on price. Explain that compost adds organic matter, holds water, and reduces the need for chemicals over time. If a farmer uses 2 bags of compost instead of 1 bag of NPK, he may spend less and get better soil. Offer a small discount for buying 10 bags or more, or for paying cash up front. Keep records of every sale in a notebook: date, customer name, quantity, price, and whether they paid. After one season, you will know your real profit and can adjust prices.

3. Selling at Local Markets and Agro-dealer Shops

Local markets are the fastest way to meet many farmers in one day. Find out the market days in your areaâmany are every 5 or 7 days. Arrive early, before 7 a.m., to get a good spot. Bring 20â30 small bags (1 kg and 5 kg) and 5â10 large bags (50 kg). Display them on a mat or small table with a clear sign: âOrganic Compost â 500 per kg, 3,500 per 10 kg, 15,000 per 50 kg.â Put a small bowl of compost on the table so people can touch and smell it. Have a laminated photo or a small potted plant showing maize or vegetables grown with your compost.

Talk to people as they pass. Do not just sit. Say: âGood morning, this is compost made from local manure and crop waste. It feeds your soil and holds water. Try a 1 kg pack for 500 shillings.â Give a free sample to the first 10 farmers who listen. Ask for their phone number so you can follow up. Many farmers will not buy the first time; they will buy after they see a neighbourâs results. Your goal at market is to get 5â10 serious contacts and 3â5 sales.

Agro-dealer shops are another route. These shops already sell seeds, chemicals and tools to farmers. Visit the owner and offer to supply compost on consignment: you leave 20 bags, they sell them, and you pay them a 10â15% commission. Or sell to them at a wholesale price, for example 11,000 shillings per 50 kg bag, and let them sell at 15,000. Give the shop a free display bag and a poster. Check stock every week and replace sold bags. A good agro-dealer can sell 50â100 bags per month for you without you standing at the market.

Also approach schools, churches, hotels and NGOs. A school garden may buy 20 bags per term. A hotel may buy 10 bags per month for its vegetable garden. Write a simple one-page flyer with your name, phone number, prices, and the benefits of compost. Leave it at the agro-dealer, church notice board, and extension office. Always follow up by phone after one week.

4. Using Demonstrations to Show Results

Farmers believe what they see, not what they hear. A demonstration plot is the most powerful marketing tool you have. Choose a visible field beside a road or near a market. Ask a respected farmer to lend you a small plot, or use your own garden. Divide the plot into two equal halves. On one half, apply your compost at 2 handfuls per planting hole or 2 tonnes per acre. On the other half, use the farmerâs normal practiceâno fertiliser or chemical fertiliser. Plant the same crop on the same day, using the same seed and spacing.

Label the plots clearly with wooden signs: âWith Compostâ and âWithout Compost.â Take photos every two weeks. Measure plant height and count leaves. At harvest, weigh the yield from each half. A good result might be 3 bags of maize from the compost half versus 1.5 bags from the control half. Invite farmers to a field day at flowering or harvest. Serve tea and mandazi. Show them the difference and let them touch the soil. The compost soil will be darker, softer, and hold more moisture.

Run demonstrations for different crops: maize, tomatoes, kale, beans, and cassava. Each crop shows different benefits. For vegetables, compost often increases leaf size and reduces disease. For maize, it improves cob size and soil water holding. Keep a simple record book: date planted, amount of compost, rainfall, yield. Share these numbers with farmers. âLast season, this plot gave 3 bags of maize; the next plot gave 1.5 bags. That is double yield from the same seed and rain.â

Use the demonstration plot as a meeting place. Invite the agricultural extension officer to speak. Ask the host farmer to tell neighbours what he saw. Give every visitor a small free sample and your phone number. After the field day, follow up within three days with a phone call or SMS: âDid you see the maize? Would you like 2 bags of compost for your field?â Many farmers will order immediately after seeing the harvest. One good demonstration can sell 50â100 bags in a season.

5. Building Relationships with Farmers and Cooperatives

Farming is a relationship business. A farmer who trusts you will buy from you every season and tell others. Start by keeping your promises. If you say you will deliver on Tuesday, deliver on Tuesday. If a bag is short weight, add extra. Trust is built slowly and lost quickly. Always greet farmers by name, ask about their family and crops, and remember what they told you last time. Keep a small notebook with names, phone numbers, crops, and past orders.

Join or work with farmer cooperatives. A cooperative is a group of farmers who buy and sell together. In many African countries, cooperatives handle maize, coffee, cocoa, cotton or dairy. Meet the chairperson and treasurer. Explain that you can supply compost in bulk at a discount. Offer 10% off for orders of 50 bags or more. Ask to speak at their monthly meeting for 10 minutes. Bring your demonstration photos and a few sample bags. If the cooperative agrees, they can collect orders from members and you deliver once to the cooperative store. This saves you transport and gives you many customers at once.

Support farmers after the sale. Visit their fields one month after they apply your compost. Ask: âHow is the crop looking? Did you use it correctly?â If they have a problem, help them solve it. If they get a good result, ask for a testimonial. Write down what they say and use it in your marketing: âMrs. Achieng from Bondo got 4 bags of maize from one acre using our compost.â Farmers trust other farmers more than salespeople.

Offer a loyalty scheme. For example, after a farmer buys 10 bags, give one bag free. Or give a free 1 kg pack for every 5 bags bought. Keep a card for each customer and mark it. This encourages repeat buying. Also remember the influencers: extension officers, lead farmers, and agro-dealers. Send them a small free sample each season and keep them informed. A good relationship with one extension officer can bring you 20 new customers. Treat every farmer with respect, whether they buy one small pack or one tonne.

6. Using Mobile Phones for Orders and Payments

A mobile phone is your office. Most farmers in Africa have a phone, even in rural areas. Use it to take orders, send reminders, and receive payments. Start by getting a dedicated phone number for your compost business. Write it on every bag, flyer, and market sign. If possible, register for a mobile money account such as M-Pesa, Airtel Money, MTN Mobile Money, or Orange Money. This lets farmers pay you instantly without cash.

Set up a simple ordering system. When a farmer calls, ask: name, location, quantity, and when they need it. Write it in an order book or a notebook. Send an SMS confirmation: âHello Mary, your order of 2 bags of compost is confirmed for Thursday. Total 30,000. Pay to 07XX-XXXXXX. Thank you.â If you have a smartphone, use WhatsApp. Create a broadcast list of customers. Send a photo of your demonstration plot and a message: âCompost available this week. 50 kg bag 15,000. Order now.â Do not send too many messages; once a week is enough.

Use mobile money for payments. Ask farmers to send money before delivery for large orders, or pay on delivery for small orders. Always send a receipt by SMS: âReceived 30,000 from Mary for 2 bags. Thank you.â Keep a record of every payment in your notebook. If a farmer cannot pay by mobile money, accept cash but write a receipt. Never keep large amounts of cash at home; deposit it at the nearest mobile money agent or bank.

Use your phone to follow up. After a farmer buys, call or SMS after two weeks: âHello, how is your crop? Did the compost help?â After harvest, call again: âHow was your yield? Would you like to order for next season?â This simple follow-up brings repeat sales. You can also use the phone to check prices at different markets, order more bags from your supplier, and arrange transport. If you have a smartphone, take photos of your demonstration plots and share them on WhatsApp groups for farmers, cooperatives, and extension officers. A short video of a farmer saying âI got double maize with this compostâ is powerful advertising. Keep your phone charged and always answer calls politely.

1. Identifying Your Target Customers

Not every farmer is your customer. Organic fertilizer sells fastest to four segments, in this order of ease:

  • Vegetable and horticulture farmers â high value crops, fast ROI, willing to pay for quality. This is your beachhead.
  • Smallholder maize and cereal farmers â high volume, price sensitive, need demonstrations to convert.
  • Agro-dealers and input shops â they buy in bulk and resell; you trade margin for volume and reach.
  • Cooperatives and farmer groups â one sale, many farmers, but slow decision cycles.

Segment by crop, farm size, and cash flow cycle, not by geography alone. A 2-hectare tomato farmer near a market is a better first customer than a 10-hectare maize farmer 40 km away.

3. Selling at Local Markets and Agro-dealer Shops

Two channels, two strategies:

  • Direct market selling: Higher margin, lower volume. Best for horticulture farmers and weekend markets. Bring samples, a scale, and a one-page leaflet with application rates.
  • Agro-dealer shops: Lower margin (give them 20â30%), higher volume and credibility. Dealers already have farmer traffic and trust.

When approaching a dealer, lead with turnover, not product features. Offer consignment stock for the first order to reduce their risk. Provide shelf-ready bags, a poster, and a simple application guide in the local language.

4. Using Demonstrations to Show Results

African farmers buy what they can see. A demonstration plot is the single most effective sales tool you have. Run paired plots â one with your fertilizer, one without â on a respected farmer's land, near a road or market.

Measure and display: plant height, leaf colour, number of cobs or fruits, and yield in kg. Take photos at every stage. Invite neighbouring farmers to a field day at harvest. Let the host farmer speak â peer testimony outperforms any sales pitch.

5. Building Relationships with Farmers and Cooperatives

In African agribusiness, trust is the currency. Relationships convert one-time buyers into repeat customers who refer others. Practical actions:

  • Visit farmers at planting and top-dressing time â not when you want to sell.
  • Keep a simple customer register: name, crop, hectares, phone, last purchase, next expected purchase.
  • For cooperatives, offer a group discount plus free training in exchange for a bulk order commitment.
  • Follow up after harvest. Ask about results. This gives you testimonials and data.
  • Attend cooperative meetings and district farmer field days. Be visible.

Cooperatives can order 5â50 tonnes at a time. Treat the cooperative leadership as a sales channel, not just a customer.

6. Using Mobile Phones for Orders and Payments

Mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, Orange Money) and WhatsApp are now core business infrastructure across Africa. Use them deliberately:

  • WhatsApp Business: Catalogue, broadcast lists, quick replies, location pin. Farmers order with one message.
  • USSD or SMS: For low-end phones, a simple "Text FERT to 12345" order line works.
  • Mobile money: Accept deposits before delivery. This reduces default risk and funds production.
  • Record keeping: Log every order and payment in a notebook or free app (e.g., Wave, Kobo Collect).

Set a clear policy: 50% deposit on order, balance on delivery. Confirm every order in writing via SMS or WhatsApp so both sides have a record.
