Lessons

1. Spray drying: principles and equipment

Spray drying turns a liquid detergent slurry into dry powder in one step. You pump the slurryabout 60-70% waterinto a tall drying chamber through a nozzle or spinning disc. Hot air (200-300C) meets the droplets, water evaporates in seconds, and dry particles fall to the bottom. In Africa, small plants often use a box-type spray dryer made locally from mild steel, with a kerosene or gas burner. A 100 kg/hour unit costs around $15,000$25,000. Key parts: feed pump, atomizer, air heater, cyclone, and bag filter. The atomizer is criticala worn nozzle gives big, wet lumps. Check nozzle pressure daily; it should be 2030 bar for pressure nozzles. For a spinning disc, speed is 10,00020,000 rpm. Inlet air temperature should be 250300C; outlet 90100C. If outlet drops below 80C, powder will be sticky. In humid African climates, preheat the air to avoid condensation. Start by mixing your slurry: 30% active detergent, 40% sodium sulphate, 10% sodium silicate, 5% sodium carbonate, and water to balance. Keep slurry at 6070C and stir continuously. A simple test: dip a glass rod, let it coolif it forms a hard film, solids are right. Never let slurry sit overnight; it separates. After drying, powder goes to a cyclone where fine dust is separated, then to a bag filter. Collect powder from both. Yield should be 95% of solids. Energy use: about 1.2 kg of fuel oil per kg of water evaporated. In Nigeria or Kenya, use rice husk or sawdust burners to cut costs. Always wear a dust mask and gogglesfine detergent dust irritates eyes and lungs. Clean the chamber daily to prevent caking.

2. Agglomeration and granulation techniques

Agglomeration makes powder particles stick together into larger, free-flowing granules. This reduces dust and makes the product dissolve faster. The simplest method is batch granulation in a concrete mixer or rotating drum. You start with dry base powder (spray-dried or mixed), then spray a liquid binderusually a 40% sodium silicate solution or waterwhile tumbling. For 100 kg of powder, use 1015 liters of binder. Mix for 510 minutes until you see small balls forming. Stop before they become too big. A good granule is 0.52 mm. Test by squeezing a handful: it should hold shape but crumble easily. If it sticks to your hand, too much binder. For small-scale production, a pan granulator (like a tilted rotating disc) works well. Set the disc angle at 4550 degrees and speed at 2030 rpm. Feed powder continuously and spray binder at the top. The granules roll and grow. Discharge at the bottom. In Africa, you can build a pan granulator from a 200-liter drum cut in half, mounted on a shaft with a small motor. Cost: under $500. Another method is extrusion: push wet paste through a screen with 12 mm holes, then dry the strands. This gives uniform granules. For detergent bars, you can use a meat mincer as a small extruder. After granulation, dry the granules in a tray dryer at 6070C for 23 hours until moisture is below 5%. Then sieve to remove fines and oversize. Fines go back to the mixer; oversize is crushed. Always add 12% of a anti-caking agent like sodium aluminosilicate before packing. Store granules in lined bags to keep out humidity. In coastal areas, use silica gel sachets in storage bins.

3. Fluid bed processing

A fluid bed processor blows hot air upward through a bed of powder, making it behave like a boiling liquid. This is used for drying, granulating, and coating detergent powders. For small African workshops, a fluid bed dryer can be made from a 200-liter drum with a perforated bottom plate (2 mm holes), a fan, and a heater. Air velocity must be high enough to lift particles but not blow them out. For 12 mm granules, use 1.52.5 m/s air speed. Inlet air temperature: 80120C. You can dry 50 kg of granules in 2030 minutes, much faster than tray drying. To granulate in a fluid bed, start with dry powder, fluidize it, then spray binder (water or silicate solution) from a nozzle at the top. The particles stick together and grow. Stop when you reach the desired size. This is called top-spray granulation. For coating, spray a solution of polymer or wax to protect the detergent from moisture. A common coating is 2% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 4000. In Africa, fluid bed dryers are used in tea and coffee processing, so you can learn from those factories. Key controls: air flow, inlet temperature, and spray rate. If the bed temperature drops below 40C, particles become sticky and defluidize. If it rises above 80C, binder dries too fast and you get dust. Always start with a low spray rate, then increase. After processing, cool the powder with unheated air for 5 minutes before discharging. Clean the filter bags every batchdetergent dust clogs them quickly. Safety: fine detergent dust can explode if ignited. Ground all equipment and use explosion-proof motors. Never open the chamber while hot air is flowing.

4. Particle size distribution and control

Particle size distribution (PSD) means the range of particle sizes in your powder. For detergents, you want most particles between 0.2 mm and 2 mm. Too fine (below 0.1 mm) causes dust and poor flow. Too coarse (above 3 mm) dissolves slowly. To measure PSD, use a set of sieves: 0.1 mm, 0.5 mm, 1 mm, 2 mm, and 3 mm. Stack them with the coarsest on top. Put 100 g of powder on the top sieve and shake for 5 minutes by hand or with a sieve shaker. Weigh the powder on each sieve. Calculate percentages. A good spray-dried powder has: 1020% below 0.1 mm, 4060% between 0.1 and 0.5 mm, 2030% between 0.5 and 1 mm, and less than 5% above 2 mm. If you have too many fines, increase binder in agglomeration or reduce atomizer pressure in spray drying. If too many coarse particles, increase atomizer pressure or reduce binder. For fluid bed granulation, adjust spray rate and air velocity. In Africa, you can buy a set of sieves from a laboratory supplier for about $200. Or make your own from mosquito netting and metal rings. Always take a sample from the middle of the batch, not the top or bottom. Record results in a notebook. If PSD is off, adjust your process and re-test. For consistent product, blend different batches to meet the target. Store powders in a dry placemoisture makes particles stick together and changes PSD. Use a moisture meter (cost $50$100) to check. Aim for less than 3% moisture. For hand-dishwashing powders, a slightly coarser PSD (0.52 mm) is preferred because it feels less dusty. For machine powders, finer is better for quick dissolution.

5. Dust control and explosion prevention

Detergent dust is not just a nuisanceit can explode. Fine organic and inorganic powders (like sodium lauryl sulphate or sodium carbonate) can form explosive clouds if ignited. In Africa, many small plants ignore this and risk lives. To prevent explosions: first, control dust at the source. Enclose conveyors, mixers, and sieving machines. Use local exhaust ventilation (LEV) with a fan pulling air away from the operator. A simple LEV hood over a sieving station costs $300$500. Second, ground all metal equipment. Static electricity can spark. Use copper wire to bond bins, pipes, and mixers to a ground rod. Third, use explosion-proof motors and switches in dusty areas. If not available, put motors outside the dust zone and use belt drives. Fourth, keep the workplace clean. Never let dust accumulate on floors or beamssweep or vacuum daily. Do not use compressed air to blow dust; it creates a cloud. Use a vacuum with a HEPA filter. Fifth, install a pressure relief vent on any enclosed dust collector. A burst panel (cost $200) directs explosion pressure outdoors. Sixth, train workers to wear N95 masks and goggles. Dust irritates eyes and lungs; long-term exposure causes breathing problems. In humid climates, dust is less likely to become airborne, but still dangerous. Test your dust: take a small sample, try to ignite it with a match in a safe outdoor area. If it burns quickly, it is explosive. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder type) near every process. Never smoke or use open flames. For small-scale production, the biggest risk is from sieving and bag filling. Do those tasks in a separate room with good ventilation. If you cannot afford explosion-proof equipment, do granulation (which reduces dust) instead of selling fine powders.

6. Quality parameters for powders

To sell detergent powder in Africa, you must meet basic quality standards. Key parameters: moisture content, bulk density, flowability, solubility, pH, and active matter. Moisture: should be below 3% for stable storage. Use a moisture meter or dry a 10 g sample in an oven at 105C for 2 hours; weight loss is moisture. Bulk density: free-flowing powder should be 0.50.9 g/mL. Measure by pouring 100 g into a 250 mL graduated cylinder and tapping 20 times. Read volume; density = weight/volume. If density is too low, powder is fluffy and bags will be large. Too high, it dissolves slowly. Flowability: test by pouring powder through a funnel with a 10 mm opening. It should flow freely without shaking. If it sticks, add 12% anti-caking agent (sodium aluminosilicate or magnesium stearate). Solubility: stir 5 g in 100 mL water at 30C for 1 minute. It should dissolve completely with no lumps. If not, increase the amount of sodium carbonate or reduce particle size. pH: make a 1% solution and measure with a pH meter or litmus paper. For hand dishwash, pH 79 is safe. For machine dishwash, pH 1012 is normal. Active matter: this is the actual detergent (e.g., linear alkylbenzene sulphonate). For hand wash, 1525% active matter. For machine, 2030%. Test by titration with a standard solutionor send a sample to a local university lab for $20. Also check appearance: uniform colour, no lumps, no foreign matter. Packaging: use laminated bags (PET/PE) to keep out moisture. Store on pallets, not on the floor. In Africa, buyers often check foam height: shake 1 g in 100 mL waterfoam should last at least 2 minutes. If not, adjust the surfactant. Keep a batch record for every production run. If a batch fails, do not sell itrework it into the next batch. Consistency builds trust and repeat customers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Granulation and Powder Production for African Detergent Enterprises

For detergent manufacturers across Africa, powder production is not merely a technical exercise it is the commercial engine of the business. Whether you are supplying sachets to informal traders in Kano, bulk drums to institutional buyers in Nairobi, or branded packs to supermarkets in Johannesburg, the physical form of your product determines its shelf life, its performance in the wash, and ultimately your margin. This practical reference brings together the core unit operations spray drying, agglomeration, fluid bed processing, particle sizing, dust control, and quality assurance into a single working document for plant managers, production supervisors, and entrepreneurs scaling from pilot to commercial output.

In the African context, three realities shape every decision. First, humidity and temperature vary enormously between coastal West Africa, the Sahel, the highveld, and the tropics; powders must be formulated and packaged to survive moisture ingress. Second, energy reliability is uneven, so equipment selection must balance thermal efficiency against the risk of power interruption. Third, raw material logistics soda ash, STPP, LABSA, sodium silicate, zeolites often involve import lead times measured in weeks, so process flexibility matters. The guidance below assumes you are working with a spray tower, a granulator, or a fluid bed unit, or planning to invest in one.

1. Spray Drying: Principles and Equipment

Spray drying converts a pumpable slurry typically 6065% solids containing surfactant, builder, silicate, and minor ingredients into dry hollow beads in a single pass. The slurry is atomised into fine droplets, which meet a hot air stream. Water evaporates rapidly, and the droplet solidifies into a particle whose size, density, and porosity are set by atomisation pressure, nozzle geometry, and the temperature profile inside the tower.

Two atomisation routes dominate. Pressure nozzles (high-pressure pumps, 2060 bar) give coarser, denser particles and are cheaper to maintain a good fit for African plants where spare-part logistics are slow. Rotary atomisers (spinning discs at 10,00020,000 rpm) produce finer, more uniform droplets and are preferred for high-throughput towers. Inlet air temperature is typically 200350C; outlet temperature is held at 90110C to avoid thermal degradation of surfactants and to keep the powder free-flowing.

Key equipment components are the crutcher (mixing vessel), high-pressure pump, atomiser, drying chamber, cyclone or bag filter for fines recovery, and a fluid bed cooler downstream. Energy consumption is the dominant operating cost 3,0005,000 kJ per kg of water evaporated so heat recovery from exhaust air is a genuine commercial lever, not a luxury.

2. Agglomeration and Granulation Techniques

Spray-dried powder is dusty and low in density. Agglomeration deliberately building larger, porous clusters improves solubility, reduces dust, and gives a premium "compact" feel. The two workhorse routes are spray-dry agglomeration (recycling fines into the tower or a separate agglomeration chamber) and mechanical granulation (high-shear mixers, pan granulators, or extrusion).

In high-shear granulation, dry ingredients are wetted with a binder solution (often sodium silicate or a surfactant slurry) under intense mixing. The binder bridges particles into granules. Critical variables are binder concentration, impeller speed, and moisture content. Over-wetting produces dough-like lumps; under-wetting leaves friable, dusty granules. For African producers targeting the sachet market, a narrow particle size distribution (300800 m) is commercially critical it fills sachets consistently and dissolves fast in hand-wash conditions.

Spray-dry agglomeration is capital-intensive but gives the best flowability and appearance. Mechanical granulation is cheaper, more flexible, and better suited to small and medium enterprises (SMEs) that need to switch formulations frequently.

3. Fluid Bed Processing

Fluid bed units perform three functions in sequence: drying, cooling, and post-dosing (spraying heat-sensitive ingredients such as enzymes, perfume, or bleach activators onto already-dry particles). Air is forced upward through a perforated plate, suspending the powder bed. Heat and mass transfer are excellent, and residence time is short typically 520 minutes.

For African plants, fluid beds offer a key advantage: they can be run on lower-grade thermal energy (hot water or low-pressure steam) and are more tolerant of intermittent power than a full spray tower. They also allow enzyme and perfume addition after the thermal step, preserving activity a real quality differentiator in markets where consumers judge by smell and stain removal.

4. Particle Size Distribution and Control

Particle size distribution (PSD) drives every downstream behaviour: flow, dust, dissolution, and packing density. A typical detergent powder has a median diameter (D50) of 300600 m, with less than 5% below 100 m (fines) and less than 10% above 1,000 m (oversize).

Control levers include atomiser pressure, slurry viscosity, tower airflow, and the recycle ratio of fines. Sieving and air classification are standard. In practice, a simple sieve stack (100 m, 500 m, 1,000 m) plus a monthly external PSD check is sufficient for most SMEs.

SAFETY WARNING DUST EXPLOSION AND RESPIRATORY HAZARD

Detergent powders containing surfactants, zeolites, and organic additives can form explosive dust clouds. A single ignition source a hot surface, static discharge, or a spark can cause a primary explosion, and the dislodged dust can trigger a far more destructive secondary explosion. In African plants where earthing and ventilation may be substandard, this risk is amplified. Mandatory controls: bond and earth all metal equipment; use conductive ducting; avoid plastic buckets and nylon clothing in dusty areas; install explosion venting on cyclones and bag filters; keep housekeeping continuous (no dust layers above 1 mm); and provide N95 or better respirators. Never use compressed air to blow down dust it creates exactly the cloud you are trying to avoid.

5. Dust Control and Explosion Prevention

Dust is both a safety hazard and an economic loss every kilogram of fines lost to the filter is raw material you paid for. Control starts at the source: minimise fines generation through proper atomisation and avoid over-drying. Enclosed transfer points, local exhaust ventilation, and well-maintained bag filters or cyclones are essential. Regular filter inspection matters: a torn bag releases dust continuously and can seed an explosion.

Explosion prevention follows a hierarchy: eliminate ignition sources, control dust concentrations, and install protective systems (venting, suppression, isolation). In practice, earthing and housekeeping deliver the greatest risk reduction per dollar. For SMEs, a written hot-work permit and a no-smoking policy in the powder area are non-negotiable.

6. Quality Parameters for Powders

Quality is checked at three levels: chemical (active matter, alkalinity, moisture), physical (bulk density, PSD, flowability), and sensory (colour, odour, feel). Moisture content above 35% causes caking in humid African climates; below 1% can make the powder brittle. Flowability is measured by angle of repose or a simple funnel test. Bulk density is measured by filling a known volume and weighing.

For export or supermarket supply, you will also need to meet labelling and safety data requirements. Locally, KEBS (Kenya), SON (Nigeria), and SABS (South Africa) publish relevant standards check them before you scale.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Slurry check: Confirm 6065% solids and 6075C before atomisation.
  2. Atomiser condition: Inspect nozzle or rotary disc daily; replace worn parts before PSD drifts.
  3. Temperature log: Record inlet and outlet air temperatures every shift; investigate deviations >10C.
  4. PSD sieve test: Run a 100/500/1,000 m sieve check at least weekly.
  5. Moisture test: Target 13% moisture; test each batch before packing.
  6. Dust housekeeping: No visible dust layers; earth all equipment; inspect filters monthly.
  7. Fluid bed post-dosing: Add enzymes and perfume only after the bed is below 45C.
  8. Batch traceability: Record raw material lot numbers, process parameters, and operator for every batch.
