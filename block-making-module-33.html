Sand and Aggregate Sourcing

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Types of sand (sharp vs soft)

Not all sand is the same. Using the wrong sand ruins blocks.

Sharp sand (pit sand, coarse sand):

  • Angular grains, rough texture
  • Graded 0–4 mm typically
  • Best for concrete and blocks — grains lock together
  • Colour: reddish-brown or grey

Soft sand (builder's sand, fine sand):

  • Rounded, smooth grains
  • 0–2 mm typically
  • Used for mortar and plaster, NOT for structural concrete
  • Colour: yellow or white

Sea sand: Contains salt — never use for concrete unless washed thoroughly. Salt corrodes reinforcement and weakens the mix.

⚠ Always specify "sharp sand" or "concrete sand" to your supplier. Never accept soft sand for blocks.

2 Checking sand quality

Bad sand = weak blocks. Test before you buy.

Field tests:

  • Squeeze test: Squeeze wet sand in your hand. Good sand holds shape but breaks apart when touched. Clay-rich sand holds tight and stains your hand.
  • Smell test: Organic matter smells musty. Reject.
  • Jar test: Put 2 cm of sand in a jar, add water, shake, let settle. Sand settles in 1 minute. If the water stays cloudy for hours, the sand has too much silt/clay.
  • Salt test: Taste a tiny amount. Salty = sea sand. Reject.

Clean sand is 20–30% more expensive than dirty sand — but it uses 20% less cement. Worth the premium.

3 Aggregate sizes and uses

Aggregate (crushed stone) is graded by size. Wrong size ruins the block.

Common sizes:

  • 0–4 mm: Quarry dust or fine gravel. Used to replace part of the sand in some mixes.
  • 4–6 mm: Standard block and paver aggregate. Best all-round.
  • 6–10 mm: For heavier blocks and kerbs.
  • 10–20 mm: Concrete foundations, not blocks.
  • 20–40 mm: Sub-base for paving.

For blocks: 4–6 mm is standard. Never use stone bigger than 1/3 of the block's thinnest wall. For a hollow block with 30 mm walls, that means 10 mm maximum.

Crushed stone has angular faces that grip. Rounded river gravel works but gives lower strength.

4 Sourcing from local pits

Where to find sand and gravel:

  • Quarries: Best quality, consistent grading. Licensed and regulated.
  • River pits: Good quality sand if clean. Beware riverbed changes and environmental restrictions.
  • Pit sand (open pit): Most common. Quality varies hugely.
  • Crushed rock from demolition: Cheapest but must be tested for contamination.

Licensing: In many countries, extracting sand from riverbeds requires a permit. Buying from unlicensed pits is illegal and can get you shut down.

Always visit the pit in person. Watch how they excavate and screen. Meet the owner. Poor pits mix contaminated material.

5 Transport cost maths

Sand is cheap; transport is often more than the sand itself.

Example:

  • Sand at pit: $8 per tonne
  • Truck 20 tonnes: $160
  • Transport 30 km: $120 (fuel, driver, wear)
  • Delivery cost per tonne: $14 ($8 + $6 transport)

If your supplier is 100 km away, transport might be $40/tonne — 5× the sand price.

Rule: Source sand and stone within 30 km if possible. Beyond that, look for a closer pit or negotiate volume.

Bulk delivery (20 tonnes per trip) is much cheaper per tonne than small loads. Always buy full truckloads.

6 Stockpiling on site

Keep 2 weeks of sand and stone on site so you never stop production.

Bay design:

  • Separate bays for sand, stone, and any additives
  • Solid concrete or stone base — not dirt
  • Dividing walls between bays (concrete or timber)
  • Covered or partly covered
  • Accessible by loader or wheelbarrow

Bay size for 5,000 blocks/day:

  • Sand: 5 tonnes/day × 14 days = 70 tonnes = 45 m³ (about 4.5 × 3 × 3.5 m bay)
  • Stone: 8 tonnes/day × 14 days = 112 tonnes = 70 m³
  • Total: two bays of about 50 m² each

Compact the base with a plate compactor before adding stone — prevents sinking.

7 Washing dirty sand

If sand has more than 5% clay/silt, wash it before use.

Simple washing method:

  1. Build a sloping concrete pad with a low wall around it
  2. Dump sand on the upper end
  3. Spray water from a hose continuously
  4. Sand settles on the pad; clay washes off the lower end
  5. Turn the sand twice during washing
  6. Test again

Washing uses about 500 litres per tonne of sand. Cost: $1–$3 per tonne. Worth it if clay content is high.

After washing, sand takes 1–2 days to drain to usable moisture content. Plan ahead.

⚠ Do not wash sand with cement-contaminated water. Use clean water only.

8 Buying from quarries vs pits

Two sourcing options with different trade-offs:

FeatureQuarryPit
QualityConsistent, gradedVariable
PriceHigherLower
LicensingLicensedOften unlicensed
DeliveryReliableVariable
Best forQuality blocksCost-saving

Buy from a quarry if quality matters (most commercial work). Use pits only for non-critical or bulk fill. Test every new pit before committing.

💰 Sand and stone are 40-50% of your material cost. Sourcing well saves more than any other efficiency. Get 3 quotes, visit each site, and test each sample.
